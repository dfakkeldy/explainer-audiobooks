## Chapter 4 — Give the Parcel a Biography

### What the Municipality Has Already Done

The Inverness packet does more work than a typical auction advertisement. For
each detailed entry, it gathers account and parcel identifiers, assessment
figures, mapped views, and a legal description. A researcher who begins by
copying those fields into a prettier page has changed the presentation, not the
state of knowledge.

The useful first move is an inventory. Which facts came from the municipal
packet? Which source did the municipality name? Which details describe the
auction event, which describe an assessment account, and which purport to
describe the parcel? The inventory credits the municipality and prevents later
research from being mistaken for original discovery.

Take a fictional composite entry on Birch Point Road. Its packet row supplies a
lien number, AAN, PID, civic location, assessment fields, recovery amount, and
redemption marker. The detailed page adds mapped orientation and descriptive
material. Chapter 2 already assigned each field a job. Chapter 4 gives the
collection a history.

The first page in the research file is a source ledger. It might say that the
event status and recovery amount came from the municipality's dated notice. The
assessment fields came through the account information reproduced in that
packet. The parcel identifier points toward provincial parcel and registry
systems. The mapped image is orientation. The legal description is text that
requires land-record interpretation. Each line records a source, a retrieval
date, and a limitation.

That ledger matters when the records disagree. Inverness's August packet has a
summary lien with no matching detailed sheet, and two recovery amounts differ
between the summary and detail portions. A clean-looking research report could
hide those conflicts by choosing one number. A parcel biography preserves both
records, identifies their dates and locations, and routes the current-event
question back to the municipality.

The biography begins with the PID because it is the stable parcel-routing handle
already present in the file. It does not begin with an owner's name. The public
teaching record can remain owner-free while still showing the method: exact PID,
source, observation, limitation, unresolved question, and next authority.

The living public map can open that biography through either of two bounded
routes. An exact PID can select the parcel returned for that identifier. An
authoritative provincial civic-address result can supply a civic point and open
the parcel polygon that contains it. Both routes save time at the front of the
file. Neither proves ownership, occupancy, legal access, a survey boundary, or
permission to enter. Chapter 5 will operate those routes; here they simply
deliver the researcher to the correct source-led questions.

An authorized land-record researcher can use Property Online to connect that PID
to provincial ownership, mapping, and registry information. Search can also use
an AAN, civic address, or owner, but those routes answer different matching
problems. The public book does not reproduce subscriber screenshots, plans,
registry documents, owner information, or structured extracts. Subscription
access is a way to conduct authorized research, not a licence to republish the
underlying material.

One record encountered there is the **parcel register**. It is the indexed land-
registration record associated with a registered parcel and its interests. The
register is valuable because it moves the file beyond the assessed-owner label
on an auction notice. It may identify registered interests and instruments that
matter to a title examination.

It is not a self-executing conclusion. A document number, interest entry, or
name in a register does not tell an untrained reader how the interest operates,
whether another instrument changes it, or what the tax-sale statutes do to it.
The register creates a more precise set of questions. It does not turn the
researcher into the lawyer who answers them.

The same discipline applies to the **legal description**. A civic address helps
people find a place. A legal description identifies land through the language
used in the conveyancing record. It may refer to bearings, distances, adjoining
lands, plans, lots, roads, shorelines, or older descriptions. The description's
job is not to paint an easy picture for a driver. Its job is to state what the
instrument describes.

Birch Point Road's civic location may bring the researcher to the correct area.
Its PID may open a polygon on a public parcel map. An aerial view may show a roof,
tree line, road, or clearing. The legal description may refer to boundaries that
are neither visible nor safely reconstructed from those pixels. The three
records can support one another without becoming interchangeable.

That is why a public parcel boundary is a screening exhibit. It can show the
shape reported by the current provincial service, support orientation, and help
form questions about apparent road or water relationships. It does not certify
where a boundary lies on the ground. A surveyor's evidence and the legal record
answer that problem at a different level.

Public NS Aerial and NSPRD views also carry a licence obligation. A rendered
view using the covered Province services must preserve the Province attribution:
“Contains information obtained under license from the Province of Nova Scotia
which is provided without warranty or liability for errors or omissions.” The
application must link to the licence. The attribution survives in the image
because a screenshot may later travel outside the app that supplied it.

The licence permits the checked public display. It does not erase the map's
substantive limits, and it is not treated here as permission to redistribute raw
geometry or a tile cache. Rights, provenance, and evidentiary meaning are three
different questions. A view can be lawfully displayed and still be incapable of
proving a boundary.

The final land-record term is **title search**. The phrase names an examination
of the ownership and interest chain rather than a casual lookup of the latest
name. Before a tax sale, the municipality itself performs title work as part of
the statutory notice process. That work helps identify people entitled to
notice. A prospective bidder has a different decision and may need a lawyer to
examine the records for that purpose.

The municipal title work does not become a purchaser's title opinion merely
because both inquiries involve the same parcel. The municipality is moving a
tax-enforcement process through its required stages. The bidder is deciding
whether the legal position, uncertainty, project, and price belong together.
The questions overlap; the professional duty and requested conclusion do not.

For Birch Point Road, the source ledger can now carry four distinct entries.
The municipal notice establishes the dated auction record. The parcel register
routes registered interests to legal review. The legal description supplies the
recorded land description. Public parcel and aerial services provide attributed
screening views. None is labelled “the truth” while the others become decoration.

The researcher then writes observations in deliberately bounded language:

- The municipal packet associates this exact PID with this dated auction entry.
- The current provincial parcel service returns mapped geometry for the PID.
- The aerial service shows visible features within and near that reported
  geometry on its captured imagery.
- The land record contains a legal description and instrument references that
  require professional interpretation.

The verbs do useful work. “Associates,” “returns,” “shows,” and “contains” report
what a source did. They do not silently become “owns,” “bounds,” “permits,” or
“guarantees.”

Now the legal question can become specific. Instead of asking a lawyer, “Is this
property okay?” the file can ask which registered interests affect the parcel,
how the legal description and referenced plan should be read, what the tax deed
would do to those interests, and what cannot be resolved before sale. A surveyor
can receive the mapped-boundary and description question without being asked to
interpret the tax-sale statute. Each professional sees the source material and
the uncertainty that belongs to that profession.

Birch Point Road has acquired a biography, but not a verdict. Its life in the
file begins with the municipal event, passes through the parcel and registry
records, and reaches attributed public screening views. The remaining gaps are
now visible enough to classify rather than hide.

### Four Places an Unknown Can Go

The source ledger for Birch Point Road contains facts and gaps on the same page.
That is a sign of a functioning file. A report that permits only confident
sentences will convert its gaps into assumptions or omit them entirely.

Start with a verified fact: the municipality's dated notice associates an exact
PID with a particular auction entry. The file has the source, date, and captured
record. “Verified” describes the match to that source. It does not make the
municipal notice responsible for a survey boundary, building condition, or
title conclusion it never offered.

A second entry might be professionally verifiable. The public parcel service
reports mapped geometry that appears to meet a road corridor. The legal
description refers to a plan. The file cannot determine whether the apparent
relationship supplies the frontage or right-of-way required for the intended
use. A surveyor can investigate the boundary evidence. A lawyer can examine the
registered interests and legal access. A planner can apply current municipal
requirements to the proposed project.

The phrase “professionally verifiable” is not a promise that the answer will be
favourable. It identifies a question, evidence source, and competent route to an
answer. The result may remove the concern, define a manageable condition, or
end the project.

A third entry may be priceable uncertainty. Suppose an insurer confirms that a
vacant building can be covered only under specified terms and at a higher known
premium. The uncertainty has narrowed enough to enter a budget. The same may be
true of a quoted survey, a defined legal review, or a known municipal application
fee.

Priceable does not mean trivial. A cost can be known and still make the parcel
unworkable. It means the file can attach a documented amount or bounded range to
the uncertainty without inventing a number.

The fourth destination is no-go uncertainty. The researcher may be unable to
confirm a fact that is essential to the intended project before the sale. An
answer may depend on entry the municipality cannot authorize, a study that
cannot be completed in time, evidence that remains contradictory, or a legal
position too consequential to carry unresolved. The correct label can be “do
not proceed under these conditions.”

That label is not a prediction that the land is bad. It describes the fit
between the available evidence, the deadline, the researcher's tolerance, and
the proposed use. Another person with different expertise, authority, time, or
risk capacity may reach a different process decision. The public book does not
turn either decision into a parcel recommendation.

Assessment illustrates the classification problem. The assessed value and
classification are verified account information when matched to the proper
source and date. Their implications for market value, condition, legal access,
or a maximum bid are not verified by that match. The assessment can route a
question; it cannot fill the empty spaces in the biography.

The same four destinations can be applied after an interruption. Ask what the
file actually establishes. Ask whether the next gap belongs to a named
professional or source. Ask whether a documented answer can enter the budget.
If an essential gap cannot be resolved or carried, stop the project before
attachment rewrites the evidence.

Birch Point Road's apparent road relationship demonstrates the handoff. The
researcher does not send a lawyer a screenshot with “Looks accessible?” in the
subject line. The file supplies the exact PID, dated municipal record, relevant
instrument references, legal description, captured public view with attribution,
and the intended use. The question asks which registered rights provide legal
access and what interests or documents must be reviewed.

The surveyor receives a different question from the same file: what evidence is
needed to locate the relevant boundary or plan relationship, and can that work
be completed before the decision deadline? The planner receives the proposed
use, current municipal source, and parcel identifiers, then identifies which
planning, lot, frontage, servicing, or permit questions require confirmation.

Those roles overlap around the parcel without becoming substitutes. A surveyor
locating a boundary does not decide what a tax deed does to a registered
interest. A lawyer interpreting an interest does not certify where a monument
lies on the ground. A planner explaining current land-use rules does not confirm
title or physical condition.

Professional fees now have a defined purpose in the research file. They buy a
particular reduction in uncertainty. The invoice is not generic closing
overhead; it is connected to a question whose answer can change the decision.
That connection also permits triage. A bidder may pay first for the question
most likely to end the project before ordering lower-priority work.

Some legal routes sit outside an ordinary bidder's file. Nova Scotia's municipal
statutes allow a treasurer to seek a court order addressing matters such as
arrears, interests conveyed, notice, and the manner of sale. A sale under such
an order has the protected effect described by the order and the statute. That
mechanism belongs to the municipality, court, and legal process. It is not a
routine cure a bidder can assume will repair ambiguity in a current listing.

If a notice or legal professional identifies a court-directed process, the
actual order becomes an essential source. The file records what the order says
and routes its effect to legal interpretation. The researcher does not compress
“court ordered” into “risk free.”

By the end of first review, Birch Point Road may have several green entries and
one red one. The exact PID-to-notice match is verified. A survey quote is
priceable. The access question is with the lawyer and surveyor. An essential
site-condition question cannot be answered lawfully before sale and is labelled
no-go for the proposed project.

The red entry governs even if the aerial image is attractive. Evidence is not a
vote in which three comfortable facts outnumber one unresolved essential fact.
The importance of the unknown depends on the project it can defeat.

This is the point of giving the parcel a biography before giving it a future.
The file remembers where each statement came from, who can strengthen it, what
it would cost to resolve, and where the evidence must stop. Emotional attachment
arrives later, if it arrives at all.

Chapter 5 will put that biography into motion on the living map. Its job is not
to replace the ledger or the professionals. It will shorten the route from a
dated notice to an exact parcel, isolate one context question, and preserve the
unknown and handoff without allowing a vivid map to write the ending.
