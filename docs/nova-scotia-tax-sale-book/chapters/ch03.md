## Chapter 3 — The Six-Month Door

### The Document Between a Bid and a Deed

Harbour Road has reached the auction room again. This time the fictional bidder
raises a card, becomes the leading bidder, and completes the payment required by
the process. The public scene feels finished. The legal position has just become
more complicated.

For a redeemable property, full payment produces a **certificate of sale**. The
municipal treasurer prepares and registers that certificate. The purchaser is
now the **certificate holder**.

The document matters because a leading bid and a completed paid sale are
different stages. A raised card expresses a bid. Payment completes the
purchaser's financial obligation under the sale terms. The registered
certificate records the statutory position created by that completed sale.
None of those stages is the tax deed.

Chapter 2's redemption marker now opens into a mechanism. For most tax-sale
properties, a qualifying owner or interest holder has six months after the sale
to redeem. The older-arrears category follows the immediate-deed branch instead
because the normal statutory redemption right does not apply when the taxes
were already more than six years in arrears at sale.

Harbour Road is in the six-month category. The certificate holder has paid, but
the owner and other qualifying interest holders still have a live statutory
route back to the property. The certificate holder therefore occupies a middle
state: more responsibility than a prospective bidder, less finality than a
deeded owner.

The statutes give that middle state practical content. A certificate holder may
protect the land and may use it without diminishing its value. The holder may
collect rent. The holder must avoid injuring the property, must not cut trees,
and must insure buildings that are insurable.

Each power arrives with a boundary. Protection can support work needed to keep
an insurable building from deteriorating. It does not create a general licence
to renovate for a preferred future use. Non-diminishing use does not erase
planning law, tenancy, occupancy, safety, or the continuing redemption right.
Rent collection does not answer who occupies the premises or how lawful
communication and accounting must occur in a real case.

The insurance duty contains a further uncertainty. A statute can require the
certificate holder to insure an insurable building. It cannot force an insurer
to offer a policy for every vacant, damaged, occupied, remote, or otherwise
non-standard risk. The word “insurable” is doing real work. A bidder who cannot
obtain a workable answer before sale may inherit a statutory responsibility
without a practical insurance product ready to satisfy it.

Consider a modest fictional building on Harbour Road. A storm damages part of
the roof during the second month. Water is entering near an exterior wall. The
certificate holder cannot treat the redemption period as an excuse to watch the
building decay. The protective question is urgent: what work is necessary to
prevent further damage, who should authorize it, which insurer must be notified,
and what records will later prove the nature and cost of the work?

The same facts do not support gutting the interior, removing healthy trees for a
view, changing the use, or treating the premises as vacant without evidence.
Protection is connected to preserving the property through a conditional
period. Improvement for the holder's preferred project is a different job.

The certificate holder also needs an accounting habit from the first day. New
tax bills, insurance premiums, necessary protective work, rent, and other income
can matter if redemption occurs. Chapter 11 will operate that ledger in detail.
At this stage, the mechanism is enough: money and authorization cannot be
reconstructed reliably after several months from bank statements and memory.

People outside the auction room remain part of this clock. The owner may be
trying to arrange funds. A lender or another interest holder may be assessing
its position. An occupant may experience contact from someone who has paid at a
tax sale but does not yet hold a deed. A contractor may see a leaking roof
without knowing who can authorize which work. The treasurer remains the owner
of the municipal process, while lawyers, insurers, and other professionals own
questions within their authority.

The certificate holder should therefore be able to answer four stage questions
before taking action:

- What document do I hold?
- Which power or duty supports the proposed action?
- Which continuing right or legal boundary limits it?
- What record will be needed if the property is redeemed or proceeds to deed?

For the leaking roof, the document is the registered certificate. The proposed
action is protective rather than developmental. The redemption right and any
occupancy or tenancy obligations remain. The file needs insurer communication,
professional advice where required, photographs obtained lawfully, invoices,
proof of payment, and the municipality's approval where the statutory
accounting calls for it.

The four questions are a stage check, not a do-it-yourself legal test. They
prevent the holder from using the broad word “owner” to skip over the document
and limits that govern the current moment.

Staged responsibility can be pictured as a relay baton if the comparison is
kept narrow. The bid, payment, certificate, possible discharge, and possible
deed are documented handoffs. Each stage carries a different permitted job.
The same purchaser may hold every stage, and legal rights are not literally
passed between runners. The image helps only with sequence: the holder must know
which document is currently in hand.

Now interrupt the story in month four. Harbour Road's building has been insured
and protected. Necessary work has been documented. The owner produces the funds
required to redeem.

The certificate holder does not get to choose whether redemption is convenient.
The six-month branch is controlled by statute. The file that looked like the
beginning of an ownership project is about to become an accounting and discharge
file.

### Two Endings, Two Documents

Redemption is not completed by reimbursing whatever the certificate holder
chooses to submit. The statutes define the categories used in the calculation.
They include the purchase price, interest at ten percent a year, and specified
costs and offsets connected to the certificate period.

The categories can include later taxes and interest, registration of the
certificate of discharge, fire-insurance premiums, and necessary repairs that
receive the required approval. Rent or other income from the property can affect
the accounting in the other direction. The exact facts, dates, authorization,
and local administration matter in a real redemption.

For a road listener, the durable idea is an evidence ledger rather than a
formula recited from memory. Every claimed dollar needs a source. Every repair
needs a reason and the required approval. Every offset needs a record. The
ledger should be capable of answering a treasurer or lawyer without relying on
the certificate holder's recollection of a chaotic month.

Harbour Road's leaking roof shows why. An invoice proves that a contractor was
paid. It does not by itself prove that the work was necessary, that the amount
belongs in the statutory redemption calculation, or that the required approval
was obtained. The insurance file, photographs, professional assessment,
municipal communication, invoice, and proof of payment perform different jobs.

The holder's use of the property needs the same discipline. If rent was
collected, the income cannot disappear from the file because it makes the
repayment smaller. If a cost improved the holder's preferred future project
rather than protecting the property, calling it a repair does not make it a
qualifying item. The statute controls the accounting categories; the holder's
labels do not.

When the qualifying person pays the full redemption amount, the purchaser is
repaid under the statutory process and loses rights in the land. The ending is
documented through a **discharge** of the certificate. The certificate that
created the middle state no longer supports continued control of Harbour Road.

That loss of rights is easy to understate when the certificate holder has spent
months protecting a building and imagining a future use. The purchaser may
receive the repayment required by the statute. The purchaser does not receive a
choice between the repayment and the land. Redemption is one of the outcomes
attached to the position purchased at sale.

The owner and interest holders were therefore present in the legal story even
when they were absent from Port Hood. The six-month door remained open to
qualifying people throughout the certificate period. Its closing through
redemption changes the certificate holder's job from protecting a conditional
interest to proving the accounting and releasing that interest.

Now give Harbour Road a different ending. Six months pass without redemption.
Or begin with a property whose taxes were already more than six years in
arrears, so the normal redemption right did not apply. The purchaser can move
toward a **tax deed**.

Under the municipal statutes, the tax deed vests fee-simple title free of
encumbrances, subject to the statutes' treatment of continuing easements and
rights-of-way. Those words are consequential. They require legal interpretation
against the actual parcel and records. They should not be shortened into “clean
title” during an auction conversation.

Fee simple describes a form of ownership. It does not report the physical
condition of the land. Freedom from encumbrances under the tax-deed provision
does not erase the stated treatment of easements and rights-of-way. The deed
also does not certify a boundary, create road access, approve development,
remove an occupant, supply insurance, or guarantee that the title will never be
contested.

Immediate deed is therefore a description of timing within the redemption
system. It means the normal six-month redemption period is absent for the
older-arrears category. “Immediate” does not promise that every practical and
legal question can be resolved on sale day.

The document sequence helps keep the distinction stable. A leading bid is an
auction event. Completed payment leads to the purchaser's statutory position.
A registered certificate governs the redeemable middle period. Full redemption
ends that position through repayment and discharge. Expiry without redemption,
or the older-arrears exception, permits the deed route. The registered tax deed
begins a different set of ownership, possession, title, planning, insurance,
and physical-property questions.

Consider two fictional parcels called at the same auction. Harbour Road is
marked redeemable. Quarry Lane is in the older-arrears category. Both purchasers
pay in full.

Four months later, Harbour Road is redeemed. Its purchaser supplies the records
needed for the statutory accounting, receives repayment through the process,
and loses rights in the land. The certificate is discharged.

Quarry Lane follows the deed branch without the normal six-month redemption
right. Its purchaser requests the deed and registers it. The deed supplies the
ownership document for that stage. It does not answer whether an apparent
driveway is a legal right-of-way or whether someone occupies a building.

If the listener can narrate those two endings, the redemption marker from
Chapter 2 has become a working mechanism. “Redeemable” points toward a
certificate period that may end in discharge or deed. “Non-redeemable” points
toward the deed route without that normal six-month door. Neither marker
describes possession, condition, access, insurability, or suitability.

Return once more to the relay-baton limit. The documents identify the stage,
but they do not make the course safe. A certificate holder can mishandle the
property. A deeded owner can discover an access problem. A discharged purchaser
can face a disputed accounting. Sequence prevents one category of mistake; it
does not remove the need for evidence and professional judgment.

Harbour Road leaves this chapter through discharge. Quarry Lane leaves through
a registered deed. The first purchaser's rights have ended. The second
purchaser's ownership work has begun. Neither ending permits the researcher to
skip the parcel biography that comes next.
