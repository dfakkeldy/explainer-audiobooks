## Chapter 9 — The Number You Decide Before the Room

### What a Result Sheet Can Tell You

A tax-sale result sheet can look like a list of answers. It places a recovery
amount beside a winning bid, sometimes beside an assessment, and invites the eye
to measure the distance between them. A small first number and a larger second
number can feel like proof that bidders discovered hidden value.

The sheet records competition. It does not record what the bidders knew, what
the property needed, whether the land was later redeemed, what legal work
followed, or whether the winner achieved an intended use.

Inverness County's May 2025 records make that boundary visible before any ratio
is calculated. The municipality advertised fifty properties. Later council
minutes report that thirty-five sold and fifteen were removed before the sale
after collection or legal advice. The public result sheet contains thirty-one
bid rows. The four-sale difference between the reported total and the published
rows remains unresolved.

All three counts belong in the account of the event. Fifty describes what was
advertised. Thirty-five describes the sales reported to council. Thirty-one
describes the rows available for calculation in the public result sheet. A
researcher who silently calls the thirty-one-row sheet “all sales” has changed
the source rather than clarified it.

Within those thirty-one published rows, the median winning bid was 4.53 times
the advertised recovery amount. Twenty-four were at least twice the recovery
amount, fifteen were at least five times, and seven were at least ten times.
The range ran from approximately the recovery amount to more than twenty-one
times it.

Those observations establish that competition often moved well beyond the
municipal recovery number in that dated sample. They do not establish that the
higher bids were correct, that the lower bids were bargains, or that a future
Inverness auction will behave the same way. The result sheet has no column for
legal access, remediation, possession, insurance, repair, financing, later
redemption, or regret.

The council minutes add another number: $608,693.21 in reported tax-sale
surplus. Subtracting the recovery amounts in the thirty-one public rows from
their bids produces a different total. That arithmetic covers a different row
set and is not the municipality's accounting definition of surplus. The two
measures should remain separate rather than be forced into agreement.

CBRM's official March 2026 result sheet supplies a useful contrast. Twenty-four
recorded sales contained both a minimum and a winning bid. Their median ratio
was 3.17, and the range extended from exactly the minimum to more than forty-two
times it. Eight sold at the minimum; nine reached at least five times the
minimum.

That mixture matters more than any one spectacular row. Competition was not a
single force applied evenly across the list. Some rows stopped immediately.
Others climbed far beyond the debt-recovery starting point. The result set
cannot say whether those differences came from parcel characteristics, bidder
research, mistaken identity, appetite for risk, intended use, or the people who
happened to attend.

Richmond County's June 2026 table is smaller still. Its three published sold
rows were about 1.33, 6.59, and 6.28 times the listed taxes, interest, and
charges. Three rows are enough to demonstrate variation within one event. They
are far too few to support a municipal price rule.

Cross-municipality comparison therefore requires more than placing ratios in
three columns. Each comparison carries the municipality, event date, sale
format, published-row count, denominator, and known completeness limit. A
minimum bid in one event and listed charges in another may have similar
economic roles without being perfectly interchangeable fields. Descriptive
samples can challenge folklore while remaining unsuitable for prediction.

The historical layer in NS Marks The Spot has the same narrow use. It can route
the listener to a dated official outcome and preserve the municipality, event,
PID, result label, amount, and source link. A selected historical parcel is a
record of that event. Its old bid is not a present appraisal, a comparable
sale, or a suggested maximum for the next parcel. The map shortens the route to
the source; it does not make the source answer a valuation question.

Assessment has a separate job. The assessed value supports the property-tax
system. It may help identify the account, classification, and scale of the
municipal record. It does not certify condition, access, title quality,
development feasibility, auction value, or a safe bid. Comparing a winning bid
with assessment can describe two published numbers. Calling the gap “equity”
would require conclusions those numbers do not contain.

Consider a fictional Cedar Street building with a four-figure recovery amount
and a six-figure assessment. The apparent discount arrives before the file has
answered whether the building is occupied, insurable, structurally usable, or
lawfully reachable for the intended work. Chapter 8's possession question,
Chapter 7's environmental questions, and Chapter 6's access and use questions
do not disappear when the price looks small. They become economic dependencies.

This is the problem that makes **all-in cost** useful. All-in cost is the total
exposure required to reach a defined use, including the bid, applicable taxes,
professional work, carrying, condition, possession, and a reserve for unresolved
uncertainty. The definition begins with a use because “own the parcel” is not a
complete economic destination. A year-round home, a serviced building lot, a
woodlot, and a long-term hold require different evidence and different work.

The cost stack resembles an iceberg. The winning bid is the visible tip. Legal
work, tax, survey, insurance, security, carrying, repair, remediation,
possession, and uncertainty can sit below the surface. The analogy has an
important limit: the submerged proportion is not fixed, and known costs should
be researched and estimated rather than treated as a mysterious mass. Its
purpose is to prevent the visible bid from impersonating the whole exposure.

An old result can now do one honest job in the Cedar Street file. It can show
that other bidders sometimes paid several multiples of municipal recovery
amounts in particular past events. It cannot say which multiple Cedar Street
deserves. Competition measures how the room behaved. It does not complete the
parcel biography.

After an interruption, the retrieval is compact. Which number describes debt
recovery? Which describes tax assessment? Which records a dated winning bid?
What facts would be required before any of them could support a present value
conclusion?

The next number will not be selected by multiplying the recovery amount. It
will be built backward from a defined use, supported value boundary, known
costs, unresolved dependencies, and the amount of uncertainty the fictional
bidder can safely carry.

### Build the Number Backward

Cedar Street's intended use is a modest rehabilitation project. That statement
does not make the project feasible. It gives the cost file a destination. The
researcher can now ask which expenditures are required before the building can
lawfully and safely reach that use.

The first group is acquisition and legal work. It can include the bid,
registration charges, title and deed review, searches, advice about continuing
interests, and any work needed to understand possession. A survey or boundary
opinion belongs here when the intended project depends on a fact that existing
records do not establish. Chapter 8's occupied-building question is now a cost
and timing dependency without becoming an invitation to guess at an eviction
budget.

The second group carries the property through time. Insurance may be available
only on particular terms, or may remain unresolved until an insurer understands
the building and legal stage. Taxes continue. Security, heat, utilities,
weather protection, financing, travel, and professional coordination can accrue
while title, redemption, possession, design, or approvals remain unfinished.
The calendar has a price even when no repair crew is working.

The third group concerns the intended physical use. Building work, water and
sewage, access, servicing, environmental assessment, remediation, demolition,
and permit-dependent work belong only where evidence supports them. An exterior
photograph cannot generate a reliable rehabilitation estimate. A missing
environmental record cannot generate a zero. A visible road cannot remove a
survey or access dependency.

The three cost groups can each contain different evidence states. Some items can
be quoted before the auction. Some can be bounded by a defensible range or
allowance. Other items, regardless of which cost group they occupy, cannot be
priced responsibly with the available access, time or evidence. An unpriceable
essential item needs a stop condition, not a larger miscellaneous line.

An **uncertainty reserve** is an amount held back for unresolved risks that are
real enough to affect the decision but cannot yet be quoted exactly. It is not
a payment to make ignorance disappear. The reserve works only when the bidder
can describe the uncertainty, explain why some range is tolerable, and survive
the adverse outcome without sacrificing the intended use or required
obligations.

An unknown foundation repair might support a reserve when exterior and
professional evidence provide a meaningful range. Unverified legal access for
a project that requires road access may remain a no-go. Unknown occupancy costs
may be unpriceable when the lawful process and timing cannot be established.
The reserve catches bounded variation. It does not bridge a missing legal right
or manufacture permission.

The road-book arithmetic can now stay small. Suppose the fictional file carries
a thirty-thousand-dollar bid, forty thousand dollars of known non-bid costs,
and a twenty-thousand-dollar uncertainty reserve. The all-in exposure is ninety
thousand dollars. The concrete reset is the structure, not the total: bid,
known costs, reserve.

The actual numbers for Cedar Street would require property-specific evidence.
Changing the intended use changes the file. So does a lawyer's title finding,
an insurer's refusal, a survey result, an environmental opinion, or a lawful
inspection. A cost stack should change when evidence changes. It should not
change because another bidder raises a card.

Tax questions branch before the ceiling is written. Nova Scotia's Municipal
Government Act exempts a deed given pursuant to a tax sale from municipal deed
transfer tax. That is a specific exemption. It does not eliminate registration
charges, HST, the provincial non-resident deed transfer tax, income-tax
consequences, or the cost of advice.

The current provincial non-resident deed transfer tax can apply to qualifying
non-residents acquiring residential property. For qualifying transfers after
March 2025, the Province states a ten-percent rate based on the non-resident
interest and the higher of purchase price or assessed value, subject to the
governing definitions and exemptions. One exemption route concerns qualifying
individuals who move to Nova Scotia within six months and provide the required
proof. A bidder should establish status and treatment with current provincial
guidance and professional advice before the auction. The assessment can matter
to this tax calculation even though it still does not establish market value.

HST is a different branch. CBRM's current event instructions say HST applies to
vacant land and commercially assessed property. That is an important rule for
a CBRM bidder to clarify and fund. It is not a safe universal sentence about
every Nova Scotia tax sale. Canada Revenue Agency guidance shows that real-
property treatment can depend on the seller, prior use, property, transaction,
and purchaser's registration status. The cost file should record the event's
terms and obtain case-specific tax advice rather than choose whichever reading
produces the lower ceiling.

Eligibility comes before price. The federal prohibition on certain direct or
indirect purchases of residential property by non-Canadians is currently
scheduled through January 1, 2027. The Act and regulations contain definitions
and exceptions, and current guidance discusses geography, vacant land, and
genuine development. Those categories require exact current review. An
auction's willingness to accept a bid does not certify that the bidder or
transaction is lawful.

The four branches can now be stated without calculation:

- Does the municipal tax-sale-deed exemption apply to this deed?
- Could the provincial non-resident tax apply to this buyer and property?
- What is the HST treatment of this particular transaction?
- Is the buyer eligible under current federal, provincial, and municipal law?

An unanswered branch does not automatically mean no bid. It means the file is
not ready until the effect is confirmed, bounded, or treated as a stop
condition. A tax large enough to change the project cannot be left for the
three-business-day payment window.

This produces the chapter's final term. A **maximum bid** is the written highest
amount the bidder is authorized to offer after accounting for the intended use,
supported value boundary, known costs, reserves, taxes, and no-go conditions.
It is a stopping rule, not a prediction of what the property is worth to
everyone else.

The first input needs its own boundary. A **supported value boundary** is the
highest all-in amount the bidder can defend for the stated use under explicit
title, condition and market assumptions. The bidder establishes it from
property-specific valuation evidence, using qualified appraisal or valuation
advice when the stakes or uncertainty require it. It is not the assessment,
recovery amount, a historical bid or the price another participant appears
willing to pay. If the available evidence cannot support that boundary before
the decision deadline, no defensible maximum can be calculated.

The number is built backward. The fictional bidder begins with that supported
boundary. Known non-bid costs and the uncertainty reserve consume part of that
boundary.
What remains can become the maximum bid if every essential legal, eligibility,
payment, and no-go condition is satisfied. If nothing remains, the research has
produced a no-bid result.

The ceiling also has to be fundable. Under the statutory payment structure, the
purchaser must make the required immediate payment and complete any remaining
balance within three business days. Current municipal terms control the
accepted methods and event details. A written maximum without ready funds is
not an executable decision.

Cedar Street's worksheet is complete before anyone enters the room. It records
the intended use, source dates, professional findings, known costs, reserve,
tax and eligibility branches, payment readiness, maximum bid, and the facts
that would cancel authority to participate. The worksheet contains no line for
“one more increment because the room is excited.”

Suppose the live auction reaches the written maximum and another card rises.
No new title document has appeared. The building has not become easier to
insure. The occupants have not left. The environmental and access questions
have not improved. The only new fact is that another person will pay more.

The fictional bidder lowers the card. That action does not lose Cedar Street.
It executes the decision the research supported. The winning number in this
chapter is the one that still protects the listener after the room has moved
past it.
