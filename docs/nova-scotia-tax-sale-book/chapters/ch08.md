## Chapter 8 — Title Is Not Possession

### What the Deed Actually Changes

Before the book returns to pre-bid valuation, this chapter deliberately steps
forward to a deeded case. The flash-forward isolates one question a bidder must
understand in advance: what a deed would change, and what it would leave for
later. The fictional Foundry Street property's redemption question has been
resolved, the municipal fee has been paid, and a tax deed has been registered.
A search of the correct PID now has a new document at its centre.

The deed matters. It changes the legal relationship to the land. It does not
answer every question that shares the property's address.

Under Nova Scotia's Municipal Government Act, the tax deed is evidence that the
statutory sale provisions were followed. The Act says the deed vests the land in
the grantee in **fee simple**. Fee simple is the broad form of private ownership
estate ordinarily meant when people speak of owning land. The phrase describes
the legal interest conveyed. It does not describe the building's condition,
identify every person inside, certify a development plan, or turn a graphical
parcel boundary into a survey.

The Act also says the land is vested free and discharged from all
**encumbrances**. An encumbrance is a claim, interest, charge, or burden that can
affect title to land. That statutory language is powerful. It has a title job,
and the exact job must be read with the rest of the statute, any court order,
the deed, and the property record.

Foundry Street's older parcel register shows a mortgage, a judgment, and a
registered right-of-way serving the house behind it. A hurried summary might
say that the tax deed erased everything. The statute itself prevents that
summary. When an easement or right-of-way benefits the land being sold, the
benefit passes with the land. When the land being sold is burdened by an
easement or right-of-way, the sale does not terminate or affect that continuing
relationship as the Act specifies.

The right-of-way retrieves the access lesson from Chapter 6 in a fresh
direction. There, the question was whether a route gave the tax-sale parcel a
legally supportable way out. Here, the Foundry Street parcel may be the route
another parcel relies on. The same registered relationship can benefit one
piece of land and burden another. A deed does not create a private island around
whichever PID changed hands most recently.

The title file therefore needs more than the phrase “free of encumbrances.” It
needs the exact deed, the current parcel register, the instruments behind
relevant entries, the legal description, and a lawyer's interpretation of how
the governing provisions apply to this parcel. If a right-of-way continues,
the file also needs its route, purpose, width, parties, maintenance language,
and any other terms the source instruments actually contain. None should be
reconstructed from a driveway line in imagery.

There can also be a court-order branch. A municipal treasurer may apply to a
court for findings about tax arrears and for an order addressing the interests
the deed will convey, the interests it will remain subject to, notice, and the
manner of sale. A sale conducted under that route takes its legal effect from
the statute and the actual order, including any exceptions, exclusions, or
partial interests set out there.

That makes the order part of the Foundry Street question, if an order exists.
The useful note is not “court approved,” as though two words resolved the
parcel. It is:

> Confirm whether this sale proceeded under a court order. If so, obtain the
> order and have counsel identify the conveyed interest, every stated
> exception or exclusion, the redemption treatment, and the relationship
> between the order, deed, parcel register, and source instruments.

One money question must be kept outside this title analysis. A prior interest
holder may have a separate statutory route to claim a share of tax-sale surplus
proceeds. That possible proceeds claim is not automatically a continuing burden
on the land. Chapter 12 follows the money route; this chapter stays with title
and possession.

This is why “clear title” is an unsafe all-purpose label. A municipality may
describe the statutory deed process, yet current municipal guidance also warns
bidders that clear title is not guaranteed. The two statements are not enemies.
One describes the legal effect the statute gives a qualifying deed. The other
refuses to promise that a municipality's auction page has completed a
property-specific title opinion, found every factual problem, resolved
possession, or made the interest readily insurable or marketable for a
particular transaction.

Title quality can require questions beyond a list of visible register entries.
Which statute governs this municipality? Was there a court order? What exactly
did it say? Does the legal description match the land the listener thought was
being researched? Which easements or rights-of-way benefit or burden the land?
Do the documents reveal an exception, exclusion, partial interest, boundary
issue, or other qualification? Is another statute or property-specific record
relevant? Those questions belong with a Nova Scotia property lawyer reviewing
the actual file. The book cannot settle them by naming categories that might or
might not apply.

The careful explanation preserves the deed's force without turning it into a
magic eraser. Foundry Street's tax deed may discharge interests the statute
discharges. Its registered right-of-way may continue. A court order may define
the interest or preserve an exception. A former interest holder may have a
route to surplus money rather than a surviving claim against the parcel. Each
result comes from its governing source.

After an interruption, the recovery exercise is short. What did the deed
convey? Which easement or right-of-way continues under the deed-effect rule? Was
the sale shaped by a court order? Which remaining conclusion requires the
lawyer's review of the exact PID and documents?

The answer should never depend on the reassuring sound of “fee simple” or “free
of encumbrances” alone. Those phrases have legal content. Their safe use is
specific: identify the interest conveyed, preserve the statute's continuing
relationships and order-specific qualifications, and ask counsel to state the
result for this property.

One question still sits outside the title file. Foundry Street is not empty.
Lights are on in two windows, a vehicle moves between parking spaces, and mail
continues to arrive. The deed has changed. The human scene has not disappeared.

### The House Does Not Empty on Paper

Nothing in the exterior scene identifies the occupants. They might include a
former owner, a tenant, a family member, a guest, or someone whose situation
does not fit a quick label. A parked vehicle does not establish a tenancy. A
curtain in a window does not establish abandonment. Even a conversation at the
door would not give an untrained purchaser the complete legal facts.

Current CBRM guidance supplies the operational example: occupied properties can
be sold, but that municipality does not remove residents or provide access.
Other events require their own current municipal materials. In every case,
the auction itself is not a substitute for the lawful, fact-specific possession
process. The deed is not a key handed across the registration counter.

This introduces **vacant possession**: the state in which the buyer can lawfully
take possession of the property without another person remaining entitled to
occupy it. Whether and how that state can be obtained depends on the actual
facts and applicable law. It cannot be inferred from “immediate deed,” a paid
balance, or the absence of a tenant's name in the sale advertisement.

Title and possession resemble a name on a coat-check ticket and the coat in
hand. The ticket resembles evidence of entitlement. Holding the coat resembles
actual control. One does not become the other merely because the entitlement
is strong. The analogy stops there. Land, homes, tenancies, court remedies, and
human safety are vastly more complex than a coat check. The ticket does not
authorize self-help, and neither does this comparison.

Foundry Street therefore calls for restraint before classification. No locks
are changed. No utilities are interrupted. No belongings are moved. No demand
for rent or departure is improvised at the door. Those choices could affect
people's rights and safety, destroy evidence about the occupancy arrangement,
or make a difficult legal problem worse.

The research file can still become more precise. Counsel may need to determine
who is present, what documents or agreements exist, whether residential-tenancy
law applies, what notices or proceedings are legally required, and how access
can be obtained. The facts may lead to different processes for an owner who
remains, a tenant, an apparently empty unit containing goods, or an active
dispute. This chapter deliberately does not select or teach one of those
processes.

The same boundary reaches back into the redemption period. Chapter 3 explained
that a certificate holder has limited statutory powers and duties, including
protecting the property, avoiding injury, and collecting rent in the
circumstances the statute describes. Those provisions assign responsibility;
they do not turn uncertainty about an occupant into permission for locks,
confrontation, or an invented eviction route. The current document and legal
stage still control the holder's job.

Goods inside create another branch. A room that appears unused may still
contain someone's furniture, records, medicine, tools, or personal effects.
Nova Scotia's residential-tenancy system has specific resources and forms for
tenant property left behind. Their existence demonstrates that “looks
abandoned” is not a disposal standard. The applicable facts and lawful process
need to be established before anyone treats goods as abandoned.

For a pre-bid listener, possession becomes an input to the decision rather than
an afterthought. The likely cost and time of property-specific legal work may be
unknown. Interior condition may remain unverified. Insurance, repairs, security,
and intended use may depend on lawful access that the auction materials have not
supplied. A bidder who cannot tolerate those unresolved dependencies has learned
something decisive without approaching the door.

Now change one fact. Instead of an occupied apartment building, the listing
involves a manufactured home. The familiar PID appears in the notice, and the
map shows a land parcel beneath the symbol. That is not enough to establish what
asset is being sold.

Nova Scotia has specific tax-sale regulations and prescribed forms for mobile
homes. The legal file must first identify whether the sale concerns land, a
mobile home, or some combination described by the controlling documents. An
unaffixed home can also raise personal-property questions. Nova Scotia's
Personal Property Registry is the system used to search for security interests
in personal property, including mobile and manufactured homes. It is not the
land registry. A clean-looking PID search does not answer a search that belongs
in the personal-property system.

Location introduces a third record family. A manufactured home may sit on land
owned by someone else in a land-lease community. Nova Scotia's Residential
Tenancies framework applies to those communities and includes rules about the
space, the tenancy relationship, and sale of a home that remains there. Ownership
of the home, ownership of the ground, the right to keep the home on the space,
and any registered personal-property security are distinct questions.

Imagine a fictional Harbour Park listing. The municipal notice uses a mobile
home identifier and a civic location. The map selects the underlying land PID.
A roadside view shows a manufactured home on a serviced pad. None of those
facts establishes that the mobile home and land share an owner, that the home is
affixed to the land, that the pad agreement transfers, that a personal-property
lien is absent, or that the home can remain where it sits after a sale.

The bounded file reads:

> Asset identity unresolved. Confirm from the current municipal notice,
> prescribed sale documents, land records, mobile-home records, and counsel
> whether the sale concerns the home, the land, or both. Search the appropriate
> personal-property system for the home and determine whether a land-lease
> relationship applies. No PID-only conclusion is supported.

That note may feel slower than selecting the parcel and reading the aerial. It
is faster than discovering after payment that the mapped ground and visible
home belong to different legal files.

Foundry Street and Harbour Park now share one recovery exercise. What do you
own? What burdens or benefits it? Who is there, and under what status? If a
mobile home is involved, what exactly is the asset and which registry or tenancy
system governs each part?

The questions do not all need favourable answers. They need supportable answers
or explicit stop conditions before a bid. Title counsel can interpret the deed
and land interests. Property and tenancy counsel can identify the lawful
possession route. The municipality can clarify its current sale documents and
event terms without becoming the purchaser's lawyer or access provider. The
Personal Property Registry and land records answer different searches. A map
can route the researcher to the right parcel and source; it cannot merge those
legal systems.

The dramatic action in an occupied tax sale is often the action not taken. No
lock turns because an auction concluded. No occupant becomes a trespasser by
assumption. No box of belongings becomes garbage by appearance. No home and
land become one asset because they occupy the same pixels.

The deed answers a title question. Vacant possession is a separate researched
outcome. Keeping those statements apart protects the accuracy of the file and
the people whose lives do not vanish when ownership paperwork changes.
