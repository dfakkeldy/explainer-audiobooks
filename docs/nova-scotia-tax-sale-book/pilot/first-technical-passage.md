### The First Clock

Start with the fact that the municipality is not choosing Harbour Road because
it thinks the parcel will appeal to buyers. The process begins somewhere else:
real-property taxes remain unpaid, and the debt attaches to the property with a
legal priority of its own.

That attachment is the first term we need. A **tax lien** is the municipality's
legal claim against the property for the unpaid tax. Under Nova Scotia's
municipal statutes, it ranks ahead of other claims, liens, and encumbrances and
does not have to be registered to hold that priority. This is why the treasurer
is in our final scene. The municipality is enforcing a charge created by law,
not acting as an owner who has decided to list a property.

The lien does not send Harbour Road straight to an auction. It starts a clock.

Outside Halifax, the Municipal Government Act permits proceedings for taxes
left unpaid from the immediately preceding taxation year, but not before June
thirtieth of the following year. It also sets a longer backstop: subject to the
Act's exceptions and a possible council deferral, property must be put up for
sale once taxes are unpaid for the three preceding fiscal years. A municipality
may operate within that window using a shorter trigger. Inverness and Chester,
for example, publicly describe accounts as eligible after two years.

Those statements are not competing versions of the same deadline. One is the
provincial legal frame. The other is a municipality describing how it operates
inside that frame. If Harbour Road appeared in a different municipality, we
would read that municipality's current process and current notice rather than
borrowing Inverness County's practice.

Now put the people back into the clock.

Before a sale, the municipality sends a preliminary notice allowing at least
fourteen days to pay. After that period, it conducts a title search and may
order a survey. The search is not a decorative formality. It helps identify the
owner and people whose mortgages, liens, or charges give them an interest in
what is about to happen. Those parties then receive a further notice allowing
sixty days to pay before the sale.

That further document is our second term: the **notice of intent**. It is a
formal warning that the municipality intends to sell the property if the
amounts are not paid. The bidder has still not entered the story. The owner and
interest holders have. The title search, possible survey, and notices explain
why the treasurer's file is organized around legal identity and process rather
than curb appeal.

Only after that does the sale become broadly visible. A sale or tender must
receive at least thirty consecutive days of qualifying public notice, either
through newspaper publication that meets the statute or by posting on the
municipal website. The advertisement may identify the property briefly and
direct the reader to the treasurer for the full description.

Imagine that a municipal website posts this fictional line: “Harbour Road,
parcel identifier ending four-two-one, taxes and expenses owing.” Is that line
trying to persuade you that Harbour Road is desirable?

No. Its job is to identify the property in a collection process and tell the
public where to seek the fuller record. It is notice, not marketing. That
distinction carries forward into the map. A marker or parcel outline derived
from an official notice helps you find the event and the apparent parcel. It
does not turn the municipality into the property's seller in the ordinary real
estate sense, and it does not turn the notice into a condition report.

The process can end in more than one kind of event. The statutory default is a
public auction. With council's consent, the treasurer may call for tenders
instead, and council may define an acceptable minimum where the property may
not recover all taxes, interest, and expenses. Dated municipal examples show
the variation: Inverness and Richmond have used open auctions; Pictou has used
a sealed tender; Annapolis has moved unsold auction properties into tender
treatment; and Chester says it will not hold a 2026 sale.

The common legal frame does not create one province-wide event calendar or one
set of bidding instructions. It tells you why a sale can occur. The current
municipal notice tells you whether this sale is occurring, in what form, and
under which local instructions.

Halifax requires one more piece of discipline. It has a parallel tax-sale
framework in the Halifax Regional Municipality Charter rather than the tax-sale
division of the Municipal Government Act. The broad sequence is recognizably
similar, but the correct authority is different. Halifax's administrative rules
also make the hidden work in the file unusually visible by listing possible
sale-expense categories: notice, title search, survey, certificate and deed
preparation, registration, closing, and redemption administration. Those
categories help us see what a process can involve. They are not a price list for
Inverness, Pictou, or any other municipality.

Here is the retrieval test. Suppose the map shows two current events: an
Inverness auction and a Halifax sale notice. You want to know how Halifax will
conduct its event. Which source controls: the familiar Inverness packet, the
provincial map marker, or Halifax's current notice under its own Charter
framework?

Halifax's current notice does. The packet can teach you what careful municipal
information looks like, and the map can route you to the right place. Neither
can silently replace the authority that owns the event.

We can now name the whole visible process. A **tax sale** is the statutory
collection process through which the municipality offers the property after
the required steps. It is not an ordinary listing with an unusually low asking
price. Its public face arrives only after a lien, elapsed time, record work, and
notices involving people who may never appear in the auction room.

Return to Port Hood at nine twenty-nine. The treasurer's list is no longer a
menu of parcels that happened to become available. Each row is the far end of a
municipal file and a legal clock. When the first parcel is called, the
municipality is there to collect under that process. It is not there to tell a
bidder what Harbour Road is worth, whether it suits a plan, or what uncertainty
remains after the hammer falls.
