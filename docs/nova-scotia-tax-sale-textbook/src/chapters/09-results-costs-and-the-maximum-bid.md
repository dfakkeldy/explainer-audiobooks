# Results, Costs and the Maximum Bid

::: {.chain links="unknowns,handoff"}
Research chain: notice → parcel → context → **unknowns** → **handoff**. This chapter turns the
file's unknowns into costs, reserves and stop conditions, and hands the tax, valuation and
eligibility questions to the people who can answer them.
:::

::: {.objectives}
1. Read a result sheet with its completeness limits (50 advertised, 35 sold, 31 rows).
2. Describe the Inverness 2025, CBRM March 2026 and Richmond June 2026 samples and why they cannot
   predict.
3. Build an all-in cost from a defined use, with an uncertainty reserve that does not bridge a
   missing right.
4. Identify the four tax and eligibility branches.
5. Derive a maximum bid backward from a supported value boundary, and check it is fundable.
:::

## What a result sheet can tell you {#sec-09-result-sheet}

A tax-sale result sheet can look like a list of answers. It places a recovery amount beside a
winning bid, sometimes beside an assessment, and invites the eye to measure the distance between
them. A small first number and a larger second number can feel like proof that bidders discovered
hidden value.

The sheet records competition. It does not record:

- what the bidders knew;
- what the property needed;
- whether the land was later redeemed;
- what legal work followed;
- whether the winner achieved an intended use.

### Three counts for one event

Inverness County's May 2025 records make that boundary visible before any ratio is calculated:

- The municipality advertised 50 properties.
- Later council minutes report that 35 sold and 15 were removed before the sale after collection
  or legal advice.
- The public result sheet contains 31 bid rows.

The four-sale difference between the reported total and the published rows remains unresolved.

All three counts belong in the account of the event, because each answers a different question.
Fifty describes what was advertised. Thirty-five describes the sales reported to council.
Thirty-one describes the rows available for calculation in the public result sheet. A researcher
who silently calls the 31-row sheet "all sales" has changed the source rather than clarified it.

### What the 31 rows show

Within those 31 published rows, the median winning bid was 4.53 times the advertised recovery
amount. Twenty-four were at least twice the recovery amount, 15 were at least five times, and seven
were at least ten times. The range ran from approximately the recovery amount to more than 21
times it.

Those observations establish that competition often moved well beyond the municipal recovery
number in that dated sample. They do not establish that the higher bids were correct, that the
lower bids were bargains, or that a future Inverness auction will behave the same way.

### Two measures of surplus

The council minutes add another number: $608,693.21 in reported tax-sale surplus. Subtracting the
recovery amounts in the 31 public rows from their bids produces a different total. That arithmetic
covers a different row set and is not the municipality's accounting definition of surplus. Keep the
two measures separate rather than forcing them into agreement.

### CBRM and Richmond

CBRM's official March 2026 result sheet supplies a useful contrast. Twenty-four recorded sales
contained both a minimum and a winning bid. Their median ratio was 3.17, and the range extended
from exactly the minimum to more than 42 times it. Eight sold at the minimum; nine reached at least
five times the minimum.

That mixture matters more than any one spectacular row. Competition was not a single force applied
evenly across the list. Some rows stopped immediately. Others climbed far beyond the debt-recovery
starting point. The result set cannot say whether those differences came from parcel
characteristics, bidder research, mistaken identity, appetite for risk, intended use, or the people
who happened to attend.

Richmond County's June 2026 table is smaller still. Its three published sold rows were about 1.33,
6.59, and 6.28 times the listed taxes, interest, and charges. Three rows are enough to demonstrate
variation within one event. They are far too few to support a municipal price rule.

### Comparing municipalities

Cross-municipality comparison therefore requires more than placing ratios in three columns. Each
comparison carries:

- the municipality;
- the event date;
- the sale format;
- the published-row count;
- the denominator;
- the known completeness limit.

A minimum bid in one event and listed charges in another may have similar economic roles without
being perfectly interchangeable fields. Descriptive samples can challenge folklore while remaining
unsuitable for prediction.

![**Definitions travel with the numbers.** Cross-municipal numbers are useful only when procedure, sample and denominator travel with them. ### Historical outcomes and assessment

The historical layer in NS Marks The Spot has the same narrow use. It routes the researcher to a dated
official outcome and preserves the municipality, event, PID, result label, amount, and source
link. A selected historical parcel is a record of that event.

| A historical bid is | It is not |
|---|---|
| A record of that event | A present appraisal |
| | A comparable sale |
| | A suggested maximum for the next parcel |

Assessment has a separate job. The assessed value supports the property-tax system. It helps identify
the account, classification, and scale of the municipal record. It does not certify
condition, access, title quality, development feasibility, auction value, or a safe bid. Comparing a
winning bid with assessment describes two published numbers. Calling the gap "equity" would
require conclusions those numbers do not contain.

### All-in cost

This is the problem that makes **all-in cost** useful. All-in cost is the total exposure required
to reach a defined use, including the bid, applicable taxes, professional work, carrying,
condition, possession, and a reserve for unresolved uncertainty.

The definition begins with a use because "own the parcel" is not a complete economic destination. A
year-round home, a serviced building lot, a woodlot, and a long-term hold require different
evidence and different work.

::: {.rail label="A way to picture it"}
The cost stack resembles an iceberg. The winning bid is the visible tip. Legal work, tax, survey,
insurance, security, carrying, repair, remediation, possession, and uncertainty can sit below the
surface. The analogy has an important limit: the submerged proportion is not fixed, and known costs
should be researched and estimated rather than treated as a mysterious mass. Its purpose is to
prevent the visible bid from impersonating the whole exposure.
:::

![**The bid is only the first layer.** The winning bid is one layer in the acquisition's uncertainty budget. The bid is the visible layer; tax, legal and registry work, survey, insurance, carrying, repair and remediation, possession and an uncertainty reserve can sit below the waterline. Layers are drawn the same size: the submerged proportion is not fixed. Not to scale."}

::: {.recall}
1. Which number describes debt recovery? **The recovery amount**, the municipal recovery number
   (in CBRM's results, the minimum bid, the debt-recovery starting point).
2. Which describes tax assessment? **The assessed value**, which supports the property-tax system.
3. Which records a dated winning bid? **A row on a result sheet**, or a historical outcome that
   routes to one.
4. What facts would be required before any of them could support a present value conclusion?
   **Property-specific facts** that none of them records, such as legal access, condition,
   possession, insurance and the intended use, and valuation evidence for that use.
:::

The next number will not be selected by multiplying the recovery amount. It will be built backward
from a defined use, supported value boundary, known costs, unresolved dependencies, and the amount
of uncertainty the fictional bidder can safely carry.

## Build the number backward {#sec-09-build-backward}

::: {.case name="Cedar Street"}
Composite case — not a real property.

The fictional Cedar Street's intended use is a modest rehabilitation project. The statement gives
the cost file a destination. The researcher can now ask
which expenditures are required before the building can lawfully and safely reach that use.
:::

### Three cost groups

**Acquisition and legal work.** The first group includes the bid, registration charges, title
and deed review, searches, advice about continuing interests, and any work needed to understand
possession. A survey or boundary opinion belongs here when the intended project depends on a fact
that existing records do not establish. Chapter 8's occupied-building question is now a cost and
timing dependency without becoming an invitation to guess at an eviction budget.

**Carrying the property through time.** The second group carries the property through time. Insurance
may be available only on particular terms, or may remain unresolved until an insurer understands
the building and legal stage. Taxes continue. Security, heat, utilities, weather protection,
financing, travel, and professional coordination accrue while title, redemption, possession,
design, or approvals remain unfinished. The calendar has a price even when no repair crew is
working.

**The intended physical use.** The third group covers building work, water and sewage, access,
servicing, environmental assessment, remediation, demolition, and permit-dependent work. Each
belongs only where evidence supports it.

| This evidence | Cannot generate |
|---|---|
| An exterior photograph | A reliable rehabilitation estimate |
| A missing environmental record | A zero |
| A visible road | The removal of a survey or access dependency |

### Evidence states within the groups

Each of the three cost groups holds items in different evidence states:

- Some items can be quoted before the auction.
- Some can be bounded by a defensible range or allowance.
- Other items cannot be priced responsibly with the available access, time or evidence.

An unpriceable essential item needs a stop condition, not a larger miscellaneous line.

### The uncertainty reserve

An **uncertainty reserve** is an amount held back for unresolved risks that are real enough to
affect the decision but cannot yet be quoted exactly. The reserve works only when the bidder can:

- describe the uncertainty;
- explain why some range is tolerable;
- survive the adverse outcome without sacrificing the intended use or required obligations.

Three examples show where the reserve's job ends:

| Unknown | Treatment |
|---|---|
| An unknown foundation repair | Might support a reserve when exterior and professional evidence provide a meaningful range |
| Unverified legal access, for a project that requires road access | May remain a no-go |
| Unknown occupancy costs | May be unpriceable when the lawful process and timing cannot be established |

The reserve catches bounded variation. It does not bridge a missing legal right or manufacture
permission.

### A worked example

The arithmetic can stay small. Suppose the fictional file carries a $30,000 bid, $40,000 of known
non-bid costs, and a $20,000 uncertainty reserve:

| Layer | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Bid | $30,000 |
| Known non-bid costs | $40,000 |
| Uncertainty reserve | $20,000 |
| **All-in exposure** | **$90,000** |

The point to keep is the structure, not the total: bid, known costs, reserve.

Changing the intended use changes the file. So does a lawyer's title finding, an insurer's refusal, a survey result, an
environmental opinion, or a lawful inspection. A cost stack should change when evidence changes. It
should not change because another bidder raises a card.

### Tax and eligibility branches

Tax questions branch before the ceiling is written.

**Municipal deed transfer tax.** Nova Scotia's Municipal Government Act exempts a deed given
pursuant to a tax sale from municipal deed transfer tax. That is a specific exemption. It does not
eliminate registration charges, HST, the provincial non-resident deed transfer tax, income-tax
consequences, or the cost of advice.

**Provincial non-resident deed transfer tax.** The current provincial non-resident deed transfer
tax can apply to qualifying non-residents acquiring residential property. For qualifying transfers
after March 2025, the Province states a 10 percent rate based on the non-resident interest and the
higher of purchase price or assessed value, subject to the governing definitions and exemptions.
One exemption route concerns qualifying individuals who move to Nova Scotia within six months and
provide the required proof. A bidder should establish status and treatment with current provincial
guidance and professional advice before the auction. The assessment can matter to this tax
calculation.

**HST.** HST is a different branch. CBRM's current event instructions say HST applies to vacant land
and commercially assessed property. That is an important rule for a CBRM bidder to clarify and
fund. It is not a safe universal sentence about every Nova Scotia tax sale. Canada Revenue Agency
guidance shows that real-property treatment can depend on the seller, prior use, property,
transaction, and purchaser's registration status. The cost file should record the event's terms and
obtain case-specific tax advice rather than choose whichever reading produces the lower ceiling.

**Eligibility.** Eligibility comes before price. The federal prohibition on certain direct or
indirect purchases of residential property by non-Canadians is currently scheduled through
January 1, 2027. The Act and regulations contain definitions and exceptions, and current guidance
discusses geography, vacant land, and genuine development. An auction's willingness to accept a bid does not certify that the bidder or transaction is
lawful.

The four branches can now be stated without calculation:

| Branch | Question for the file |
|---|---|
| Municipal deed transfer tax | Does the municipal tax-sale-deed exemption apply to this deed? |
| Provincial non-resident tax | Could the provincial non-resident tax apply to this buyer and property? |
| HST | What is the HST treatment of this particular transaction? |
| Eligibility | Is the buyer eligible under current federal, provincial, and municipal law? |

An unanswered branch does not automatically mean no bid. It means the file is not ready until the
effect is confirmed, bounded, or treated as a stop condition. A tax large enough to change the
project cannot be left for the three-business-day payment window.

![**Four branches before the ceiling.** Each tax and eligibility question carries its own dated fact, as of July 2026. The municipal tax-sale-deed exemption does not eliminate the other three; CBRM's HST instruction is an event rule, not a universal one; the federal prohibition is currently scheduled through January 1, 2027. ### The maximum bid

This produces the chapter's final term. A **maximum bid** is the written highest amount the bidder
is authorized to offer after accounting for the intended use, supported value boundary, known
costs, reserves, taxes, and no-go conditions. It is a stopping rule, not a prediction of what the
property is worth to everyone else.

The first input needs its own boundary. A **supported value boundary** is the highest all-in amount
the bidder can defend for the stated use under explicit title, condition and market assumptions.
The bidder establishes it from property-specific valuation evidence, using qualified appraisal or
valuation advice when the stakes or uncertainty require it.

It is not the assessment, recovery amount, a historical bid or the price another participant
appears willing to pay. If the available evidence cannot support that boundary before the decision
deadline, no defensible maximum can be calculated.

The number is built backward ([Figure 9.6](#fig-09-build-backward)):

1. The fictional bidder begins with that supported boundary.
2. Known non-bid costs and the uncertainty reserve consume part of that boundary.
3. What remains can become the maximum bid if every essential legal, eligibility, payment, and
   no-go condition is satisfied.

If nothing remains, the research has produced a no-bid result.

![**Exposure and the maximum, kept apart.** Panel a adds up the fictional file's all-in exposure; panel b, which has no amounts and is not to scale, shows the maximum bid built backward from a supported value boundary. The maximum stands only if every essential legal, eligibility, payment and no-go condition is satisfied and the amount is fundable; if nothing remains, the result is no bid. ### Funding the ceiling

The ceiling also has to be fundable. Under the statutory payment structure, the purchaser must make
the required immediate payment and complete any remaining balance within three business days.
Current municipal terms control the accepted methods and event details. A written maximum without
ready funds is not an executable decision.

### The card that stays down

::: {.case name="Cedar Street"}
Composite case — not a real property.

The fictional Cedar Street's worksheet is complete before anyone enters the room. It records:

- the intended use;
- source dates;
- professional findings;
- known costs;
- reserve;
- tax and eligibility branches;
- payment readiness;
- maximum bid;
- the facts that would cancel authority to participate.

The worksheet contains no line for "one more increment because the room is excited."

Suppose the live auction reaches the written maximum and another card rises. No new title document
has appeared. The building has not become easier to insure. The occupants have not left. The
environmental and access questions have not improved. The only new fact is that another person will
pay more.

The fictional bidder lowers the card. That action does not lose Cedar Street. It executes the
decision the research supported.
:::

The winning number in this chapter is the one that still protects the bidder after the room has
moved past it.
