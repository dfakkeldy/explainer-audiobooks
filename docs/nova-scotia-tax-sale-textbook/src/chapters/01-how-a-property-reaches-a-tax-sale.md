# How a Property Reaches a Tax Sale

::: {.chain links="notice"}
Research chain: **notice** → parcel → context → unknowns → handoff. This chapter covers the notice
and the process behind it.
:::

::: {.objectives}
1. Explain why a municipality sells land for taxes, using the terms tax lien, notice of intent and
   tax sale.
2. Put the pre-sale steps in order with their periods (at least 14 days, 60 days, at least 30
   consecutive days) and the window in which proceedings can begin.
3. Distinguish the provincial legal frame, a municipality's practice inside it and the current
   event notice, and say which one governs a Halifax event.
4. Say what the Inverness packet establishes and what it cannot decide.
:::

::: {.keyterms}
- tax lien
- notice of intent
- tax sale
:::

## The packet and the question it raises {#sec-01-packet}

Picture a fictionalized August auction room in Port Hood shortly before a sale begins. The place
comes from the Inverness notice; the room details are a composite, not event instructions. A
municipal treasurer has a list. Registered bidders wait for a parcel to be called. Somewhere
outside the room are owners, lenders, heirs, neighbours and perhaps occupants, whose lives do not
fit neatly into the row about to be called.

![**Auction morning is collection work.** A tax sale begins as municipal collection work, not a treasure hunt. The room is a composite, not a depiction of a real venue or event instructions. Illustration from the 2026 edition (figure-01), shown without its slide title band.](../assets/figures/kept/fig-01-auction-morning.png){#fig-01-auction-morning alt="Editorial illustration of a quiet Cape Breton community hall on an auction morning, with folders and bidder cards visible through the entrance."}

The auction is the visible part of a tax sale. It is also the last scene in a much longer story.

### What the Inverness packet contains

A reader who first meets a Nova Scotia tax sale through an advertisement is tempted to begin with
the shortest line: location, parcel number, amount owing. The Municipality of the County of
Inverness gives much more than that. Its August 2026 packet is a substantial document. A typical
detailed entry combines:

- municipal account and parcel facts;
- an aerial image with a property overlay;
- a Property Online map;
- a registry legal description.

The packet also warns that the map is not a survey, and it encourages title research.

That is useful work. It saves a curious member of the public from starting with a blank page. It
also raises a better question than "What can I add to this packet?" The better question is "What
does each part of this packet actually establish?"

### What each part can and cannot establish

::: {.case name="Harbour Road"}
Composite case — not a real property.

Harbour Road is a fictional composite parcel. It is not one of the live properties, and it has no
owner attached to it. Its municipal page gives it an account number and a parcel identifier. The
page places a shape over an aerial photograph, reports assessment information and includes words
from the land registry. It may say whether a dwelling is listed.

Each of those facts can be valuable. None of them quietly answers all the other questions a bidder
might carry into the room.

For Harbour Road, the municipal packet can establish that the municipality published a particular
set of information for a dated sale. It can help locate the apparent parcel, identify the account
being offered, and begin registry and planning research. It can show what the municipality knew
well enough to put in its own information product. It cannot make a purchase decision for a bidder.
:::

Each item in a packet does one job. The table sets each item beside what it does not establish.

| Packet item | What it does not establish |
|---|---|
| Parcel shape | A boundary survey |
| Aerial photograph | A current inspection |
| Legal description (words from the land registry) | A plain-language promise of road access |
| Assessment information | A condition report, or an appraisal prepared for this sale |
| A line saying that no dwelling is listed | Proof that the ground is empty, safe, buildable, serviced, or available for immediate possession |

Even a thick packet remains a collection of sources with different jobs.

::: careful
This distinction is easy to lose because maps feel conclusive. A coloured line around a piece of
land has the visual authority of an answer. It may be an excellent orientation tool, and it may
help you find the right record and ask a better question. But the line does not know whether an
old right-of-way remains effective, whether a driveway crosses another parcel, whether a structure
has changed since the image was captured, or whether somebody lawfully occupies the property.
:::

The municipal packet does not claim to settle those matters. Trouble begins when a reader silently
asks it to.

### When official records disagree

The packet also shows something less dramatic and more useful: official records can disagree
without either record becoming worthless.

- The first-page schedule for the August sale contains 45 lien entries.
- The detailed portion contains 44 property sheets.
- Lien 6 appears in the summary but has no matching detailed sheet in the downloaded packet.
- Two recovery amounts differ between the summary and their detailed pages.

These differences do not prove that a parcel is defective, and they do not tell you which number
controls on sale day. They produce a question for the municipality. Careful research often looks
like this: you preserve the conflict, name the two sources, and ask the authority that owns the
process. You do not choose the friendlier number because it makes the arithmetic work.

::: {.rail label="The idea"}
This is the first skill the book keeps returning to: separate the record from the conclusion you
want to draw from it.
:::

### From one packet to a method

The work that remains after the packet is not one giant mystery called due diligence. It is a set
of smaller questions that belong to different sources and, sometimes, to different professionals:

- Which legal interest is being sold?
- Which parcel does the identifier refer to?
- Is there lawful access?
- What uses do current planning rules permit?
- What is physically on the land?
- Is anyone in possession?
- What could happen between a successful bid and a deed?
- Which answers can a public record support, and which require a lawyer, surveyor, planner,
  inspector, insurer or other qualified professional?

The route through those questions is **notice, parcel, context, unknowns and handoff**. Later
chapters carry the resulting files into a written bid decision, the sale, the certificate period
and the deed stage. No single fictional parcel can demonstrate every branch without contradiction:
a redeemed file does not also become an unredeemed deed file. Harbour Road opens the method, and a
small portfolio of clearly named composite cases tests it under different facts.

::: {.rail label="Why it matters"}
The researcher in this book organizes those questions. The fictional bidder still owns the
decision.
:::

That division matters because tax-sale material attracts the language of hidden gems, bargains and
opportunities. That language turns a municipal collection process into a treasure hunt and pushes
the people outside the auction room out of the story. A clearer account gives everyone their proper
place:

- The municipality is collecting taxes under a legal process.
- Owners and interest holders have rights that do not disappear merely because an advertisement is
  interesting.
- A bidder faces uncertainty and staged responsibilities.
- A researcher can improve the evidence without pretending to remove uncertainty.

The book therefore begins by giving the Inverness packet full credit. It is not a thin notice
waiting for someone else to make it useful; it is a strong starting source. The value of the next
layer comes from restraint: reconcile the records, add lawful public research, mark the limits,
and route the unanswered questions to the right authority.

Before Harbour Road can be called, the chapter travels backward: why the municipality is selling
land at all, what happened before the advertisement, and why the treasurer's file begins with a
debt attached to the property rather than with a description written to market it.

## The first clock {#sec-01-first-clock}

The municipality is not choosing Harbour Road because it thinks the parcel will appeal to buyers.
The process begins somewhere else: real-property taxes remain unpaid, and the debt attaches to the
property with a legal priority of its own.

::: {.rail label="The idea"}
That attachment is the first key term. A **tax lien** is the municipality's legal claim against
the property for the unpaid tax. Under Nova Scotia's municipal statutes, it ranks ahead of other
claims, liens and encumbrances, and it does not have to be registered to hold that priority.
:::

This is why the treasurer is in the auction room. The municipality is enforcing a charge created
by law. It is not acting as an owner who has decided to list a property.

The lien does not send Harbour Road straight to an auction. It starts a clock.

### When proceedings can begin

Outside Halifax, the Municipal Government Act sets the window:

- **Earliest start.** It permits proceedings for taxes left unpaid from the immediately preceding
  taxation year, but not before June 30 of the following year.
- **Backstop.** Subject to the Act's exceptions and a possible council deferral, property must be
  put up for sale once taxes are unpaid for the three preceding fiscal years.

A municipality may operate within that window using a shorter trigger.

::: {.rail label="In practice"}
Inverness and Chester, for example, publicly describe accounts as eligible after two years.
:::

These statements are not competing versions of the same deadline. One is the provincial legal
frame. The other is a municipality describing how it operates inside that frame. If Harbour Road
were in a different municipality, the right sources would be that municipality's current process
and current notice, not Inverness County's practice.

### Notices to the people with a stake

::: {.rail label="Why it matters"}
The people come into the clock before any bidder does.
:::

Before a sale, the municipality sends a preliminary notice allowing at least 14 days to pay. After
that period, it conducts a title search and may order a survey. The search is not a decorative
formality. It helps identify the owner and the people whose mortgages, liens or charges give them
an interest in what is about to happen. Those parties then receive a further notice allowing 60
days to pay before the sale.

That further document is the second key term. The **notice of intent** is a formal warning that the
municipality intends to sell the property if the amounts are not paid.

At this point the bidder has still not entered the story; the owner and interest holders have. The
title search, the possible survey and the notices explain why the treasurer's file is organized
around legal identity and process rather than curb appeal.

### Advertisement

Only after that does the sale become broadly visible. A sale or tender must receive at least 30
consecutive days of qualifying public notice, either through newspaper publication that meets the
statute or by posting on the municipal website. The advertisement may identify the property briefly
and direct the reader to the treasurer for the full description.

shows where they sit in the whole process: the municipal collection clock runs up to auction day,
and a second clock, the purchaser's and the redemption clock, starts after it.

## Notice, not marketing {#sec-01-notice}

Suppose a municipal website posts this fictional line: "Harbour Road, parcel identifier ending
four-two-one, taxes and expenses owing." The line is not trying to persuade anyone that Harbour
Road is desirable.

Its job is to identify the property in a collection process and to tell the public where to seek
the fuller record. It is notice, not marketing.

The same distinction carries forward to maps. A marker or parcel outline derived from an official
notice helps you find the event and the apparent parcel. It does not turn the municipality into the
property's seller in the ordinary real estate sense, and it does not turn the notice into a
condition report.

### Auction or tender

The process can end in more than one kind of event. The statutory default is a public auction. With
council's consent, the treasurer may call for tenders instead, and council may define an acceptable
minimum where the property may not recover all taxes, interest and expenses.

::: {.rail label="In practice"}
Dated municipal examples show the variation. Inverness and Richmond have used open auctions; Pictou
has used a sealed tender; Annapolis has moved unsold auction properties into tender treatment; and
Chester says it will not hold a 2026 sale.
:::

The common legal frame does not create one province-wide event calendar or one set of bidding
instructions. It tells you why a sale can occur. The current municipal notice tells you whether
this sale is occurring, in what form, and under which local instructions.

## Halifax: a similar sequence under a different statute {#sec-01-halifax}

Halifax requires one more piece of discipline. It has a parallel tax-sale framework in the Halifax
Regional Municipality Charter, rather than in the tax-sale division of the Municipal Government
Act. The broad sequence is recognizably similar, but the correct authority is different.

Halifax's administrative rules also make the hidden work in a file unusually visible, because they
list possible sale-expense categories:

- notice;
- title search;
- survey;
- certificate and deed preparation;
- registration;
- closing;
- redemption administration.

Those categories help show what a process can involve. They are not a price list for Inverness,
Pictou or any other municipality.

## What a tax sale is {#sec-01-tax-sale}

The whole visible process now has a name. A **tax sale** is the statutory collection process
through which the municipality offers the property after the required steps. It is not an ordinary
listing with an unusually low asking price. Its public face arrives only after a lien, elapsed time,
record work, and notices involving people who may never appear in the auction room.

Back in the fictionalized Port Hood room, the treasurer's list is no longer a menu of parcels that
happened to become available. Each row is the far end of a municipal file and a legal clock. When
the first parcel is called, the municipality is there to collect under that process. It is not
there to tell a bidder what Harbour Road is worth, whether it suits a plan, or what uncertainty
remains after the hammer falls.

::: source
Municipal Government Act, ss. 133–142 (evidence notes LAW-001 to LAW-005), as for the sections
above.
:::
