# Reading the Notice

::: {.chain links="notice,parcel"}
Research chain: **notice** → **parcel** → context → unknowns → handoff. This chapter reads the
notice and begins the parcel question.
:::

::: {.objectives}
1. Identify the fields of a sale-schedule row and the record system each one belongs to.
2. Explain the different jobs of the AAN and the PID, and why an address is related to the PID but
   not interchangeable with it.
3. Explain why the recovery amount is a collection figure rather than an appraisal, and what a
   redemption marker does and does not tell you.
4. Keep a versioned research file with source-state labels, and treat a withdrawal as narrow
   evidence.
:::

- Assessment Account Number (AAN)
- Parcel Identification Number (PID)
- assessment
- recovery amount

## Six fields, six different jobs {#sec-02-six-fields}

The Inverness packet gives each property a compact row before it gives the reader a detailed
sheet. The row looks like a summary of one thing. It is actually a bundle of references to several
record systems.

Harbour Road, the fictional composite parcel from Chapter 1, shows how. Its row might contain:

- a lien number;
- an assessed-owner field;
- an assessment account number;
- a parcel identification number;
- a location;
- separate land and building entries.

The page may also show a recovery amount and whether a redemption period applies. A reader can
take in the row in a second and feel that the property has been described.

The row has done a different job: it has supplied the handles needed to begin research.

### The lien number and the assessed-owner field {#sec-02-lien-owner}

Start with the **lien number**, the municipality's label for the item in this sale. It helps the
treasurer call the right entry, helps a bidder follow an amended list, and helps everyone refer to
the same row.

The lien number is local to the event. Lien 6 in an August packet is not a permanent identity for
the land, and a lien number from Inverness tells you nothing about how Halifax or Pictou labels its
own material.

The **assessed-owner** field belongs to the tax account. Municipal notices may publish a name
because the collection process has notice requirements and the account must be identifiable.

::: {.rail label="Careful"}
For this book's method, that name is neither a character sketch nor an invitation to search for a
private story.
:::

The name can help a qualified researcher reconcile the municipal account with authorized land
records. It does not prove:

- who occupies the property today;
- who has a beneficial interest;
- whether an estate or relationship complicates title;
- what a person intends to do about the arrears.

Harbour Road therefore remains owner-free. Removing the name does not weaken the lesson, because
the lesson concerns the jobs performed by records.

### Two catalogue keys: AAN and PID {#sec-02-aan-pid}

The first technical identifier is the **Assessment Account Number**, usually shortened to
**AAN**. It identifies the assessment account used in the property assessment and municipal
taxation system. An account can carry assessed value, classification and other taxation
information. The AAN is the right handle when the question concerns the assessment record.

An AAN is not the land itself. It does not establish the legal boundary, prove road access, settle
ownership quality or approve a use. If the AAN is treated as a universal property number, research
can stay internally consistent while following the wrong object.

The second identifier is the **Parcel Identification Number**, or **PID**. It identifies a mapped
land parcel in Nova Scotia's land-information systems. The PID is the stronger starting handle when
the question is "Which parcel do these records appear to concern?" It can route a researcher toward
parcel mapping and registry information.

The word *parcel* deserves restraint. A parcel is a unit in the land-record system. A shape drawn
for that PID is useful for orientation and record lookup. The shape is not automatically a survey
opinion about the location of every boundary on the ground. The PID also does not certify access,
permitted use, physical condition, or freedom from competing interests.

::: {.rail label="A way to picture it"}
AAN and PID can be remembered as two catalogue keys. One leads toward the assessment account; the
other leads toward the mapped parcel and related land records. The comparison ends there. Land
records do more than describe an object sitting on a shelf, and legal interests cannot be reduced
to a library entry. The value of the analogy is simply that choosing the wrong key opens the wrong
catalogue.
:::

### Address, assessment and Property Online {#sec-02-address-assessment}

The civic-address or location field performs another job: it helps a person orient to a place.
Sometimes the notice supplies a street address. Sometimes it supplies a community, road name or
general location.

An address can point toward a building or civic point, while a PID identifies the mapped land
parcel. Those are related facts, but they are not interchangeable:

- A building may sit near a parcel edge.
- A civic point may have been mapped for emergency-response and location purposes.
- A rural parcel may lack the kind of street address familiar from an ordinary house listing.
- More than one parcel may be involved in one sale entry, or one apparent property may cross
  record boundaries that are invisible from the road.

The address helps find the area; the PID keeps the parcel question anchored.

The land and building fields come from assessment. That gives the third core term: an
**assessment** is the valuation and classification record used to support property taxation.
Separate land and building amounts can tell a researcher how the assessment system has
represented the account. A zero or blank building entry can be important enough to investigate.

It still cannot carry the conclusion "vacant land." A structure may be omitted, changed, removed,
damaged, newly built, or associated differently in the records. The date and purpose of the
assessment matter. The field does not replace:

- an inspection;
- an appraisal prepared for a current transaction;
- a survey;
- registry research;
- planning confirmation.

It also does not establish an auction value or a safe bid.

The assessed amount can feel more authoritative than an aerial image because it arrives as a
precise number. Precision is not the same as scope. The number is precise within an assessment
system whose job is taxation. Condition, access, title quality, market demand, repair cost and the
uncertainty of a tax-sale process live outside that job.

**Property Online** connects several of these handles. Nova Scotia's subscription system contains
ownership, parcel mapping and registry information and supports searches using PID, owner, AAN and
civic address. That makes it a powerful professional research environment. It also explains why
the packet can include land-record material without turning the public packet into the complete
registry file.

::: careful
This book keeps Property Online behind a firm boundary. Subscription screens, plans, registry
documents, names and extracted private research do not become public illustrations merely because
the author has lawful account access. The public lesson can still explain which identifier would be
used, which kind of record may answer the question, and when a lawyer or surveyor should interpret
the result.
:::

::: source
Nova Scotia, Sign in to Property Online and Property Online User Guide (source no. 15):

### When the packet disagrees with itself {#sec-02-disagrees}

Now compare the summary schedule with the detailed portion of the real August 2026 packet:

- The schedule contains 45 lien entries.
- The detailed portion contains 44 property sheets.
- Lien 6 appears in the summary without a corresponding detailed sheet in the downloaded packet.
- Two entries also carry amounts that differ between the summary and their detail pages.

The correct response begins with identification. Preserve both versions. Name the lien number,
AAN, PID, page and retrieval date available in each source. Do not merge the rows by eye, and do
not discard the awkward version. The municipality owns the live sale process and can answer which
information controls.

## The fields that pull hardest {#sec-02-fields-pull}

Two fields tend to take over a tax-sale notice even when they occupy very little space. One is the
advertised amount. The other is the redemption marker.

### The recovery amount {#sec-02-recovery}

The advertised amount looks like a price. It arrives beside a property identifier, often in a
column of currency, and it may be far below the numbers a reader associates with ordinary real
estate. Typography does most of the persuasion: a parcel, a dollar figure and a sale date appear on
one line, so the mind supplies the familiar idea of an asking price.

The municipal process gives the number a narrower job. The amount is built around the taxes,
interest and sale expenses the municipality seeks to recover through the process. It was not
produced by inspecting the property and asking what an informed buyer would pay for it. It does not
absorb:

- the condition of a building;
- the quality of title;
- the existence of legal access;
- the cost of repairs;
- the likelihood of insurance;
- the restrictions on a future use.

That is why this book calls it the **recovery amount** when the municipal packet does. The label
identifies the collection purpose. A different municipality may use "minimum bid" or another
event-specific term, so its current notice still controls. Neither label converts the number into
an appraisal.

::: {.case name="Harbour Road"}
Composite case — not a real property.

Imagine a recovery amount of $4,000 for Harbour Road. That number supports the statement "The
municipality published $4,000 for this dated event under its stated terms." It does not support
"Harbour Road is worth more than $4,000." It also does not support the opposite conclusion.

Without title, access, planning, condition, occupancy, tax, repair and uncertainty work, a reader
cannot derive value merely by objecting to the small municipal number.
:::

Assessment cannot repair that gap. The assessed land and building figures come from a taxation
system. The recovery amount comes from a collection process. Putting the two numbers beside each
other produces a comparison between two administrative records. It does not produce a current
appraisal or a safe maximum bid.

The real Inverness packet demonstrates why even the administrative number must remain attached to
its source. Two recovery amounts differ between the summary schedule and the corresponding detailed
pages. A careful file preserves both amounts and asks the municipality which one governs.

::: {.rail label="Careful"}
Choosing the lower amount would be wishful arithmetic. Choosing the higher one simply because it
feels safer would still be an unsupported correction.
:::

::: source
Evidence note DATA-001: the minimum bid is built around taxes, interest and sale expenses, not a
property appraisal (Municipal Government Act, ss. 141, 143, 148). DATA-002 for the two differing
Inverness amounts (August 11, 2026 property packet, source no. 36). Assessment as a dated
taxation record: LAND-003 (Property Valuation Services Corporation, no. 17).
:::

### The redemption marker {#sec-02-redemption-marker}

The redemption marker pulls in a different direction. A word such as "redeemable" can sound like a
feature of the parcel. It is a feature of the legal path after sale.

Nova Scotia's municipal statutes normally provide a six-month redemption period. An owner or
another person with a qualifying interest may redeem within that period. The statutes also remove
that right when the taxes were already more than six years in arrears at the time of sale.
Municipal material may therefore distinguish a redeemable entry from one that can move toward an
immediate deed.

At the notice-reading stage, the marker tells the researcher which post-sale branch to prepare for.
It does not yet explain the purchaser's certificate, protective duties, repayment accounting, deed
request, possession or title quality. Those consequences need room of their own (Chapter 3). The
useful reading is smaller:

- this row points toward a six-month statutory branch;
- that row points toward a branch without that redemption right.

The August summary makes the difference visible across the event: 27 entries are marked redeemable
and 18 are marked non-redeemable. Those counts describe the published packet on its date. They do
not promise that all 45 entries will reach the room or remain in the same category without a
municipal update.

::: source
Municipal Government Act, s. 152(1) and s. 155; Halifax Regional Municipality Charter, s. 167(1)
and s. 170: the six-month redemption right and the older-arrears exception (evidence note LAW-009;
law checked July 19, 2026). DATA-002 for the 27 redeemable and 18 non-redeemable entries in the
Inverness August 11, 2026 summary (source no. 36). Figure 1.3 in Chapter 1 shows the
older-arrears route as a dashed branch.
:::

### A list that can shrink {#sec-02-list-shrink}

A tax-sale list is fluid because the collection process is still active:

- A listed parcel may be paid, redeemed, removed or deferred before or around the event.
- A municipality may publish a revision.
- A sale may contain fewer rows than an earlier advertisement.
- A person who researched a parcel carefully can arrive to find that the lien will not be called.

That outcome is ordinary process evidence. The municipality's job is to collect under the statute,
not to preserve an opportunity for a bidder. Payment or lawful removal means the public list did
what a collection notice was meant to help accomplish. The hours spent researching the row do not
create a claim on the auction.

This is where the living map first enters the book's method. The checked NS Marks The Spot build
organizes current notices for the July 2026 Cape Breton Regional Municipality event and the August
2026 Inverness event. It can filter by municipality, event and redemption category, and it can open
a direct official source link.

::: {.rail label="Why it matters"}
The map is useful because it shortens the route back to authority. It is a dated catalogue of
public records, not the authority that decides whether a lien remains for sale.
:::

If the app, a saved PDF and the municipal page disagree, the discrepancy belongs in the file, and
the current municipality must answer the event-status question.

::: {.rail label="In practice"}
Suppose a researcher saved the Inverness packet when it contained 45 summary rows. On auction
morning, the map shows fewer current entries. The researcher also has a screenshot made several
days earlier. None of those secondary copies can settle alone which count goes onto the bidder's
working list.
:::

The steps are:

1. Open the map's direct municipal source.
2. Check the current municipal notice or update.
3. Ask the treasurer when the published sources still conflict.
4. Record the retrieval time.

The earlier packet remains valuable evidence of what was published earlier. It does not become
false merely because the live process changed.

The same discipline applies to a redemption filter. A filter can help separate rows that the
checked data labels redeemable or non-redeemable. It cannot reclassify a parcel or interpret the
statute for a live decision. The source row, the current notice and qualified legal advice remain
the route when the classification matters.

::: {.recall}
Tax-sale research rarely happens in one quiet sitting. A person may return after several days with
a printed packet, a browser bookmark and notes that made sense when they were written. If those
notes cannot answer four plain questions, the file is still too dependent on memory.

1. Which number identifies the mapped parcel? **The PID.**
2. Which number identifies the assessment account? **The AAN.**
3. Which source says whether the lien remains in the current event? **The municipality's current
   notice, or direct confirmation from the authority running the sale.**
4. What does a non-redeemable marker establish? **It identifies the branch without the normal
   six-month statutory redemption right because of the age of the arrears.** It does not establish
   possession, insurability, condition, clean title or permission to use the land.

The answers are deliberately short. The file should carry the evidence and dates that support them,
but the reader should not need to reconstruct the entire packet to recover the basic source
hierarchy after an interruption.
:::

### Dating the file {#sec-02-dating}

The four-question test also exposes a common versioning error. A researcher may label a file
"Inverness tax sale" and then mix the original packet, a later municipal update, a map snapshot and
auction-day notes without dates. Every source can be authentic while the combined file becomes
misleading. The reader no longer knows which rows existed at which point in the process.

Harbour Road's file can avoid that problem with **source-state labels**:

- "Packet retrieved July 20" describes one snapshot.
- "Municipal page checked August 10" describes a later state.
- "Treasurer confirmed withdrawn August 11" would describe an event-day fact, if that confirmation
  actually occurred.

The dates preserve change instead of forcing one document to erase another.

![**A dated research file.** Harbour Road's file card records two dates and keeps a source beside every amount and marker; source-state labels keep each snapshot separate. Harbour Road is a composite — not a real property. The third label is conditional: it belongs in the file only if the confirmation actually occurred. Not to scale. The file card records two dates, when the packet was retrieved and when event status was last checked, and a source page and date beside every amount and marker."}

Withdrawal itself should remain narrow evidence. It can mean the amount was paid or the row was
otherwise removed or deferred under the process. Without an authoritative explanation, it does not
prove that the property was defective, that the owner prevailed in a dispute, or that the
municipality made an error. The public record may support only the status word and date.

The map's historical-result mode belongs on the other side of the same boundary. A verified result
can document that a parcel was recorded with a particular outcome at an earlier event. It cannot
tell the researcher whether a current lien remains active. Current notices and historical outcomes
are different record families, even when the same PID appears in both.

Harbour Road's index card now needs two dates: one records when the packet was retrieved; the other
records the last event-status check. The card also needs a source beside every amount and marker.
"Recovery amount" without a page and date is a number detached from its authority. "Redeemable"
without the current notice is a label detached from its branch.

The packet can now be read without letting its strongest visual signals make the decision:

| Field | Its job |
|---|---|
| AAN and PID | Route identity questions |
| Address | Supplies orientation |
| Assessment | Supports taxation |
| Recovery amount | Describes the municipality's dated collection figure |
| Redemption marker | Points toward a post-sale legal path |
| The live municipal source | Determines whether the row is still in play |

That final distinction changes the emotional meaning of a withdrawn parcel. The row did not escape from a
shopping list. A collection process changed before the event, and the research file should show
when and how the public record changed.

::: {.summary}
- A sale-schedule row is a bundle of references to several record systems: lien number,
  assessed-owner field, AAN, PID, location, and land and building entries, sometimes with a
  recovery amount and a redemption marker.
- The lien number is local to the event. The assessed-owner field belongs to the tax account and
  does not prove who occupies the property, who has a beneficial interest or what anyone intends
  to do about the arrears.
- The AAN identifies the assessment account and does not establish the legal boundary, road
  access, ownership quality or an approved use. The PID identifies a mapped land parcel and is the
  stronger starting handle for "Which parcel?"; its shape is not automatically a survey opinion,
  and it does not certify access, permitted use, physical condition, or freedom from competing
  interests.
- An address helps find the area; the PID keeps the parcel question anchored. They are related but
  not interchangeable.
- An assessment is the valuation and classification record used to support property taxation; it
  cannot carry the conclusion "vacant land" and does not establish an auction value or a safe bid.
- The recovery amount is built around the taxes, interest and sale expenses the municipality seeks
  to recover; it is not an appraisal, and when two versions differ the file keeps both and asks
  the municipality which one governs.
- A redemption marker is a feature of the legal path after sale, not of the parcel: normally a
  six-month redemption period, removed when the taxes were already more than six years in arrears
  at the time of sale.
- Lists shrink. The municipality's current notice or direct confirmation decides whether a lien
  remains in the event; the file records retrieval dates and treats a withdrawal as narrow evidence.
:::
