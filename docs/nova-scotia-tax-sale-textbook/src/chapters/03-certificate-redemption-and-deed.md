## The document between a bid and a deed {#sec-03-certificate}

Harbour Road, the fictional composite parcel from the earlier chapters, has reached the auction room
again. This time the fictional bidder raises a card, becomes the leading bidder, and completes the
payment required by the process. The public scene feels finished. The legal position has just become
more complicated.

### The middle state

For a redeemable property, full payment produces a **certificate of sale**. The municipal treasurer
prepares and registers that certificate. The purchaser is now the **certificate holder**.

The document matters because a leading bid and a completed paid sale are different stages:

- A raised card expresses a bid.
- Payment completes the purchaser's financial obligation under the sale terms.
- The registered certificate records the statutory position created by that completed sale.

None of those stages is the tax deed.

The redemption marker on the notice ([Chapter 2](#sec-02-redemption-marker)) now opens into a mechanism. For most tax-sale
properties, a qualifying owner or interest holder has six months after the sale to redeem. The
older-arrears category follows the immediate-deed branch instead, because the normal statutory
redemption right does not apply when the taxes were already more than six years in arrears at sale.

Harbour Road is in the six-month category. The certificate holder has paid, but the owner and other
qualifying interest holders still have a live statutory route back to the property. The certificate
holder therefore occupies a middle state: more responsibility than a prospective bidder, less
finality than a deeded owner. [Figure 3.1](#fig-03-redeemable-route) shows the whole redeemable
route.

### Powers and duties

The statutes give that middle state practical content. Each power or duty arrives with a boundary.

| The statutes say the certificate holder… | The boundary |
|---|---|
| may protect the land | Protection covers work needed to keep an insurable building from deteriorating. It does not create a general licence to renovate for a preferred future use. |
| may use the land without diminishing its value | Non-diminishing use does not erase planning law, tenancy, occupancy, safety, or the continuing redemption right. |
| may collect rent | Rent collection does not answer who occupies the premises or how lawful communication and accounting must occur in a real case. |
| must avoid injuring the property, and must not cut trees | — |
| must insure buildings that are insurable | See the panel below. |

::: careful
The insurance duty contains a further uncertainty. A statute can require the certificate holder to
insure an insurable building. It cannot force an insurer to offer a policy for every vacant,
damaged, occupied, remote, or otherwise non-standard risk. The word "insurable" is doing real work.
A bidder who cannot obtain a workable answer before sale may inherit a statutory responsibility
without a practical insurance product ready to satisfy it.
:::

::: {.case name="Harbour Road"}
Composite case — not a real property.

Consider a modest fictional building on Harbour Road. A storm damages part of the roof during the
second month. Water is entering near an exterior wall.

The certificate holder cannot treat the redemption period as an excuse to watch the building decay.
The protective question is urgent:

- What work is necessary to prevent further damage?
- Who should authorize it?
- Which insurer must be notified?
- What records will later prove the nature and cost of the work?

The same facts do not support gutting the interior, removing healthy trees for a view, changing the
use, or treating the premises as vacant without evidence.
:::

### An accounting habit from the first day

The certificate holder also needs an accounting habit from the first day. New tax bills, insurance
premiums, necessary protective work, rent, and other income matter if redemption occurs. During
the certificate-holder months, that ledger becomes the working record (Chapter 11 returns to it).
Money and authorization cannot be reconstructed reliably after several months from bank statements
and memory.

::: {.rail label="Why it matters"}
People outside the auction room remain part of this clock.
:::

- The owner may be trying to arrange funds.
- A lender or another interest holder may be assessing its position.
- An occupant may experience contact from someone who has paid at a tax sale but does not yet hold
  a deed.
- A contractor may see a leaking roof without knowing who can authorize which work.

### Four stage questions

The certificate holder therefore needs answers to four stage questions before taking action.

::: {.recall}
1. What document do I hold?
   **For the Harbour Road roof:** the registered certificate.
2. Which power or duty supports the proposed action?
   **For the roof:** the proposed action is protective rather than developmental.
3. Which continuing right or legal boundary limits it?
   **For the roof:** the redemption right and any occupancy or tenancy obligations remain.
4. What record will be needed if the property is redeemed or proceeds to deed?
   **For the roof:** the file needs insurer communication, professional advice where required,
   photographs obtained lawfully, invoices, proof of payment, and the municipality's approval where
   the statutory accounting calls for it.
:::

The four questions prevent the holder from using the broad word "owner" to skip over the document
and limits that govern the current moment.

::: {.rail label="A way to picture it"}
Staged responsibility can be pictured as a relay baton. The bid,
payment, certificate, possible discharge and possible deed are documented handoffs, and each stage
carries a different permitted job. The limit: the same purchaser may hold every stage, and legal
rights are not literally passed between runners. The image helps only with sequence: the holder must
know which document is currently in hand.
:::

### Month four: redemption is not the holder's choice

::: {.case name="Harbour Road"}
Composite case — not a real property.

Move the story to month four. Harbour Road's building has been insured and protected. Necessary work
has been documented. The owner produces the funds required to redeem.
:::

The certificate holder does not get to choose whether redemption is convenient. The six-month branch
is controlled by statute. For Harbour Road, the file that looked like the beginning of an ownership
project is about to become an accounting and discharge file.

## Two endings, two documents {#sec-03-endings}

### Redemption and the evidence ledger

Redemption is not completed by reimbursing whatever the certificate holder chooses to submit. The
statutes define the categories used in the calculation. They include:

- the purchase price;
- interest at 10 percent a year;
- specified costs and offsets connected to the certificate period.

The costs and offsets include later taxes and interest, registration of the certificate of discharge,
fire-insurance premiums, and necessary repairs that receive the required approval. Rent or other
income from the property counts in the other direction. The exact facts, dates,
authorization, and local administration matter in a real redemption.

::: {.rail label="The idea"}
The durable idea is an evidence ledger rather than a formula recited from memory.
:::

- Every claimed dollar needs a source.
- Every repair needs a reason and the required approval.
- Every offset needs a record.

The ledger should answer a treasurer or lawyer without relying on the certificate holder's
recollection of a chaotic month.

Harbour Road's leaking roof shows why. An invoice proves that a contractor was paid. It does not by
itself prove that the work was necessary, that the amount belongs in the statutory redemption
calculation, or that the required approval was obtained. The insurance file, photographs,
professional assessment, municipal communication, invoice, and proof of payment perform different
jobs.

The holder's use of the property needs the same discipline. If rent was collected, the income
cannot disappear from the file because it makes the repayment smaller. If a cost improved the
holder's preferred future project rather than protecting the property, calling it a repair does not
make it a qualifying item. The statute controls the accounting categories; the holder's labels do
not.

### When redemption is paid

When the qualifying person pays the full redemption amount, the purchaser is repaid under the
statutory process and loses rights in the land. The ending is documented through a **discharge** of
the certificate. The certificate that created the middle state no longer supports continued control
of Harbour Road.

That loss of rights is easy to understate when the certificate holder has spent months protecting a
building and imagining a future use. The purchaser receives the repayment required by the
statute. The purchaser does not receive a choice between the repayment and the land.

The owner and interest holders were therefore present in the legal story even when they were absent
from Port Hood. The six-month door's closing through redemption changes the certificate holder's
job from protecting a conditional interest to proving the accounting and releasing that interest.

### The deed branch

Now give Harbour Road a different ending: six months pass without redemption. Or begin with a
property whose taxes were already more than six years in arrears, so the normal redemption right did
not apply. In either case the purchaser can move toward a **tax deed**.

Under the municipal statutes, the tax deed vests fee-simple title free of encumbrances, subject to
the statutes' treatment of continuing easements and rights-of-way. Those words are consequential.
They require legal interpretation against the actual parcel and records. They should not be
shortened into "clean title" during an auction conversation.

Fee simple describes a form of ownership. It does not report the physical condition of the land.

The deed also does not:

- certify a boundary;
- create road access;
- approve development;
- remove an occupant;
- supply insurance;
- guarantee that the title will never be contested.

Immediate deed is therefore a description of timing within the redemption system. It means the
normal six-month redemption period is absent for the older-arrears category. "Immediate" does not
promise that every practical and legal question can be resolved on sale day.

### The document sequence

The document sequence keeps the distinction stable.

| Stage | What it is or does |
|---|---|
| Leading bid | An auction event |
| Completed payment | Leads to the purchaser's statutory position |
| Registered certificate | Governs the redeemable middle period |
| Full redemption | Ends that position through repayment and discharge |
| Expiry without redemption, or the older-arrears exception | Permits the deed route |
| Registered tax deed | Begins a different set of ownership, possession, title, planning, insurance, and physical-property questions |

::: {.case name="Harbour Road and Quarry Lane"}
Composite cases — not real properties.

Consider two fictional parcels called at the same auction. Harbour Road is marked redeemable. Quarry
Lane is in the older-arrears category. Both purchasers pay in full.

Four months later, Harbour Road is redeemed. Its purchaser supplies the records needed for the
statutory accounting, receives repayment through the process, and loses rights in the land. The
certificate is discharged.

Quarry Lane follows the deed branch without the normal six-month redemption right. Its purchaser
requests the deed and registers it. The deed supplies the ownership document for that stage. It
does not answer whether an apparent driveway is a legal right-of-way or whether someone occupies a
building.

Harbour Road leaves this chapter through discharge; the first purchaser's rights have ended. Quarry
Lane leaves through a registered deed; the second purchaser's ownership work has begun.
[Figure 3.3](#fig-03-two-parcels-two-endings) follows both parcels.
:::

A reader who can narrate those two endings has turned the redemption marker from Chapter 2 into a
working mechanism:

- "Redeemable" points toward a certificate period that may end in discharge or deed.
- "Non-redeemable" points toward the deed route without that normal six-month door.

::: {.rail label="A way to picture it"}
The relay-baton image has a second limit. The documents identify the stage, but they do not make the
course safe. A certificate holder can mishandle the property. A deeded owner can discover an access
problem. A discharged purchaser can face a disputed accounting.
:::
