# Building the Parcel File

::: {.chain links="parcel,unknowns,handoff"}
Research chain: notice → **parcel** → context → **unknowns** → **handoff**. This chapter builds the
parcel file, sorts its unknowns and prepares the questions for professionals.
:::

::: {.objectives}
1. Take inventory of what the municipal packet already provides before adding research of your
   own.
2. Keep a source ledger in which each line records a source, a retrieval date and a limitation.
3. Keep the parcel-register snapshot, the source instruments and their legal effect apart, and
   distinguish a legal description from a civic address and a map.
4. Apply the Province's attribution and licence boundary to public NS Aerial and NSPRD views.
5. Sort unknowns into four destinations and explain why one essential no-go governs a file.
:::

::: {.keyterms}
- parcel register
- legal description
- title search
- four destinations: verified, professionally verifiable, priceable uncertainty, no-go
:::

## What the municipality has already done {#sec-04-baseline}

### The inventory

The Inverness packet does more work than a typical auction advertisement. For each detailed entry,
it gathers:

- account and parcel identifiers;
- assessment figures;
- mapped views;
- a legal description.

A researcher who begins by copying those fields into a prettier page has changed the presentation,
not the state of knowledge.

The useful first move is an inventory. It asks three questions:

- Which facts came from the municipal packet?
- Which source did the municipality name?
- Which details describe the auction event, which describe an assessment account, and which
  purport to describe the parcel?

The inventory credits the municipality and prevents later research from being mistaken for
original discovery. [Figure 4.1](#fig-04-beyond-the-packet) separates what the packet supplies from
the research and handoff work that comes after it.

### The source ledger

::: {.case name="Birch Point Road"}
Composite case — not a real property.

Take a fictional composite entry on Birch Point Road. Its packet row supplies a lien number, AAN,
PID, civic location, assessment fields, recovery amount and redemption marker. The detailed page
adds mapped orientation and descriptive material. Each field already has a job. The biography gives
the collection a history.

The first page of its research file, the source ledger, might say:

- The event status and recovery amount came from the municipality's dated notice.
- The assessment fields came through the account information reproduced in that packet.
- The parcel identifier points toward provincial parcel and registry systems.
- The mapped image is orientation.
- The legal description is text that requires land-record interpretation.
:::

The first page in the research file is a source ledger. Each line records a source, a retrieval
date and a limitation.

![**Build a traceable evidence file.** The researcher's product is a traceable evidence file, not a verdict. Illustration from the 2026 edition (figure-09), shown without its slide title band.](../assets/figures/kept/fig-04-evidence-desk.png){#fig-04-evidence-desk alt="Overhead editorial illustration of a desk with a municipal packet, map layers, statute, source log and three folders marked known, unresolved and professional."}

The ledger matters when the records disagree. Inverness's August packet has a summary lien with no
matching detailed sheet, and two recovery amounts differ between the summary and detail portions
(see [Chapter 1](#sec-01-packet)). A clean-looking research report could hide those conflicts by
choosing one number. A parcel biography instead preserves both records, identifies their dates and
locations, and routes the current-event question back to the municipality.

### Begin with the PID, not an owner

The biography begins with the PID because it is the stable parcel-routing handle already present in
the file. It does not begin with an owner's name. The public teaching record can remain owner-free
while still showing the method:

- exact PID;
- source;
- observation;
- limitation;
- unresolved question;
- next authority.

::: {.rail label="A way to picture it"}
Think of the file as the parcel's biography. It remembers where each statement came from, who can
strengthen it, what it would cost to resolve, and where the evidence must stop. The limit of the
picture: a biography is not a verdict.
:::

### Two routes into the map

The living public map can open that biography through either of two bounded routes:

- **By exact PID.** An exact PID can select the parcel returned for that identifier.
- **By civic address.** An authoritative provincial civic-address result can supply a civic point
  and open the parcel polygon that contains it.

Both routes save time at the front of the file. Neither proves ownership, occupancy, legal access, a
survey boundary, or permission to enter. Chapter 5 operates those routes; here they simply deliver
the researcher to the correct source-led questions.

### Property Online and the parcel register

An authorized land-record researcher can use Property Online to connect that PID to provincial
ownership, mapping and registry information. Property Online can be searched by PID, AAN, owner or
address, and it contains ownership, parcel-mapping and registry information. Search can also use an
AAN, civic address or owner, but those routes answer different matching problems.

::: careful
The public book does not reproduce subscriber screenshots, plans, registry documents, owner
information or structured extracts. Subscription access is a way to conduct authorized research,
not a licence to republish the underlying material.
:::

In a lawyer's title review, the **parcel register** is the parcel-specific registry record within
that governed land-record material. It can move the file beyond the assessed-owner label on an
auction notice and identify documents or registered interests that require interpretation.

The register is not a self-executing conclusion. A document number, interest entry or name in a
register does not tell an untrained reader:

- how the interest operates;
- whether another instrument changes it;
- what the tax-sale statutes do to it.

The register creates a more precise set of questions. It does not turn the researcher into the
lawyer who answers them.

Three records should stay separate in the file:

| Record | What it supplies or needs |
|---|---|
| The search or parcel-register view | A dated snapshot |
| The source instrument, release, agreement or order | The underlying terms |
| The legal effect of that material, including what the tax-deed provisions do or do not change | Requires the governing law and property-specific interpretation |

The appearance of a mortgage, judgment, easement or other interest proves neither its present amount
nor its release, priority or treatment after sale.

::: {.rail label="In practice"}
Record the retrieval date and refresh the land-record search near the decision point. If a recent
registration, pending document, court order or conflicting entry could matter, ask the
land-registration office or reviewing lawyer what the current search does and does not capture. A
fresh result can change the question without answering it by itself.
:::

### The legal description and the screening exhibit

The same discipline applies to the **legal description**. A civic address helps people find a place.
A legal description identifies land through the language used in the conveyancing record. It may
refer to bearings, distances, adjoining lands, plans, lots, roads, shorelines or older descriptions.
Its job is to state what the instrument describes, not to paint an easy picture for a driver.

Take the fictional Birch Point Road again. Its civic location may bring the researcher to the
correct area. Its PID may open a polygon on a public parcel map. An aerial view may show a roof, tree
line, road or clearing. The legal description may refer to boundaries that are neither visible nor
safely reconstructed from those pixels. The three records can support one another without becoming
interchangeable.

That is why a public parcel boundary is a screening exhibit. It can:

- show the shape reported by the current provincial service;
- support orientation;
- help form questions about apparent road or water relationships.

It does not certify where a boundary lies on the ground. A surveyor's evidence and the legal record
answer that problem at a different level.

### Licence and attribution

Public views from NS Aerial and the Nova Scotia Property Records Database (NSPRD) also carry a
licence obligation. A rendered view using the covered Province services must preserve the Province
attribution:

> Contains information obtained under license from the Province of Nova Scotia which is provided without warranty or liability for errors or omissions.

The application must link to the licence. The attribution survives in the image because a
screenshot may later travel outside the app that supplied it.

The licence permits the checked public display. It does not erase the map's substantive limits, and
this book does not treat it as permission to redistribute raw geometry or a tile cache. Rights,
provenance and evidentiary meaning are three different questions. A view can be lawfully displayed
and still be incapable of proving a boundary.

### Title search

The final land-record term is **title search**: an examination of the ownership and interest chain,
rather than a casual lookup of the latest name.

Before a tax sale, the municipality itself performs title work as part of the statutory notice
process. That work helps identify people entitled to notice. A prospective bidder has a different
decision and may need a lawyer to examine the records for that purpose.

| | The municipality | A prospective bidder |
|---|---|---|
| What it is doing | Moving a tax-enforcement process through its required stages | Deciding whether the legal position, uncertainty, project and price belong together |
| Why the records are examined | To help identify people entitled to notice | For the bidder's own decision, possibly through a lawyer |

The municipal title work does not become a purchaser's title opinion merely because both inquiries
involve the same parcel. The questions overlap; the professional duty and requested conclusion do
not.

### Writing bounded observations

Composite case — not a real property.

For Birch Point Road, the source ledger can now carry four distinct entries:

- The municipal notice establishes the dated auction record.
- The parcel register routes registered interests to legal review.
- The legal description supplies the recorded land description.
- Public parcel and aerial services provide attributed screening views.

None is labelled "the truth" while the others become decoration.
:::

The researcher then writes observations in deliberately bounded language:

- The municipal packet associates this exact PID with this dated auction entry.
- The current provincial parcel service returns mapped geometry for the PID.
- The aerial service shows visible features within and near that reported geometry on its captured
  imagery.
- The land record contains a legal description and instrument references that require professional
  interpretation.

The verbs do useful work. "Associates", "returns", "shows" and "contains" report what a source did.
They do not silently become "owns", "bounds", "permits" or "guarantees".

| Verbs that report what a source did | Verbs they must not silently become |
|---|---|
| associates · returns · shows · contains | owns · bounds · permits · guarantees |

Now the legal question can become specific. Instead of asking a lawyer "Is this property okay?", the
file can ask:

- which registered interests affect the parcel;
- how the legal description and referenced plan should be read;
- what the tax deed would do to those interests;
- what cannot be resolved before sale.

A surveyor can receive the mapped-boundary and description question without being asked to
interpret the tax-sale statute. Each professional sees the source material and the uncertainty that
belongs to that profession.

Birch Point Road has acquired a biography, but not a verdict. Its life in the file begins with the
municipal event, passes through the parcel and registry records, and reaches attributed public
screening views. The remaining gaps are now visible enough to classify rather than hide.

## Four places an unknown can go {#sec-04-four-destinations}

The source ledger for the fictional Birch Point Road contains facts and gaps on the same page. That
is a sign of a functioning file. A report that permits only confident sentences will convert its
gaps into assumptions or omit them entirely.

An unknown can go to one of **four destinations**: verified, professionally verifiable, priceable
uncertainty or no-go. [Figure 4.5](#fig-04-four-destinations) sets them out with the question that
leads to each.

### Verified

Start with a verified fact: the municipality's dated notice associates an exact PID with a
particular auction entry. The file has the source, date and captured record. "Verified" describes
the match to that source. It does not make the municipal notice responsible for a survey boundary,
building condition or title conclusion it never offered.

### Professionally verifiable

A second entry might be professionally verifiable. The public parcel service reports mapped geometry
that appears to meet a road corridor, and the legal description refers to a plan. The file cannot
determine whether the apparent relationship supplies the frontage or right-of-way required for the
intended use. Three professionals have a route to the answer:

- A surveyor can investigate the boundary evidence.
- A lawyer can examine the registered interests and legal access.
- A planner can apply current municipal requirements to the proposed project.

The phrase "professionally verifiable" is not a promise that the answer will be favourable. It
identifies a question, an evidence source and a competent route to an answer. The result may remove
the concern, define a manageable condition, or end the project.

### Priceable uncertainty

A third entry may be **priceable uncertainty**. Suppose an insurer confirms that a vacant building
can be covered only under specified terms and at a higher known premium. The uncertainty has
narrowed enough to enter a budget. The same may be true of a quoted survey, a defined legal review
or a known municipal application fee.

Priceable does not mean trivial. A cost can be known and still make the parcel unworkable.
Priceable means the file can attach a documented amount or bounded range to the uncertainty without
inventing a number.

### No-go

The fourth destination is **no-go** uncertainty. The researcher may be unable to confirm a fact that
is essential to the intended project before the sale. An answer may depend on:

- entry the municipality cannot authorize;
- a study that cannot be completed in time;
- evidence that remains contradictory;
- a legal position too consequential to carry unresolved.

The correct label can be "do not proceed under these conditions."

That label is not a prediction that the land is bad. It describes the fit between the available
evidence, the deadline, the researcher's tolerance and the proposed use. Another person with
different expertise, authority, time or risk capacity may reach a different process decision. This
book does not turn either decision into a parcel recommendation.

::: {.rail label="In practice"}
Assessment illustrates the classification problem. The assessed value and classification are
verified account information when matched to the proper source and date. Their implications for
market value, condition, legal access or a maximum bid are not verified by that match. The
assessment can route a question; it cannot fill the empty spaces in the biography.
:::

   that source never offered.
2. Does the next gap belong to a named professional or source? **Professionally verifiable.**

4. Can an essential gap be resolved or carried? If not, it is **no-go**: stop the project before
   attachment rewrites the evidence.
:::

### The handoff

Birch Point Road's apparent road relationship demonstrates the handoff. The researcher does not send
a lawyer a screenshot with "Looks accessible?" in the subject line. Instead, the file supplies:

- the exact PID;
- the dated municipal record;
- relevant instrument references;
- the legal description;
- the captured public view, with attribution;
- the intended use.

The question asks which registered rights provide legal access and what interests or documents must
be reviewed.

The surveyor receives a different question from the same file: what evidence is needed to locate the
relevant boundary or plan relationship, and can that work be completed before the decision deadline?
The planner receives the proposed use, the current municipal source and the parcel identifiers, then
identifies which planning, lot, frontage, servicing or permit questions require confirmation.

Those roles overlap around the parcel without becoming substitutes:

- A surveyor locating a boundary does not decide what a tax deed does to a registered interest.
- A lawyer interpreting an interest does not certify where a monument lies on the ground.
- A planner explaining current land-use rules does not confirm title or physical condition.

::: {.rail label="Why it matters"}
Professional fees now have a defined purpose in the research file. They buy a particular reduction
in uncertainty. The invoice is not generic closing overhead; it is connected to a question whose
answer can change the decision. That connection also permits triage: a bidder may pay first for the
question most likely to end the project before ordering lower-priority work.
:::

### Court-directed sales

Some legal routes sit outside an ordinary bidder's file. Nova Scotia's municipal statutes allow a
treasurer to seek a court order addressing matters such as arrears, interests conveyed, notice and
the manner of sale. A sale under such an order has the protected effect described by the order and
the statute.

That mechanism belongs to the municipality, court and legal process. It is not a routine cure a
bidder can assume will repair ambiguity in a current listing. If a notice or legal professional
identifies a court-directed process, the actual order becomes an essential source. The file records
what the order says and routes its effect to legal interpretation. The researcher does not compress
"court ordered" into "risk free".

### One essential no-go governs

::: {.case name="Birch Point Road"}
Composite case — not a real property.

By the end of first review, Birch Point Road may occupy all four destinations:

- **Verified:** the exact PID-to-notice match.
- **Priceable:** a survey quote.
- **Professionally verifiable:** the access question, by the lawyer and surveyor.
- **No-go:** an essential site-condition question that cannot be answered lawfully before sale,
  labelled no-go for the proposed project.

The no-go entry governs even if the aerial image is attractive.
:::

Evidence is not a vote in which three comfortable facts outnumber one unresolved essential fact. The
importance of the unknown depends on the project it can defeat.

This is the point of giving the parcel a biography before giving it a future. The file remembers
where each statement came from, who can strengthen it, what it would cost to resolve, and where the
evidence must stop. Emotional attachment arrives later, if it arrives at all.

The living map of Chapter 5 now puts that biography into motion. Its job is not to replace the
ledger or the professionals. It shortens the route from a dated notice to an exact parcel, isolates
one context question, and preserves the unknown and handoff without allowing a vivid map to write
the ending.

- The Inverness packet already gathers account and parcel identifiers, assessment figures, mapped

- Begin the parcel file with the PID, not an owner's name. The exact-PID and civic-address routes

- The municipality's title work helps identify people entitled to notice; it is not a purchaser's
