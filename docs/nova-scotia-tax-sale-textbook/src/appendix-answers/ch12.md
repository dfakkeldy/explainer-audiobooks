## Chapter 12 · After the Deed {#ans-12}

### 12.1 {#ans-12-1}

If the taxes were already unpaid for more than six years when the sale occurred, the purchaser may
request the deed after the sale. For a redeemable property that remains unredeemed, the request
comes after six months. In either route, the purchaser pays the fee set by council and the
municipality delivers the prescribed deed to the purchaser or as the purchaser directs. It is a
request-and-document step, not an automatic page turning on the sixth-month anniversary. The
fictional Foundry Street file confirms that the redemption period has actually expired without
redemption, obtains the municipality's current instructions, pays the applicable fee and checks the
delivered deed: the land description, grantee, signatures, municipal seal, related sale record and
any direction given by the purchaser. See [The document changes, the questions
move](#sec-12-document).

### 12.2 {#ans-12-2}

The Municipal Government Act exempts a deed given through a tax sale from municipal deed transfer
tax, but the exemption does not answer every tax question. The separate provincial non-resident
deed transfer tax and the transaction-specific HST branch remain property- and purchaser-specific,
so a lawyer and tax adviser apply the current rules to the actual transfer. The tax exemption also
does not erase the required registration information: as of July 2026, the provincial deed-transfer
service says an Affidavit of Value is required when real property is transferred, even when no
municipal deed transfer tax is payable. Land Registry fees are due when a document is registered,
recorded or filed, and those amounts can change; the file keeps the current official fee, payment
receipt, submitted documents, registration particulars and lawyer's confirmation. See [The document
changes, the questions move](#sec-12-document).

### 12.3 {#ans-12-3}

The six-month redemption period runs from the sale date, for a redeemable property; if the taxes
were unpaid for more than six years when the sale occurred, there is no six-month period. The window
to apply to the Supreme Court for surplus proceeds also runs from the sale date: after the redemption
period and before 20 years have passed from the sale. The Marketable Titles Act period runs from the
deed-registration date: a tax deed generally may be set aside only during the six years following its
registration, subject to the land-exclusion conditions, the current-owner fraud or breach-of-trust
exception and the preserved damages claim. It does not start on auction day, at the end of
redemption, at deed delivery or on first entry. Six years is not a title-review waiting strategy.
See [Figure 12.1](#fig-12-three-clocks).

### 12.4 {#ans-12-4}

In CBRM's July 2026 description, the zoning letter confirms the zoning and applicable provisions
identified by municipal planning staff, and the clearance letter reports specified outstanding
building-code, by-law or fire-code violations in municipal records. Neither letter is a universal
property approval. A zoning confirmation does not promise that a proposed design will receive
permits. A clearance response does not replace an inspection and cannot reveal a defect that was
never reported or recorded. The owner keeps the response date and qualifications and sends any
difficult answer to the appropriate municipal staff or professional. See [The second due-diligence
wave](#sec-12-second-wave).

### 12.5 {#ans-12-5}

A person claiming an interest in land sold for taxes may apply to the Supreme Court after the
redemption period and before 20 years have passed from the sale, and the court may order
proportional payment from the tax-sale surplus account. That is a route, not a prediction about
Elena's standing, evidence, share or result; her lawyer identifies the correct parties, proof and
court procedure. The surplus account is a proceeds record, not a statement about which interests
continue to burden the land, so Elena does not treat her old interest as a continuing lien on the
land, and the deed holder does not appropriate surplus money that the statute reserves for another
process. A possible proceeds claim does not grant present possession or control of the land. See
[The money and the challenge take different roads](#sec-12-money).
