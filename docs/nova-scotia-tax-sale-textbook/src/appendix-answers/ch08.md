## Chapter 8 · Title and Possession {#ans-08}

### 8.1 {#ans-08-1}

Under Nova Scotia's Municipal Government Act, the tax deed is evidence that the statutory sale
provisions were followed, and the Act says the deed vests the land in the grantee in fee simple,
free and discharged from all encumbrances. Fee simple is the broad form of private ownership estate
ordinarily meant when people speak of owning land. The phrase describes the legal interest conveyed.
It does not describe the building's condition, identify every person inside, certify a development
plan, or turn a graphical parcel boundary into a survey. See [Fee simple](#sec-08-deed).

### 8.2 {#ans-08-2}

The statute itself prevents the summary that the deed erased everything. When the land being sold
is burdened by an easement or right-of-way, the sale does not terminate or affect that continuing
relationship as the Act specifies, so Foundry Street's registered right-of-way may continue. The
deed may discharge interests the statute discharges, but which ones is for counsel to confirm. The
title file needs the exact deed, the current parcel register, the instruments behind relevant
entries, the legal description, and a lawyer's interpretation of how the governing provisions apply
to this parcel; for a continuing right-of-way, also its route, purpose, width, parties, maintenance
language, and any other terms the source instruments actually contain, never reconstructed from a
driveway line in imagery. See [One right-of-way, two pieces of land](#sec-08-deed) and
[Figure 8.2](#fig-08-deed-effect).

### 8.3 {#ans-08-3}

One describes the legal effect the statute gives a qualifying deed. The other refuses to promise
that a municipality's auction page has completed a property-specific title opinion, found every
factual problem, resolved possession, or made the interest readily insurable or marketable for a
particular transaction. Questions such as which statute governs, whether there was a court order and
what it said, and which easements or rights-of-way benefit or burden the land belong with a Nova
Scotia property lawyer reviewing the actual file. See [Why "clear title" is an unsafe
label](#sec-08-deed).

### 8.4 {#ans-08-4}

Vacant possession is the state in which the buyer can lawfully take possession of the property
without another person remaining entitled to occupy it. Whether and how it can be obtained depends
on the actual facts and applicable law; it cannot be inferred from "immediate deed," a paid balance,
or the absence of a tenant's name in the sale advertisement. Nothing in the exterior scene
identifies the occupants. No locks are changed, no utilities are interrupted, no belongings are
moved, and no demand for rent or departure is improvised at the door: those choices could affect
people's rights and safety, destroy evidence about the occupancy arrangement, or make a difficult
legal problem worse. Counsel may need to determine who is present, what documents or agreements
exist, whether residential-tenancy law applies, what notices or proceedings are legally required,
and how access can be obtained. See [The house does not empty on paper](#sec-08-possession).

### 8.5 {#ans-08-5}

The file must first identify whether the sale concerns land, a mobile home, or some combination
described by the controlling documents; Nova Scotia has specific tax-sale regulations and prescribed
forms for mobile homes. Ownership of the home, ownership of the ground, the right to keep the home
on the space, and any registered personal-property security are distinct questions. Security
interests in the home are searched in the Personal Property Registry, which is not the land
registry; if the home sits in a land-lease community, Nova Scotia's Residential Tenancies framework
applies. The bounded file confirms the asset from the current municipal notice, prescribed sale
documents, land records, mobile-home records, and counsel. No PID-only conclusion is supported. See
[When the asset is a manufactured home](#sec-08-manufactured) and
[Figure 8.5](#fig-08-manufactured-home-records).
