## Chapter 6 · Access and Intended Use {#ans-06}

### 6.1 {#ans-06-1}

Getting near the land is a physical observation. Having a right to reach it is a legal conclusion
that must be supported. Having a route capable of serving the project is a further physical and
regulatory question. A visible track answers only the first. See [The access note](#sec-06-route) and
[Figure 6.2](#fig-06-case-a-access).

### 6.2 {#ans-06-2}

The plan does not say by itself whether the line was surveyed as a boundary, drawn as an existing
road, proposed as a route, or tied to a registered interest. The researcher must also ask whether
the current parcel benefits, whether the description is clear enough to locate, and whether later work has changed the
physical route. The plan, related instruments, current survey evidence, and legal interpretation
must be reconciled rather than ranked by which picture looks most convincing. See [Changing the
facts](#sec-06-route).

### 6.3 {#ans-06-3}

Road frontage is the parcel's relationship to a road as defined and required by the applicable
rules. A right to pass over a neighbour's land may solve one legal access problem while leaving a
separate frontage or development-standard problem. The registered passage may permit vehicles to
reach the land, while the development rules may still require the lot itself, or a proposed
alteration to it, to satisfy a particular road relationship. The planning authority must answer the
rule question for the proposed project. See [Road
frontage](#sec-06-use).

### 6.4 {#ans-06-4}

The useful question names the exact PID, the proposed use, the available access evidence, and the
specific confirmation needed before a bid. A planner cannot test a vague hope. The call discloses the
access gap rather than inviting an answer built on an unstated assumption, and its wording avoids
asking the planner to guarantee a project. See [The planning call](#sec-06-use).

### 6.5 {#ans-06-5}

A permitted use removes one possible prohibition. It does not establish that the parcel is a
buildable lot, that a compliant building envelope exists, that the route can serve the dwelling, or
that water and sewage approvals will be available. Encouraging facts do not merge into a permit. If
the file still holds an essential dependency the bidder cannot resolve before the auction (an
ambiguous access instrument, say, or a planning authority that cannot confirm the lot and route can
support the use without later technical work), either is enough to stop. That stop is a
conclusion about this bidder's evidence, deadline, and intended use, not a prediction that the
parcel is worthless. See [A rational no](#sec-06-use) and
[Figure 6.5](#fig-06-independent-gates).
