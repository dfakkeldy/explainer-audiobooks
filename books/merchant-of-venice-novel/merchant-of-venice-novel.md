## 1. A Sad Part

In a street in Venice, Antonio kept company with Salarino and Salanio.

“Honestly, I don't know why I'm so sad. It wears me out. You say it wears on you. But how I caught it, found it, came by it; what it's made of, where it comes from—I still can't say. This sadness makes such a fool of me that I hardly know myself,” Antonio said.

“Your mind is being tossed about at sea, where your great ships sail like noblemen and rich merchants among the waves. They tower over the little trading boats, which bow and dip as your splendid floating processions sweep past on wings of woven sailcloth,” Salarino said.

“Believe me, if I had shipping ventures like yours out at sea, most of my heart would be abroad with everything I hoped to gain. I'd keep plucking grass to test which way the wind was blowing, studying maps for harbours, piers and anchorages. Anything that made me fear for my investments would certainly make me sad,” Salanio said.

“Even blowing on my soup would give me chills: I'd think what a stronger wind could do at sea. I couldn't watch the sand run through an hourglass without seeing sandbanks, and my ship, the Andrew, loaded with valuable cargo, run aground, her mast bowed lower than her hull, kissing her own grave. If I went to church, the stone walls would remind me of rocks that need only touch my delicate ship's side to scatter her spices on the waves and dress the roaring water in my silks. Rich one moment; worth nothing the next. Could I imagine that without imagining how it would grieve me? Don't tell me otherwise. Antonio is sad about his cargoes.”

“Believe me, no. Fortunately, I haven't put all my investments in one ship, or sent them to one place. Nor does everything I own depend on this year's fortunes. My cargoes aren't what makes me sad,” Antonio said.

“Then you're in love,” Salarino said.

“Oh, nonsense!”

“Not in love either? Then let's say you're sad because you aren't happy. You might just as easily laugh and jump about and say you're happy because you aren't sad. By Janus, the god with two faces, nature makes some strange fellows! Some laugh through half-shut eyes like parrots cackling at a bagpipe; others look so sour they wouldn't bare their teeth in a smile even if old Nestor himself—that model of sober wisdom—swore the joke was funny.”

Bassanio approached with Lorenzo and Gratiano. The conversation about Antonio's melancholy gave way to greetings.

“Here comes your noble relative Bassanio, with Gratiano and Lorenzo. Goodbye. We'll leave you with better company,” Salanio said.

“I'd have stayed until I'd cheered you up, if worthier friends hadn't arrived,” Salarino said.

“I value you very dearly. I think your own business calls you, and you're taking the opportunity to go,” Antonio said.

“Good morning, gentlemen.”

“My good friends, when are we going to have a laugh together? When? You're becoming strangers. Must it be that way?” Bassanio asked.

“We'll make our free time fit yours.”

Salarino and Salanio went on their way. Lorenzo and Gratiano remained a little longer with Bassanio and Antonio.

“Bassanio, now you've found Antonio, we'll leave you. Just remember where we're meeting for dinner,” Lorenzo said.

“I'll be there,” Bassanio said.

“You don't look well, Antonio. You take the world too seriously. Those who pay so dearly in worry to win the world end up losing it. Believe me, you've changed enormously,” Gratiano said.

“I take the world for what it is, Gratiano: a stage where everyone must play a part. And mine is a sad one,” Antonio said.

“Then let me play the fool. Let laughter put the wrinkles on my face. I'd rather warm my liver with wine than chill my heart with miserable groaning. Why should a man with warm blood in his veins sit like a marble statue of his grandfather—half asleep when he's awake, making himself ill with sourness? Antonio, I love you, and it's love that makes me say this. There are men whose faces settle like scum on stagnant water. They keep deliberately still so people will think them wise, serious and profound. It's as if they said, 'I am Sir Oracle. When I open my mouth, let no dog bark!' I know men thought wise simply because they say nothing. If they spoke, I'm sure they'd tempt their listeners into the sin of calling a fellow man a fool. More of that another time. But don't use melancholy as bait to catch that foolish fish, a reputation for wisdom. Come, Lorenzo. Goodbye for now. I'll finish my sermon after dinner.”

“We'll leave you until dinner, then. I must be one of those silent wise men. Gratiano never lets me speak,” Lorenzo said.

“Stay with me another two years and you'll forget the sound of your own voice,” Gratiano said.

“Goodbye. After all this, I'll learn to talk,” Antonio said.

“Thank you! Silence is a virtue only in a dried ox tongue and a girl who couldn't be sold as a bride.”

Gratiano left with Lorenzo, taking the promise of another sermon to dinner with him. Bassanio and Antonio were alone.

“Did any of that mean anything?” Antonio asked.

“Gratiano talks an endless amount of nonsense—more than anyone in Venice. His points are two grains of wheat hidden in two great sacks of chaff. You can search all day to find them, and when you do, they aren't worth the trouble,” Bassanio said.

“Now tell me about the lady you swore to make that secret pilgrimage to—the one you promised to tell me about today,” Antonio said.

“You know, Antonio, how badly I've damaged my finances by keeping up a grander style of life than I could afford. I'm not complaining that I have to give it up. What concerns me is getting honourably clear of the enormous debts my extravagance has left me with. I owe you most, in money and in love. Your love lets me speak freely about all my plans for getting clear of the debts I owe.”

“Please do, Bassanio. If your plan is as honourable as you are, my money, my person, everything I can possibly offer is yours to use.”

“At school, when I lost an arrow, I'd shoot another just like it in the same direction and watch more carefully. By risking both, I often found both. I bring up that childhood example because what I'm about to suggest is equally innocent. I owe you a great deal, and like a reckless boy I've lost what you lent me. But if you'd send another arrow after the first, I'd watch so carefully that I'm confident I'd recover both—or at least bring back your second investment and remain your grateful debtor for the first.”

“You know me. You're wasting time approaching my love by such a roundabout path. You hurt me more by doubting whether I'd do everything for you than you would by wasting everything I own. Just tell me what I should do—anything you know I can do—and I'm ready. Speak.”

“There's a lady in Belmont who has inherited a great fortune. She's beautiful, and more than beautiful: she has extraordinary qualities. Sometimes her eyes have sent me lovely messages without a word. Her name is Portia, and she loses nothing in comparison with that famous Roman Portia, Cato's daughter and Brutus's wife. The world knows her worth. The four winds blow distinguished suitors to her from every coast. Her sunny hair hangs at her temples like the Golden Fleece, the legendary treasure Jason sailed to Colchis to win. That makes her home at Belmont the shore of Colchis, and many suitors new Jasons sailing in search of her. Oh, Antonio, if I had the means to compete with one of them, something tells me I'd prosper, that I'd certainly be fortunate,” Bassanio said.

“You know all my wealth is invested at sea. I have neither cash nor goods I can turn into an immediate sum. Go and see what my credit can raise in Venice. It will be stretched to its limit to equip you for Belmont, to beautiful Portia. Go at once and ask where we can raise the money, and so will I. I have no doubt someone will lend on my credit, or out of friendship for me.”

Antonio and Bassanio went to find the money.


## 2. A Living Daughter’s Will

At Belmont, Portia spoke with Nerissa in a room of her house.

“Honestly, Nerissa, this little body of mine is tired of this great world,” Portia said.

“You'd have reason to be, dear lady, if your troubles were as plentiful as your blessings. Still, from what I see, people stuffed with too much are as sick as those starving with nothing. So there's a great deal of happiness in having enough, between those two extremes. Living with too much brings grey hairs sooner; people with enough to live on last longer,” Nerissa said.

“Fine sayings, beautifully delivered.”

“They'd be finer still if people followed them.”

“If doing what was best were as easy as knowing what was best to do, little chapels would be great churches and poor cottages would be princes' palaces. A good preacher follows his own advice. I can teach twenty people how to behave more easily than I can be one of the twenty practising what I preach. Reason may make rules for our desires, but hot passion jumps straight over those cold commands. Wild, reckless youth is a hare leaping over the nets laid by limping good advice. But this reasoning isn't the way to choose me a husband. Oh, that word, 'choose'! I can't choose the man I want or refuse the man I dislike. A living daughter's will is controlled by a dead father's will. Isn't that hard, Nerissa? No power to choose or refuse?”

“Your father was always a good man, and holy men are inspired by God as they approach death. So in the lottery he devised with these three chests—gold, silver and lead—whoever chooses the chest he intended also wins you. Surely only someone who loves you rightly will choose rightly. But how much affection do you feel for any of the princely suitors already here?” Nerissa asked.

“Name them one by one. I'll describe each as you name him, and you can judge my feelings from the description.”

“First, the prince from Naples.”

“A young horse himself! He talks about nothing but his horse and counts it among his greatest accomplishments that he can shoe it himself. I'm very much afraid his lady mother had an affair with a blacksmith.”

“Then the Count Palatine.”

“He does nothing but frown, as if to say, 'If you don't want me, choose as you like.' He hears funny stories without a smile. With so much ill-mannered sadness in his youth, I fear he'll grow into that philosopher who wept over everything. I'd sooner marry a skull with a bone in its mouth than either of those two. God preserve me from them!” Portia said.

“What about the French lord, Monsieur Le Bon?”

“God made him, so we'll allow that he's a man. I know it's a sin to mock, but really! His horse is better than the Neapolitan's; his bad habit of frowning is better developed than the Count's. He's every sort of man and no man of his own. Let a thrush sing and he starts dancing. He'd fence with his own shadow. Marrying him would mean marrying twenty husbands. If he despised me, I'd forgive him, because even if he loved me madly, I could never return it.”

“Then what about Falconbridge, the young English baron?”

“You know I say nothing to him. He doesn't understand me and I don't understand him. He speaks neither Latin, French nor Italian, and you'd testify in court that I know only a penny's worth of English—hardly any at all. He's a handsome picture of a man—but who can have a conversation with a silent performance? And those clothes! I think he bought his jacket in Italy, his knee-length trousers in France, his hat in Germany, and his manners everywhere.”

“What do you think of his neighbour, the Scottish lord?” Nerissa asked.

“Very neighbourly and charitable. He borrowed a slap in the face from the Englishman and swore he'd pay it back when he could. I believe the Frenchman guaranteed the debt and signed up to add another slap of his own.”

“How do you like the young German, the Duke of Saxony's nephew?”

“I can't stand him in the morning, when he's sober, and can stand him even less in the afternoon, when he's drunk. At his best he's a little less than a man; at his worst, scarcely better than a beast. Whatever the worst that can happen may be, I hope I can manage without him.”

“If he entered the contest and chose the right chest, refusing him would mean refusing to obey your father's will.”

“Then, to prevent the worst, put a tall glass of Rhine wine on a wrong chest. Even if the devil's inside, with that temptation outside he'll choose it. I'll do anything, Nerissa, before I marry a drunkard who soaks up wine like a sponge,” Portia said.

“You needn't worry about marrying any of these lords. They've told me their decisions: they're going home and giving up their courtships unless they can win you by some means other than your father's test with the chests.”

“If I live as long as the ancient prophetess Sibyl, I'll die as chaste as Diana, the virgin goddess, unless someone wins me according to my father's will. I'm glad this batch of suitors is so reasonable. There's not one of them whose absence I don't adore. God give them a pleasant journey away.”

“Do you remember, while your father was alive, a Venetian who came here with the Marquis of Montferrat? A scholar and a soldier?”

“Yes, yes. Bassanio. I think that was his name.”

“That's right, madam. Of all the men these foolish eyes have ever seen, he most deserved a beautiful lady,” Nerissa said.

“I remember him well. And I remember that he deserved your praise.”

A servant entered before the conversation could go further.

“Well? What news?” Portia asked.

“The four foreign gentlemen are looking for you to say goodbye, madam. And an advance messenger from a fifth, the Prince of Morocco, says his master will arrive tonight,” the servant said.

“If I could welcome the fifth as wholeheartedly as I can say goodbye to the other four, I'd be glad he's coming. If he has a saint's character and a devil's dark complexion, I'd rather have him hear my confession than marry me. Come, Nerissa. Lead the way, fellow. While we shut the gates on one suitor, another knocks at the door.”

The servant led the way. Portia and Nerissa went to bid farewell to the departing suitors.


## 3. A Pound of Flesh

Bassanio and Shylock met in a public place in Venice.

“Three thousand ducats. Well,” Shylock said.

“Yes, sir. For three months,” Bassanio said.

“For three months. Well.”

“And, as I told you, Antonio will guarantee the debt.”

“Antonio will sign the bond—the written promise to repay. Well.”

“Can you help me? Will you oblige me? May I have your answer?” Bassanio asked.

“Three thousand ducats, for three months, with Antonio bound to repay them.”

“And your answer?”

“Antonio is a good man.”

“Have you heard anyone suggest otherwise?”

“Oh, no, no, no. By 'a good man' I mean he's good for the money. Still, his wealth depends on money he expects to receive. He has a ship bound for Tripoli and another for the Indies. I hear at the Rialto, where the merchants meet, that he has a third ship at Mexico, a fourth sailing for England, and other ventures scattered abroad. But ships are only planks, and sailors only men. There are land rats and water rats; thieves on land and thieves at sea—I mean pirates. And then the dangers of water, wind and rocks. Nevertheless, he's good for it. Three thousand ducats. I think I could accept his bond,” Shylock said.

“You can be sure of that.”

“I intend to be sure—sure I can accept his bond. And to make sure, I'll think it over. May I speak to Antonio?”

“If you'd care to dine with us.”

“Yes—to smell pork? To eat the creature your prophet from Nazareth drove the devil into? I'll buy with you, sell with you, talk with you, walk with you, and so on. But I won't eat with you, drink with you or pray with you. What's the news at the Rialto? Who's coming here?”

Antonio approached.

“This is Antonio,” Bassanio said.

“How he looks like a grovelling tax collector! I hate him for being a Christian, but more because, in his low, foolish way, he lends money free of charge and brings down the interest rates here in Venice. If I can once get him at a disadvantage, I'll feed the old grudge I bear him until it's fat. He hates our sacred people. In the very places where merchants gather, he rails at me, my business, and my honestly earned profit, which he calls interest. Let my people be cursed if I forgive him!” Shylock thought.

“Shylock, are you listening?”

“I'm considering the money I have on hand. As nearly as I remember, I can't immediately raise the full three thousand ducats. But what of it? Tubal, a wealthy Jew of my people, will supply the rest. Wait—how many months did you want?”

Shylock turned to Antonio.

“Good day to you, sir. We were just speaking of you,” Shylock said.

“Shylock, although I neither lend nor borrow at interest, I'll break my habit to meet my friend's urgent need. Bassanio, does Shylock know how much you want?” Antonio asked.

“Yes, yes. Three thousand ducats.”

“And for three months.”

“I'd forgotten. Three months. You told me. Well, then, your bond. Let me see. But listen: I thought you said you never lend or borrow for profit.”

“I never do.”

Shylock said, “When Jacob tended his uncle Laban's sheep—Jacob was the third to inherit the blessing passed down from our holy Abraham, thanks to his clever mother's efforts on his behalf; yes, the third—”

“What about him? Did he charge interest?”

“No, not interest. Not what you'd call interest directly. But notice what Jacob did. He and Laban agreed that every streaked or spotted lamb would be Jacob's wages. At the end of autumn the ewes were in heat and went to the rams. While the woolly creatures were in the act of mating, the clever shepherd peeled stripes into some sticks and set them before the lustful ewes. They conceived, and when lambing time came they gave birth to streaked and spotted lambs. Those belonged to Jacob. That was a way to prosper, and he was blessed. Prosperity is a blessing, provided you don't steal it.”

“Jacob worked for that payment, sir, but it depended on an uncertain outcome. The result wasn't in his power. Heaven shaped it. Did you bring this up to justify charging interest? Or are your gold and silver ewes and rams, able to breed more of their own kind?”

“I couldn't say. I make them breed as quickly. But listen, sir.”

“Notice that, Bassanio. The devil can quote scripture to suit himself. An evil soul calling holy words as witnesses is a smiling villain, a handsome apple rotten at the core. What an attractive face deceit can wear!” Antonio said.

“Three thousand ducats. A good round sum. Three months out of twelve—a quarter of the year. Now let me see, the rate—”

“Well, Shylock? Shall we be indebted to you?”

“Antonio, time and again at the Rialto you've abused me about my money and the interest I charge. I've always borne it with a patient shrug. Endurance is the badge our whole people wear. You call me a follower of a false faith, a cut-throat dog. You spit on my Jewish coat—all because I earn interest on my own money. And now, it seems, you need my help. You come to me and say, 'Shylock, we want money.' You say it—you who spat into my beard and kicked me as you'd kick a stray dog away from your door. Money is what you ask for. What should I say? 'Does a dog have money? Can a dog lend three thousand ducats?' Or should I bow low, speaking in a slave's voice, hardly breathing, whispering humbly: 'Kind sir, you spat on me last Wednesday. Another day you kicked me; another time you called me a dog. In return for these courtesies, I'll lend you all that money'?”

“I'm just as likely to call you that again, spit on you again, and kick you too. If you lend this money, don't lend it as to friends. When did friendship ever ask a friend's money—lifeless metal—to breed more money? Lend it to your enemy. Then, if he defaults, you can demand the penalty without looking so bad.”

“Look how you rage! I want to be friends, to win your affection, forget the humiliations you've stained me with, meet your present need, and take not one penny of interest. And you won't hear me. It's kindness I'm offering,” Shylock said.

“That would be kindness,” Bassanio said.

“And kindness is what I'll show. Come to a notary, who can draw up the legal document, and sign your personal promise to repay. Just as a cheerful joke, if you don't repay the stated amount on the stated day at the stated place, let the penalty be exactly one pound of your fine flesh, to be cut off and taken from whatever part of your body I please.”

“Agreed. I'll sign that bond, and say the Jew has a great deal of kindness in him,” Antonio said.

“You won't sign a bond like that for me. I'd rather remain in need,” Bassanio said.

“Don't worry. I won't incur the penalty. Within two months—a month before the bond falls due—I expect returns worth nine times the amount of this loan,” Antonio said.

“Father Abraham, what creatures these Christians are! Their own harsh dealings teach them to suspect everyone else's intentions. Tell me: if he fails to pay on the due date, what would I gain by enforcing the penalty? A pound of human flesh is neither as valuable nor as profitable as mutton, beef or goat. I offer this friendship to buy his favour. If he accepts, good. If not, goodbye. And for the love I offer, please don't wrong me,” Shylock said.

“Yes, Shylock. I'll sign the bond.”

“Then meet me at the notary's at once. Tell him how to draw up this playful bond. I'll collect the ducats, look in on my house—left in the doubtful care of a wasteful rascal—and join you shortly.”

“Hurry, kind Jew.”

Shylock left to collect the money and look in on his house. Antonio and Bassanio remained, the proposed bond still between them.

“The Hebrew will turn Christian. He's growing kind,” Antonio said.

“I don't like generous terms joined to a villain's mind,” Bassanio said.

“Come. There's nothing to fear. My ships come home a month before the debt is due.”

They went towards the business of signing. Antonio counted on his ships returning a month before the payment fell due.


## 4. The Prince’s Oath

Brass horns announced the Prince of Morocco. He entered with his attendants. Portia and Nerissa were there, with other members of the household.

“Don't dislike me for my complexion, the dark uniform the blazing sun has given me, for I was born and live in a land close to the sun. Bring me the fairest man born in the north, where the sun barely thaws the icicles, and let us cut ourselves for your love to prove whose blood is redder, his or mine. Lady, this face of mine has frightened brave men. I swear by my love that the most admired virgins of my country have loved it too. I wouldn't change this colour except to capture your thoughts, my gentle queen,” the Prince of Morocco said.

“In choosing, I'm not guided only by a young woman's fussy eye for looks. Besides, the lottery governing my future takes away my freedom to choose at all. But if my father hadn't limited me, enclosing me in his ingenious condition that I marry whoever wins me by the means I've explained, you, famous prince, would have as good a chance of winning my affection as any suitor I've seen so far,” Portia said.

“I thank you even for that. Then please lead me to the chests to try my luck. By this curved sword, which killed the Persian ruler and a Persian prince who won three battles against Sultan Solyman, I'd outstare the fiercest eyes, outdare the boldest heart on earth, snatch suckling cubs from a she-bear, even mock a lion roaring for prey, to win you. But alas! If Hercules and his servant Lichas threw dice to decide the better man, the weaker hand might throw the higher number. Hercules would be beaten by his page. So, led by blind fortune, I may miss a prize a less worthy man might win, and die of grief.”

“You must take your chance. Either don't try at all, or swear before you choose that if you choose wrongly you'll never again pursue a woman with marriage in mind. Think carefully.”

“I accept. Come, lead me to my chance.”

“First, to the temple to take your oath. After dinner, you'll take your chance,” Portia said.

“Good fortune, then! To make me the most blessed or most cursed of men.”

The brass horns sounded again as they went to take the oath. The choice itself would wait until after dinner.


## 5. Conscience and the Devil

Launcelot came into a Venetian street.

“Surely my conscience will let me run away from this Jew, my master. The devil's at my elbow, tempting me: 'Gobbo, Launcelot Gobbo, good Launcelot'—or 'good Gobbo', or 'good Launcelot Gobbo'—'use your legs! Get a head start! Run away!' My conscience says, 'No. Be careful, honest Launcelot. Be careful, honest Gobbo'—or, as mentioned, 'honest Launcelot Gobbo. Don't run. Scorn taking to your heels.' Well, the devil is very brave about telling me to clear out. 'Off!' he says. 'Away! For heaven's sake, show some spirit and run!' But my conscience hangs round my heart's neck and says wisely, 'My honest friend Launcelot, you're an honest man's son.' Or rather an honest woman's son, because my father had a bit of a wild streak about him, a bit of a taste for women. Anyway, my conscience says, 'Launcelot, don't budge.' 'Budge,' says the devil. 'Don't budge,' says my conscience. 'Conscience,' I say, 'that's good advice.' 'Devil,' I say, 'that's good advice.' If conscience rules me, I stay with the Jew, my master, who—God forgive the expression—is a kind of devil. If I run away from the Jew, I'm ruled by the fiend, who—with all due respect—is the devil himself. Certainly the Jew is the devil incarnal, in the flesh. And in all conscience, my conscience is a hard-hearted conscience to advise me to stay. The devil gives friendlier advice. I'll run, devil. My heels are yours to command. I'll run,” Launcelot said to himself.

Old Gobbo entered carrying a basket.

“Young master, you there—please, which way is it to the Jew's house?” old Gobbo asked.

“Good heavens, that's the very father who fathered me! He's not just sand-blind but gravel-blind—half blind and then some. He doesn't know me. I'll try some confusions on him and see how mixed up he gets,” Launcelot thought.

“Young gentleman, please, which way is it to the Jew's house?”

“Turn right at the next turning, then at the very next turning turn left; and at the very next turning don't turn either way, but turn indirectly down to the Jew's house.”

“By God's saints, that'll be hard to find. Can you tell me whether a fellow called Launcelot, who lives with him, lives with him or not?” old Gobbo asked.

“Do you mean young Master Launcelot?”

He was about to bring on tears, Launcelot told himself.

“Do you mean young Master Launcelot?” Launcelot asked.

“No master, sir. A poor man's son. His father, though I say it myself, is an honest, exceedingly poor man, and, thank God, comfortable enough,” old Gobbo said.

“Whatever his father may be, we're talking about young Master Launcelot.”

“Your honour's friend, sir, and Launcelot,” old Gobbo said.

“But therefore, old man—therefore, I ask you—are you talking about young Master Launcelot?”

“Launcelot, if it pleases your mastership.”

“Therefore, Master Launcelot. Don't speak of Master Launcelot, old father. According to Fates and Destinies and those peculiar expressions, the Sisters Three who rule our lives, and similar branches of learning, the young gentleman is deceased—or, in plain language, gone to heaven.”

“God forbid! The boy was the walking stick of my old age, my very support.”

“Do I look like a club or a post holding up a shed? A stick or a support? Don't you know me, father?” Launcelot asked.

“Alas, I don't know you, young gentleman. But tell me, is my boy—God rest his soul—alive or dead?”

“Don't you know me, father?”

“Alas, sir, I'm half blind. I don't know you.”

Launcelot knelt.

“Even if your eyes worked, you might not recognise me. It takes a wise father to know his own child. Well, old man, here's news of your son. Give me your blessing. Truth comes to light. Murder can't stay hidden long. A man's son may stay hidden, but truth comes out in the end.”

“Please, sir, stand up. I'm sure you aren't my boy Launcelot,” old Gobbo said.

“Please, no more fooling. Give me your blessing. I am Launcelot: your boy in the past, your son in the present, your child in the future.”

“I can't believe you're my son.”

“I don't know what to make of that. But I'm Launcelot, the Jew's servant, and I'm certain your wife Margery is my mother.”

“Her name is Margery, indeed. If you're Launcelot, I swear you're my own flesh and blood. Praise the Lord, what a beard you've grown! There's more hair on your chin than on the tail of Dobbin, my cart horse.”

“Then Dobbin's tail must be growing backwards. Last time I saw him, he certainly had more hair on his tail than I have on my face,” Launcelot said.

“Lord, how you've changed! How do you and your master get along? I've brought him a present. How do you two get along now?”

“Well, well. But I've settled on running away, and I won't settle down until I've covered some ground. My master's a real Jew. A present? Give him a hanging rope! He starves me. I'm all ribs—you could count every finger I have with them. Father, I'm glad you've come. Give your present to Master Bassanio. He gives his servants splendid new uniforms. If I don't serve him, I'll run as far as God's earth goes. Oh, wonderful luck! Here he comes. Speak to him, father. I'll be a Jew myself if I serve the Jew any longer.”

Bassanio came along with Leonardo and his other followers.

“You may do that, but hurry it along so supper is ready by five at the latest. Have these letters delivered, get the servants' uniforms made, and ask Gratiano to come to my lodging shortly,” Bassanio said.

A servant left to carry out the orders. Launcelot urged his father forward.

“Speak to him, father,” Launcelot said.

“God bless your honour!” old Gobbo said.

“Thank you. Do you want something from me?” Bassanio asked.

“Here's my son, sir, a poor boy—”

Launcelot said, “Not a poor boy, sir, but the rich Jew's servant, who would like—as my father will specify—”

“He has a great infection, sir—a real desire, as you might say—to serve—”

Launcelot said, “The long and short of it is, I serve the Jew, and I want—as my father will specify—”

“His master and he, begging your honour's pardon, are scarcely the best of friends—”

“Briefly, the truth is that the Jew has wronged me, which causes me—as my father, who I hope is an old man, will fructify in his explanation to you—”

“I've brought a dish of pigeons to give your honour, and my request is—”

“Very briefly, the request is impertinent to myself—it concerns me personally—as your honour will learn from this honest old man. Though I say it, an old man, yet a poor man—my father.”

“Let one of you speak for both. What do you want?” Bassanio asked.

“To serve you, sir.”

“That's the very defect of the matter, sir—the whole point of it,” old Gobbo said.

“I know you well. Your request is granted. Your master Shylock spoke to me about you today and recommended you for advancement—if leaving a rich Jew’s service to become a servant of such a poor gentleman counts as advancement,” Bassanio said.

“The old saying goes, 'If you have God's grace, you have enough.' It's nicely divided between you and my master Shylock, sir. You have God's grace, and he has enough—enough money,” Launcelot said.

“Well said. Go with your son, father. Launcelot, say goodbye to your old master and find my lodging. Now, one of you servants: give him a uniform with more decorative trim than the others. See to it,” Bassanio said.

“Come on, father. Oh, I can't get a job, can I? I haven't a tongue in my head! Well, what man in Italy has a finer palm to stretch out over a Bible and swear on? I'm going to have good fortune. Look at this little lifeline on my palm! And look at the trifling number of wives the lines predict! Fifteen wives—nothing at all. Eleven widows and nine maidens: a modest income for one man. Then escaping drowning three times, and having my life endangered by the edge of a feather bed—such narrow escapes! Well, if Fortune is a woman, she's a good girl for sending me all this good fortune. Come, father. I'll say goodbye to the Jew in the blink of an eye.”

Launcelot and his father went off to take leave of Shylock.

“Please remember this, Leonardo. Once you've bought these things and put them in order, hurry back. I'm entertaining my closest friends tonight. Go quickly,” Bassanio said.

“I'll do my very best,” Leonardo said.

Gratiano arrived while Leonardo was still near enough to answer him.

“Where's your master?” Gratiano asked.

“Walking over there, sir,” Leonardo said.

Leonardo went about his business, leaving Gratiano to approach Bassanio.

“Bassanio!” Gratiano said.

“Gratiano!” Bassanio said.

“I have a favour to ask.”

“It's granted.”

“You mustn't refuse me. I must come to Belmont with you,” Gratiano said.

“Then you must. But listen, Gratiano. You're too wild, too rough and too loud. Those qualities suit you well enough, and we who know you don't consider them faults. But strangers may think them too uncontrolled. Please cool your bounding spirits with a few drops of modesty. Otherwise your wild behaviour may give people the wrong idea about me where we're going and ruin my hopes.”

“Listen, Bassanio. If I don't put on a sober manner, speak respectfully, swear only now and then, carry prayer books in my pocket and look modest—more than that, lower my hat over my eyes like this during grace, sigh and say 'Amen', and observe every rule of good behaviour like a boy who's practised looking solemn to please his grandmother—never trust me again.”

“Well, we'll see how you behave.”

“But tonight doesn't count. You mustn't judge me by what we do tonight.”

“No, that would be a pity. I'd rather you put on your boldest display of good spirits. Our friends intend to enjoy themselves. Goodbye for now. I've business to do,” Bassanio said.

“And I must find Lorenzo and the others. We'll see you at supper.”

They parted with Belmont ahead of them and the evening's entertainment still to come. Gratiano had secured an exception for tonight.


## 6. A Secret Letter

Jessica and Launcelot were together in a room of Shylock's house.

“I'm sorry you're leaving my father. This house is hell, and you were a cheerful devil who made it a little less dreary. Goodbye. Here's a ducat for you. Launcelot, you'll see Lorenzo at supper soon. He's your new master's guest. Give him this letter secretly. Goodbye. I don't want my father to see us talking,” Jessica said.

“Goodbye! Tears exhibit my tongue—these tears are choking off my words. Most beautiful heathen, sweetest Jew! If some Christian didn't play the rogue and father you, I'm very much mistaken. But goodbye. These foolish tears are rather drowning my manly spirit. Goodbye,” Launcelot said.

“Goodbye, dear Launcelot.”

Launcelot left with Jessica's letter. She was alone.

“What a terrible sin I'm committing: I'm ashamed of being my father's child. But though I'm his daughter by blood, I haven't inherited his ways. Lorenzo, if you keep your promise, I'll end this conflict, become a Christian, and be your loving wife,” Jessica said to herself.

Jessica went out.


## 7. The Torchbearer

Gratiano, Lorenzo, Salarino and Salanio came into the street.

“No, we'll slip away during supper, put on our disguises at my lodging and come back—all within an hour,” Lorenzo said.

“We haven't prepared properly,” Gratiano said.

“We haven't even arranged for torchbearers to light our way,” Salarino said.

“Unless we can arrange it cleverly, it'll look awful. Better not attempt it, I think,” Salanio said.

“It's only four. We have two hours to get ready,” Lorenzo said.

Launcelot arrived carrying a letter.

“My friend Launcelot! What's the news?” Lorenzo asked.

“If you'd be pleased to break open the seal, the letter should seem to explain itself,” Launcelot said.

“I know the handwriting. A beautiful hand indeed—and the beautiful hand that wrote it is whiter than the paper.”

“Love news, indeed,” Gratiano said.

“With your permission, sir, I'll go,” Launcelot said.

“Where are you going?” Lorenzo asked.

“To invite my old master the Jew to supper with my new master the Christian tonight.”

“Here, take this. Tell gentle Jessica I won't fail her. Say it privately. Go, gentlemen—”

Launcelot went off with Lorenzo's message.

“Will you get ready for tonight's masquerade? I have a torchbearer,” Lorenzo said.

“Yes, I'll get to it straight away,” Salanio said.

“And so will I,” Salarino said.

“Meet Gratiano and me at his lodging in about an hour,” Lorenzo said.

“Good. Let's do that.”

Salarino and Salanio left to prepare. Gratiano stayed with Lorenzo, who still had Jessica's letter.

“Wasn't that letter from beautiful Jessica?” Gratiano asked.

“I must tell you everything. She's given me directions for how I'm to take her from her father's house, what gold and jewels she has, and the pageboy's clothes she's prepared to wear as a disguise. If her father the Jew ever reaches heaven, it'll be for his gentle daughter's sake. May misfortune never dare cross her path, unless it uses the excuse that she's the child of an unbelieving Jew. Come with me, and read her letter as we walk. Beautiful Jessica will be my torchbearer,” Lorenzo said.

They went together, with Gratiano reading the letter as they walked.


## 8. The House’s Ears

Before Shylock's house, the departing servant heard his old master's account of what awaited him in his new place. Between his remarks to Launcelot, Shylock called for Jessica.

“Well, you'll see. Your own eyes can judge the difference between old Shylock and Bassanio. Jessica!—Launcelot, you won't stuff yourself as you did with me—Jessica!—or sleep and snore and wear out your clothes. Jessica, I say!” Shylock said.

“Jessica!” Launcelot said.

“Who told you to call? I didn't tell you to call.”

“Your honour used to say I couldn't do anything without being told.”

Jessica came to answer him.

“Did you call? What do you want?” Jessica asked.

“I've been invited out to supper, Jessica. Here are my keys. But why should I go? They haven't invited me out of love. They're flattering me. Still, I'll go with hatred in my heart, and feed upon that extravagant Christian. Jessica, my girl, look after the house. I'm very reluctant to go. Some trouble is brewing to disturb my peace, for I dreamed of moneybags last night,” Shylock said.

“Please go, sir. My young master expects your reproach—he's waiting for you to arrive,” Launcelot said.

“And I expect his reproach too.”

“If they've conspired together, I won't say you'll see a masked entertainment in the streets. But if you do, then that nosebleed was a sign after all—the one I had last Black Monday at six in the morning—which fell that year on Ash Wednesday, four years ago, in the afternoon.”

“What? A masked show? Listen, Jessica. Lock my doors. When you hear the drum and that horrible squealing flute with its crooked-necked player, don't climb to the windows or stick your head into the street to stare at Christian fools in painted masks. Stop my house's ears—I mean the windows. Don't let the sound of their empty foolishness enter my sober house. By Jacob's staff, I've no desire to eat out tonight. But I'll go. Go ahead of me, fellow. Say I'm coming.”

“I'll go ahead, sir. Mistress, look out of the window despite all this. A Christian boy is coming who'll be worth a Jewish girl's glance,” Launcelot said.

Launcelot left. Shylock had heard him speak to Jessica, but not clearly enough to know what he had said.

“What did that fool, that child of the servant-woman Hagar, say? Well?” Shylock asked.

“He said, 'Goodbye, mistress.' Nothing else,” Jessica said.

“The fool is kind enough, but he eats enormously. Slow as a snail at making a profit, and sleeps more in the daytime than a wildcat. Idle bees don't live in my hive. That's why I part with him—and send him to Bassanio, who wants his help in wasting the money he's borrowed. Well, Jessica, go inside. Perhaps I'll be back immediately. Do as I tell you. Shut the doors behind you. Lock it away, and there it will stay: a proverb that never grows stale in a thrifty mind.”

Shylock went to supper. He left Jessica with the keys and instructions to shut the house against the night's entertainment.

“Goodbye. If nothing gets in the way of my good fortune, I'll have lost a father, and you'll have lost a daughter,” Jessica said to herself.

Jessica withdrew.


## 9. The Flight

Gratiano and Salarino entered, already masked.

“This is the overhang where Lorenzo asked us to wait,” Gratiano said.

“He's nearly missed his appointed hour,” Salarino said.

“Strange that he's late. Lovers usually run ahead of the clock.”

“The doves that pull the love goddess Venus's chariot fly ten times faster to seal a new promise of love than to keep an old one unbroken.”

“Always true. Who gets up from a feast as hungry as when he sat down? What horse retraces a tiring journey with the same fire it had setting out? Everything is pursued with more spirit than it's enjoyed. Look at a ship leaving its home bay, bright with flags, like a young gallant or a spendthrift, hugged and embraced by that whore, the wind. And look how it returns, like the same spendthrift: ribs battered, sails ragged, lean and torn and beggared by that whore, the wind.”

“Here comes Lorenzo. More of that later,” Salarino said.

Lorenzo arrived.

“Dear friends, forgive the delay. It wasn't me but my business that kept you waiting. When you want to play thieves and steal yourselves wives, I'll keep watch just as long for you. Come closer. Here lives the Jew who is to be my father-in-law. Hey! Who's inside?” Lorenzo asked.

Jessica appeared at a window above them. She was dressed as a boy.

“Who are you? Tell me, so I can be certain, though I'd swear I know your voice,” Jessica said.

“Lorenzo. Your love,” Lorenzo said.

“Certainly Lorenzo, and certainly my love. Who do I love so much? And who but you, Lorenzo, knows whether I'm yours?”

“Heaven and your own thoughts can testify that you are mine,” Lorenzo said.

“Here, catch this small chest. It's worth the trouble. I'm glad it's night and you can't see me. I'm so ashamed of this change of clothes. But love is blind, and lovers can't see the pretty foolish things they do. If they could, Cupid himself would blush to see me turned into a boy.”

“Come down. You must be my torchbearer.”

“What, must I hold up a candle to light my shameful deeds? They're already far too light—too conspicuous and too indecent. That job would expose me, love, when I ought to be hidden.”

“You are hidden, sweetheart, there in those lovely boy's clothes. But come now. The night that conceals us is running away, and they're waiting for us at Bassanio's feast.”

“I'll lock the doors, put some more gold on myself—some more ducats—and come straight down,” Jessica said.

Jessica disappeared from the window.

“I swear by my hood, she's a true gentlewoman—gentle, and a Gentile: no Jew!” Gratiano said.

“Curse me if I don't love her with all my heart. She's wise, if I'm any judge; beautiful, if my eyes tell the truth; and faithful, as she's proved. So, wise, beautiful and faithful as she is, she'll live in my faithful soul,” Lorenzo said.

Jessica came down to join them, now ready to leave the house.

“Here you are! Come, gentlemen, away. Our masked companions will be waiting,” Lorenzo said.

Lorenzo went away with Jessica and Salarino. Gratiano remained behind.

Antonio arrived, looking for the men who were to sail with Bassanio.

“Who's there?” Antonio asked.

“Antonio!” Gratiano said.

“Come on, Gratiano! Where are the others? It's nine. Our friends are all waiting for you. No masked entertainment tonight. The wind has turned so Bassanio can sail, and he's about to board. I've sent twenty people looking for you.”

“I'm glad of it. Nothing would please me more than setting sail and getting away tonight.”

Gratiano left with Antonio. The changed wind had ended the plan for a masked entertainment and brought the departure forward.


## 10. All That Glitters

The brass horns sounded at Belmont. Portia entered with the Prince of Morocco and their attendants.

“Draw back the curtains and show the prince the three chests. Now make your choice,” Portia said.

“The first is gold, with this inscription: 'Whoever chooses me will gain what many men desire.' The second is silver, and promises: 'Whoever chooses me will get as much as he deserves.' The third is dull lead, with an equally blunt warning: 'Whoever chooses me must give and risk everything he has.' How will I know if I choose the right one?” the Prince of Morocco asked.

“One contains my portrait, prince. If you choose that one, I'm yours as well.”

“May some god guide my judgement. Let me look over the inscriptions again. What does this lead chest say? 'Whoever chooses me must give and risk everything he has.' Must give? For what? For lead? Risk everything for lead? This chest threatens. Men who risk everything hope for a handsome return. A golden mind won't stoop to something that looks like waste metal. I won't give or risk anything for lead. What does silver say with her virgin-white colour? 'Whoever chooses me will get as much as he deserves.' As much as he deserves. Stop there, Morocco. Weigh your worth fairly. By your own estimate, you deserve a great deal. But a great deal might still fall short of the lady. And yet doubting my own worth would be a weak way to discredit myself. As much as I deserve! That's the lady. I deserve her by birth, wealth, grace and breeding. Above all, I deserve her by my love. What if I went no further and chose this? Let's read the words engraved in gold once more. 'Whoever chooses me will gain what many men desire.' That's the lady. The whole world wants her. They come from the earth's four corners to kiss this shrine, this living, breathing saint. The far-off deserts of Hyrcania and Arabia's vast wilderness are now mere highways for princes coming to see beautiful Portia. The ocean, lifting its ambitious head to spit in heaven's face, doesn't stop foreign suitors. They cross it like a brook to see her. One of these holds her heavenly portrait. Is it likely that lead contains her? A thought so base could damn my soul. Lead would be too coarse even to enclose her burial cloth in a dark grave. Could she be walled inside silver, when it's worth ten times less than gold tested for purity? A sinful thought. A jewel so precious was never set in anything less than gold. In England they have a gold coin stamped with an angel, but that angel is only on the surface. Here an angel lies entirely within a golden bed. Give me the key. This is my choice. Let fortune do what it will.”

“Take it, prince. If my likeness is there, I'm yours.”

Morocco unlocked the golden chest.

“Hell! What's this? A rotting skull, with a written scroll in its empty eye! I'll read it,” the Prince of Morocco said.

“'Not everything that glitters is gold. You've often heard that saying told. Many a man has sold his life merely to see my outward splendour. Gold-covered tombs have worms inside. Had you been as wise as bold, young in body, in judgement old, this answer would not have been written for you. Farewell. Your courtship has grown cold.' Cold indeed—and all my labour lost. Goodbye, warmth; welcome, frost. Portia, goodbye. My heart is too grieved for a long farewell. This is how losers leave,” the Prince of Morocco read.

Morocco left with his attendants to the sound of brass horns. He had kept his farewell short.

“Glad to be rid of him. Go, close the curtains. Let everyone of his complexion choose as he did,” Portia said.

Portia and her attendants left.


## 11. News at the Rialto

Salarino and Salanio came into a Venetian street.

“I saw Bassanio sail. Gratiano went with him. And I'm certain Lorenzo isn't on their ship,” Salarino said.

“That villainous Jew raised such an outcry he roused the Duke, who went with him to search Bassanio's ship,” Salanio said.

“He was too late. The ship had sailed. But the Duke was told Lorenzo and his loving Jessica had been seen together in a gondola. Antonio also assured him they weren't aboard with Bassanio.”

“I've never heard such confused, strange, outrageous, changeable passion as that Jewish dog shouted in the streets. 'My daughter! My ducats! My daughter! Run away with a Christian! My ducats, turned Christian too! Justice! The law! My ducats and my daughter! A sealed bag—two sealed bags of ducats, double-value ducats—stolen by my daughter! And jewels! Two stones, two precious, valuable stones, stolen by my daughter! Justice! Find the girl! She has the stones on her, and the ducats!'”

“All the boys in Venice follow him, shouting, 'His stones! His daughter! His ducats!'.”

“Good Antonio had better pay on time, or he will pay for this,” Salanio said.

“That reminds me. Yesterday I spoke to a Frenchman who said a richly loaded ship from our country had been lost in the narrow channel between France and England. I thought of Antonio and silently hoped it wasn't his.”

“You'd better tell Antonio what you heard. But break it gently. It may upset him.”

“No kinder gentleman walks the earth. I saw him say goodbye to Bassanio. Bassanio said he'd hurry home. Antonio answered, 'Don't. Don't spoil what you've gone to do by rushing back for my sake. Stay until the time is ripe. And don't let the Jew's bond intrude on your thoughts of love. Enjoy yourself. Give your best attention to courtship and those lovely gestures of affection that will suit you there.' Then, his eyes full of tears, he turned his face away, reached back, and gripped Bassanio's hand with such unmistakable affection. And so they parted.”

“I think he loves the world only for Bassanio's sake. Let's find him and brighten this sadness he holds so close with some pleasure or other.”

“Let's,” Salarino said.

They went to find Antonio and cheer him.


## 12. As Much as He Deserves

At Belmont, Nerissa entered with a servant.

“Quickly, please. Draw back the curtain at once. The Prince of Arragon has sworn his oath and is coming to choose,” Nerissa said.

The Prince of Arragon entered with Portia and their attendants as the brass horns sounded.

“There are the chests, noble prince. If you choose the one that contains my portrait, we'll be married immediately. But if you fail, you must leave at once, without another word,” Portia said.

“I've sworn to observe three conditions. First, never to tell anyone which chest I chose. Second, if I choose wrongly, never again in my life to pursue a young woman with marriage in mind. Finally, if my choice is unlucky, to leave you immediately,” the Prince of Arragon said.

“Everyone who comes to take a chance on my worthless self swears to those conditions.”

“And I am ready. May fortune favour my heart's hope. Gold, silver and base lead. 'Whoever chooses me must give and risk everything he has.' Lead, you'll have to look better before I give or risk anything. What does gold say? Let me see. 'Whoever chooses me will gain what many men desire.' What many men desire. 'Many' may mean the foolish crowd, who judge by appearances and learn no more than their silly eyes can teach. They don't look within. Like swallows nesting on an outside wall, they expose themselves to the weather, right in the path of danger. I won't choose what many men desire. I won't agree with common minds and put myself among the uncivilised masses. Then you, silver treasure chest: tell me your inscription again. 'Whoever chooses me will get as much as he deserves.' Well said. Who should cheat fortune and gain honour without the stamp of merit? Let no one presume to wear a dignity he hasn't earned. If only estates, ranks and offices weren't corruptly acquired, and pure honour were won by the wearer's worth! How many who now take their hats off respectfully to their superiors would be entitled to keep them on! How many rulers would be ruled! How many low-born natures would be sifted from the true grain of honour, and how much real honour recovered from the chaff and wreckage of our times and made bright again! But to my choice. 'Whoever chooses me will get as much as he deserves.' I'll assume I deserve well. Give me the key. I'll unlock my fortune here.”

Arragon opened the silver chest. He looked at what it contained without immediately speaking.

“That's too long a pause for what you've found there,” Portia said.

“What's this? A picture of a squinting idiot holding a written message! I'll read it. How unlike Portia you are! How unlike my hopes and what I deserve! 'Whoever chooses me will get as much as he deserves.' Did I deserve nothing more than a fool's head? Is this my prize? Do I deserve no better?” the Prince of Arragon asked.

“The person who commits an offence and the person who judges it have different, opposing parts to play. You can't be the judge of your own case.”

“What's written here?”

“'Seven times the fire tested this silver. A judgement that never chooses wrongly has been tested seven times too. Some people kiss mere shadows; theirs is only a shadow's happiness. There are living fools, I know, silvered over; and so was this one. Whatever wife you take to bed, I, this fool's head, will always be the head on your shoulders. Be off. Your business here is done.' The longer I stay, the more foolish I'll look. I came courting with one fool's head; I leave with two. Dear lady, goodbye. I'll keep my oath and bear my anger patiently,” the Prince of Arragon read.

Arragon departed with his attendants, taking his disappointment and his oath with him.

“So the candle has singed the moth. These calculating fools! They think so cleverly about their choice that their own cleverness loses them the prize,” Portia said.

“The old saying tells no lie: hanging and marrying go by destiny,” Nerissa said.

“Come, Nerissa. Close the curtain.”

A servant came in.

“Where's my lady?” the servant asked.

“Here. What do you want, my lord?” Portia asked.

“Madam, a young Venetian has arrived at your gate ahead of his master to announce that he's approaching. He brings tangible greetings: not just compliments and courteous words, but valuable gifts. I've never seen such a promising ambassador of love. No sweet April day ever promised a rich summer as beautifully as this messenger heralds his master,” the servant said.

“Enough, please. You praise him with such festive eloquence I'm half afraid you'll tell me he's related to you. Come, Nerissa. I'm longing to see this courteous courier of lively Cupid.”

“God of love, let it be Bassanio, if that is your will!” Nerissa said.

Portia went with Nerissa to meet the messenger.


## 13. The Lesson of Revenge

Salanio and Salarino came into the street.

“Well, what's the news at the Rialto?” Salanio asked.

“The report still stands uncontradicted that one of Antonio's richly loaded ships was wrecked in the English Channel. The Goodwin Sands, I think they call the place. A dangerous, deadly sandbank where the bones of many great ships lie buried—if that gossip Rumour is an honest woman who keeps her word,” Salarino said.

“I wish she were as big a liar about this as any gossip who ever crunched ginger or made her neighbours believe she was crying over her third husband's death. But it's true, without wandering into long-windedness or straying off the straight road of conversation, that good Antonio, honest Antonio—if only I had a description worthy to stand beside his name—”

“Come on. Get to the full stop—finish the sentence.”

“What? The end of it is, he's lost a ship.”

“I hope that proves to be the end of his losses,” Salarino said.

“Let me say amen quickly, before the devil spoils my prayer. Here he comes in the shape of a Jew.”

Shylock came towards them.

“Well, Shylock! What's the news among the merchants?” Salanio asked.

“You know. No one knows as well as you about my daughter's flight,” Shylock said.

“Certainly. I even knew the tailor who made her disguise—the wings she flew away in,” Salarino said.

“And Shylock knew the bird had grown her feathers. Once that happens, it's their nature to leave the mother bird,” Salanio said.

“And she's damned for it,” Shylock said.

“Certainly—if the devil gets to judge her.”

“My own flesh and blood rebelling!”

“Shame on you, you old carcass! Your flesh is rising up? Are you still getting hard at your age?”

“I mean my daughter is my flesh and blood,” Shylock said.

“There's more difference between her flesh and yours than between black jet stone and white ivory; more between your blood and hers than between red wine and white Rhine wine. But tell us: have you heard whether Antonio's lost anything at sea?” Salarino asked.

“There's another bad bargain of mine. A bankrupt, a spendthrift, who hardly dares show his head at the Rialto. A beggar who used to enter the market so smartly dressed. Let him watch out for his bond. He used to call me a usurer—a moneylender who charges interest. Let him watch out for his bond. He used to lend money as a Christian kindness. Let him watch out for his bond.”

“Surely, if he defaults, you won't take his flesh. What use would it be?”

“To bait fish. If it feeds nothing else, it will feed my revenge. He's humiliated me, kept me from earning half a million, laughed at my losses, mocked my profits, scorned my people, obstructed my bargains, cooled my friends towards me and fired up my enemies against me. And why? Because I'm a Jew. Doesn't a Jew have eyes? Doesn't a Jew have hands, organs, a body, senses, feelings, passions? Don't we eat the same food, get hurt by the same weapons, catch the same diseases, recover through the same remedies, feel the same summer heat and winter cold as a Christian? If you prick us, don't we bleed? If you tickle us, don't we laugh? If you poison us, don't we die? And if you wrong us, won't we take revenge? If we're like you in everything else, we'll resemble you in that. If a Jew wrongs a Christian, what is the Christian's humble response? Revenge. If a Christian wrongs a Jew, what should the Jew's patient response be, following the Christian example? Revenge. I'll practise the wickedness you teach me. And I'll make sure I outdo the lesson.”

A servant came in.

“Gentlemen, my master Antonio is at home and would like to speak to you both,” the servant said.

“We've been looking everywhere for him,” Salarino said.

Tubal approached before the two friends had left.

“Here comes another of the tribe. You couldn't find a third to match them unless the devil himself turned Jew,” Salanio said.

Salanio and Salarino went with Antonio's servant. Shylock remained with Tubal, who had been searching in Genoa.

“Well, Tubal! What news from Genoa? Have you found my daughter?” Shylock asked.

“I often came to places where I heard news of her, but I can't find her,” Tubal said.

“There, there, there! A diamond gone that cost me two thousand ducats in Frankfurt! The curse on our people never fell until now—I never felt it until now. Two thousand ducats in that alone, and other precious, precious jewels! I wish my daughter lay dead at my feet with the jewels in her ears. I wish she lay in her coffin at my feet with the ducats inside it. No news of them? So that’s how it is. And I don't know what's been spent searching. Loss upon loss! The thief gone with so much, and so much spent finding the thief. No satisfaction. No revenge. No bad luck in the world except what lands on my shoulders. No sighs but mine. No tears but mine,” Shylock said.

“Other men have bad luck too. Antonio, I heard in Genoa—”

“What? What? Bad luck?”

“Has lost a great ship coming from Tripoli.”

“Thank God. Thank God. Is it true?”

“I spoke to sailors who escaped the wreck,” Tubal said.

“Thank you, good Tubal. Good news! Good news! Ha, ha! Where? In Genoa?”

“I heard your daughter spent eighty ducats in Genoa in a single night.”

“You stick a dagger into me. I'll never see my gold again. Eighty ducats at one sitting! Eighty ducats!”

“Several of Antonio's creditors travelled back to Venice with me. They swear he's bound to go bankrupt.”

“I'm very glad. I'll plague him. I'll torture him. I'm glad,” Shylock said.

“One showed me a ring he got from your daughter in exchange for a monkey.”

“Curse her! You torture me, Tubal. It was my turquoise. Leah, my wife, gave it to me before we were married. I wouldn't have traded it for a wilderness of monkeys.”

“But Antonio is certainly ruined.”

“Yes, that's true. Very true. Go and hire a court officer for me, Tubal. Book him two weeks in advance, ready for when the bond falls due. I'll have Antonio's heart if he defaults, because with him out of Venice I could do whatever business I pleased. Go, Tubal. Meet me at our synagogue. Go, good Tubal. At our synagogue, Tubal.”

They left.


## 14. The Lead Chest

At Belmont, Bassanio, Portia, Gratiano, Nerissa and the attendants came in.

“Please wait. Give it a day or two before you take your chance. If you choose wrongly, I lose your company. So wait a little. Something tells me—though it isn't love—that I don't want to lose you. You know hatred wouldn't give advice like that. But in case you misunderstand me—though a girl is supposed to speak only in her thoughts—I'd keep you here a month or two before you risk choosing me. I could teach you to choose correctly, but then I'd break my oath. I'll never do that. So you may lose me. And if you do, you'll make me wish I'd sinned and broken it. Curse your eyes. They've bewitched me and divided me. Half of me is yours. The other half is yours—mine, I mean. But if it's mine, then it's yours, so all of me is yours. These wicked times put barriers between owners and what belongs to them. So I'm yours, and yet not yours. If that's how it turns out, let fortune go to hell for it, not me. I'm talking too much, but only to hold time back, stretch it out, keep you from choosing,” Portia said.

“Let me choose. As I am, I'm stretched on a torture rack,” Bassanio said.

“On the rack, Bassanio? Then confess what treason is mixed with your love.”

“Only the ugly treason of mistrust, which makes me fear I may not enjoy my love. Treason and my love could live in friendship no more easily than snow and fire.”

“But I fear you're speaking under torture. Men forced that way will say anything.”

“Promise me my life and I'll confess the truth,” Bassanio said.

“Then confess and live.”

“'Confess and love' would sum up my confession. What happy torture, when my torturer teaches me the answers that will free me! But let me face my fortune and the chests.”

“Go, then. I'm locked in one of them. If you love me, you'll find me. Nerissa, everyone, stand back. Let music play while he chooses. If he loses, he'll end like the swan said to sing as it dies, fading away in music. To make the likeness complete, my eye will be the stream of tears that forms his watery deathbed. He may win. What is music then? The fanfare as loyal subjects bow to a newly crowned king. The sweet sounds at dawn that slip into a dreaming bridegroom's ear and summon him to his wedding. Now he goes, as noble in appearance and far more loving than young Hercules when he rescued the virgin whom the wailing people of Troy had offered as tribute to the sea monster. I'm the sacrifice. These others stand apart like Trojan wives, their faces stained with tears, waiting to see how the rescue ends. Go, Hercules! If you live, I live. I watch the battle with far more fear than you who fight it.”

Music played while Bassanio considered the chests to himself.

“Tell me where infatuation is born: in the heart, or in the head? How is it conceived and fed? Answer, answer. In the eyes it first takes life; gazing feeds it, and infatuation dies in its cradle—the eyes where it first came to life. Let us ring its funeral bell. I'll begin: ding, dong, bell,” the singer sang.

“Ding, dong, bell,” the singer said.

“So appearances may show least of what a thing really is. The world is always fooled by decoration. In law, what argument is so corrupt that an eloquent voice can't disguise its evil? In religion, what damnable error can't some solemn face bless and support with scripture, hiding its ugliness under fine adornment? No vice is so plain that it doesn't wear some outward sign of virtue. How many cowards, their hearts as unreliable as stairs made of sand, wear the beards of Hercules or stern Mars, the god of war? Look inside and their livers are white as milk with fear. They wear only the beard, the outward growth of bravery to make themselves look fearsome. Look at beauty: it can be bought by weight, in paint and cosmetics. And there's a miracle of nature—the women who wear the most become the lightest—light in weight and loose in sexual morals. Those curled, golden, snakelike locks that play so wantonly in the wind, making a woman supposedly beautiful, often belonged to another head whose skull now lies in a tomb. Decoration is the deceptive shore of a dangerous sea; the lovely scarf veiling an Indian beauty; in a word, the seeming truth a cunning age puts on to trap the wisest. So, showy gold—hard food for Midas, whose touch turned even his meals to gold—I want none of you. Nor you, pale silver, the common servant passed from hand to hand. But you, poor lead, which threatens rather than promises: your paleness moves me more than eloquence. Here I choose. May joy follow,” Bassanio thought.

“How every other feeling vanishes into air: doubt, despair too quickly embraced, shuddering fear, green-eyed jealousy. Love, be moderate. Quiet this ecstasy. Hold joy within its bounds. Restrain this excess. I feel your blessing too strongly. Lessen it, before it overwhelms me,” Portia thought.

“What have I found?”

Bassanio opened the leaden chest.

“Beautiful Portia's likeness! What demigod has come so close to creating life? Do these eyes move? Or do they seem to move as they ride on my own moving eyes? Here are parted lips, separated by sweet breath. Only so sweet a barrier should divide such sweet companions. In her hair the painter has played the spider, weaving a golden net to catch men's hearts more securely than a web catches gnats. But her eyes—how could he keep his sight to paint them? Once he'd made the first, surely it would have stolen both his eyes and left the second unfinished. Yet however much my praise undervalues this portrait, the portrait falls just as far behind the real woman. Here's the scroll that holds and sums up my fortune,” Bassanio said.

“'You who don't choose by outward view, may your luck be fair, your choices true. Since this good fortune falls to you, be satisfied and seek no new. If this choice has pleased you well, and you count your fortune happiness, turn to where your lady stands and claim her with a loving kiss.' A gracious scroll. Beautiful lady, with your permission, I come as instructed, to give and to receive. Like a competitor who thinks he's pleased the crowd, hears their applause and cheering, and stands dizzy, still wondering whether all that precious praise is really his—so I stand, loveliest lady, uncertain whether what I see is true until you confirm it, sign it, make it binding,” Bassanio read.

“You see me where I stand, Bassanio, just as I am. For myself alone I wouldn't be ambitious to become much better. But for you, I'd be twenty times myself, and then three times that, a thousand times more beautiful, ten thousand times richer, so that simply to stand high in your estimation I might be beyond counting in goodness, beauty, property and friends. But the whole sum of me does amount to something: counted plainly, an untaught girl, unschooled and inexperienced. Happy that she's still young enough to learn; happier that she isn't too dull to learn; happiest that her gentle spirit gives itself to yours to be directed by her lord, her governor, her king. Myself and everything I own are now made over to you and yours. A moment ago I was lord of this beautiful house, master of my servants and queen of myself. Now this house, these servants and this very self are yours, my lord. I give them with this ring. If you part with it, lose it or give it away, let that foretell the ruin of your love and give me the right to accuse you,” Portia said.

“Lady, you've taken all my words. Only the blood in my veins speaks to you. My senses are as confused as a happy crowd after a beloved prince has made a beautiful speech: all their separate voices blending into a wilderness of nothing but joy, expressed and inexpressible. But when this ring leaves this finger, life leaves this body. Then you may safely say Bassanio is dead.”

“My lord and lady, now it's our turn, who've stood by and watched our hopes come true, to wish you joy. All joy to you both!” Nerissa said.

“Bassanio, and my gracious lady, I wish you every joy you could wish yourselves. I'm sure none of your wishes can take joy from me. And when you celebrate your marriage, I ask that I may be married at the same time,” Gratiano said.

“With all my heart—if you can find a wife,” Bassanio said.

“Thank you. You've found me one. My eyes are as quick as yours. You saw the mistress; I saw the maid. You fell in love; so did I. Delay suits me no better than it suits you. Your fortune depended on the chest, and as it turns out, so did mine. I courted until I sweated, and swore so many oaths of love that the roof of my mouth dried out. At last—if promises hold—this lovely woman promised me her love, provided you succeeded in winning her mistress.”

“Is this true, Nerissa?” Portia asked.

“It is, madam, if it pleases you,” Nerissa said.

“And you're sincere, Gratiano?” Bassanio asked.

“Faithfully so, my lord,” Gratiano said.

“Your marriage will greatly honour our feast.”

“We'll bet them a thousand ducats on who has the first boy.”

“What, with the money laid down—and your other stake down too, with no erection?” Nerissa asked.

“No, we'll never win at that game with the stake down! But who's coming? Lorenzo and his unbeliever? And my old Venetian friend Salerio?”

Lorenzo and Jessica arrived with Salerio, a messenger from Venice.

“Lorenzo, Salerio, welcome—if my very new position here gives me the right to welcome you. With your permission, sweet Portia, I welcome my dear friends and countrymen,” Bassanio said.

“And so do I. They're most welcome,” Portia said.

“Thank you. I hadn't intended to visit you here, Bassanio. But I met Salerio on the way, and he insisted beyond any refusal that I come with him,” Lorenzo said.

“I did, my lord, and with reason. Antonio sends his greetings,” Salerio said.

Salerio handed Bassanio a letter from Antonio.

“Before I open his letter, tell me how my good friend is,” Bassanio said.

“Not ill, unless in his mind; not well, unless in his mind. His letter will tell you his condition,” Salerio said.

“Nerissa, welcome our visitor. Make her feel at home. Your hand, Salerio! What's the news from Venice? How is that prince of merchants, good Antonio? He'll be glad of our success. We're like Jason, the hero who won the Golden Fleece. We've won our golden prize,” Gratiano said.

“I wish you'd won the wealth he's lost.”

“There's something cruel in that letter, stealing the colour from Bassanio's face. A dear friend dead? Nothing else could change a steady man so completely. What, worse and worse? Forgive me, Bassanio, but I'm half of you. I must be freely given half of whatever that paper brings,” Portia said.

“Sweet Portia, these are some of the unhappiest words ever put on paper. When I first declared my love, I told you openly that all my wealth ran in my veins: I was a gentleman. That was true. Yet you'll see how much I boasted even when I valued myself at nothing. I should have said I was worth less than nothing. To obtain my means, I put myself in debt to a dear friend and put that friend in debt to his bitterest enemy. Here is a letter whose paper is my friend's body, every word a gaping wound pouring out his life's blood. But is it true, Salerio? Have all his ventures failed? Not one succeeded? From Tripoli, Mexico, England, Lisbon, the North African coast, India—not one ship escaped those terrible rocks that ruin merchants?” Bassanio asked.

“Not one, my lord. And it seems that even if Antonio had the money now, the Jew wouldn't accept it. I've never known a creature shaped like a man so eager, so hungry, to destroy another man. He petitions the Duke morning and night, accusing the state of betraying its freedom if it denies him justice. Twenty merchants, the Duke himself, the most distinguished nobles have all reasoned with him. None can move him from his spiteful demand for the penalty, for justice, for his bond,” Salerio said.

“When I lived with him, I heard him swear to Tubal and Chus, men of his own people, that he'd rather have Antonio's flesh than twenty times the money owed. I know that unless law, authority and power stop him, things will go badly for poor Antonio,” Jessica said.

“Is this your dear friend who's in such trouble?” Portia asked.

“My dearest friend. The kindest man, the finest nature, tireless in doing good to others. More of the old Roman honour lives in him than in anyone breathing in Italy,” Bassanio said.

“How much does he owe the Jew?”

“Three thousand ducats, for me,” Bassanio said.

“No more than that? Pay him six thousand and cancel the bond. Double six thousand, then triple that, before a friend like this loses a hair through Bassanio's fault. First come to church and call me your wife. Then go to Venice, to your friend. You'll never lie beside Portia with a troubled soul. You'll have enough gold to pay this little debt twenty times. Once it's paid, bring your faithful friend home with you. Meanwhile, Nerissa and I will live like maidens and widows. Come. You'll leave on your wedding day. Welcome your friends and look cheerful. Since you've cost me dearly, I'll love you dearly. But let me hear your friend's letter.”

“Dear Bassanio, all my ships are lost. My creditors grow cruel. My fortune is nearly gone. I have failed to repay the Jew by the date in my bond. Its penalty is now due, and paying it cannot leave me alive. If I could only see you at my death, every debt between us would be cleared. Still, do as you please. If your love doesn't persuade you to come, don't let my letter persuade you,” Bassanio read.

“Love, finish everything quickly and go!”

“Since you so willingly let me go, I'll hurry. Until I return, no bed will be guilty of detaining me. I won't let rest delay my return to you.”

They left to prepare for the marriages and the journey.


## 15. The Course of Law

Shylock, Antonio, the jailer and Salarino came into a Venetian street.

“Jailer, watch him. Don't talk to me about mercy. This is the fool who lent money for nothing. Jailer, watch him,” Shylock said.

“Hear me out, good Shylock,” Antonio said.

“I'll have my bond—the penalty it promises. Don't argue against my bond. I've sworn an oath to have my bond. You called me a dog before you had a reason. Since I'm a dog, beware my teeth. The Duke will give me justice. And you, worthless jailer—I'm amazed you're foolish enough to bring him out into the street just because he asks.”

“Please hear me speak.”

“I'll have my bond. I won't hear you speak. I'll have my bond, so say no more. I won't be turned into a soft, dull-eyed fool, shaking my head, relenting, sighing, giving in to Christians pleading for him. Don't follow me. No more talk. I'll have my bond.”

Shylock left without allowing another appeal.

“The most hard-hearted dog that ever lived among men,” Salarino said.

“Let him go. I won't follow with more useless appeals. He wants my life, and I know why. People have often come to me pleading, and I've rescued them from his penalties. That's why he hates me,” Antonio said.

“I'm certain the Duke will never let this penalty stand.”

“The Duke can't stop the course of law, because denying foreigners the rights they enjoy in Venice would bring the state's justice into question. After all, the city's trade and prosperity depend on people from every nation. So, let's go. These griefs and losses have wasted me so much I'll hardly have a pound of flesh to spare tomorrow for my bloodthirsty creditor. Come, jailer. God grant that Bassanio comes to see me pay his debt. After that, I don't care what happens.”

Antonio went on with the jailer and Salarino.


## 16. Twenty Miles

At Belmont, Portia was with Nerissa, Lorenzo and Jessica, and her servant Balthasar.

“Madam, though I say it in your presence, you have a noble, true understanding of almost divine friendship. You show it most clearly in bearing your husband's absence as you do. But if you knew the man you're honouring, what a true gentleman you're helping and how dearly he loves your husband, you'd take more pride in this act than ordinary generosity can give you,” Lorenzo said.

“I've never regretted doing good, and I won't now. Friends who spend their time together, whose souls share the same yoke of love, must resemble each other in features, manners and spirit. That makes me think Antonio, being my husband's closest friend, must resemble my husband. If so, how little I've spent to buy a man so like my husband out of hellish misery. My husband is my own soul. But that comes too close to praising myself. No more of it. Listen to something else. Lorenzo, I entrust the care and management of my house to you until my husband returns. I've made a private vow to heaven to live in prayer and contemplation, with only Nerissa attending me, until her husband and mine return. There's a religious house two miles away. We'll stay there. Please don't refuse this responsibility, which affection and a certain necessity now place on you,” Portia said.

“With all my heart, madam. I'll obey all your honourable commands.”

“My servants already know my wishes. They'll accept you and Jessica as standing in for Bassanio and me. Goodbye until we meet again.”

“May happy hours and beautiful thoughts go with you.”

“I wish you all the happiness your heart could want,” Jessica said.

“Thank you. I'm very glad to wish the same for you. Goodbye, Jessica,” Portia said.

Jessica and Lorenzo left, entrusted with the house. Portia turned to Balthasar.

“Now, Balthasar, I've always found you honest and faithful. Let me find you so again. Take this letter and make every effort to reach Padua quickly. Put it directly into my cousin Doctor Bellario's hand. Whatever papers and clothes he gives you, bring them with the speed of thought to the crossing, the public ferry to Venice. Don't waste time talking. Go. I'll be there before you,” Portia said.

“Madam, I'll go as fast as I can,” Balthasar said.

Balthasar left with the letter for Padua. The papers and clothes he would bring back were to meet Portia at the ferry to Venice.

“Come, Nerissa. I have work in hand you don't yet know about. We'll see our husbands before they expect us,” Portia said.

“Will they see us?” Nerissa asked.

“They will, but we'll be dressed so they'll take us for men, complete with the parts we lack. I'll bet anything that once we're dressed as young men I'll be the prettier fellow of the two, wear my dagger with a better swagger, speak in that thin, breaking voice between boy and man, turn two dainty steps into a manly stride, and talk about fights like a fine young braggart. I'll tell elaborate lies about distinguished ladies who wanted my love and grew sick and died when I refused them. I couldn't help it! Then I'll repent and wish, after all, I hadn't killed them. I'll tell twenty of those little lies so men will swear I've been out of school more than a year. I have a thousand of those swaggering boys' immature tricks in mind, ready to practise.”

“What, shall we turn to men?”

“Shame! What a question, if anyone with a dirty mind were listening. Come. I'll explain the whole plan in the carriage waiting at the park gate. Hurry. We have twenty miles to cover today.”

Portia and Nerissa hurried towards the carriage at the park gate. Portia would explain the rest on the road.


## 17. The Price of Pork

Launcelot and Jessica were in the garden at Belmont.

“Yes, truly. The sins of the father fall on his children. So I promise you, I'm afraid for you. I've always been plain with you, and now I'll tell you what all my agitation on the matter has led me to. Cheer up, because I really think you're damned. There's only one hope that could help you, and that's rather a bastard hope,” Launcelot said.

“What hope is that, please?” Jessica asked.

“You can hope, at least, that your father didn't father you—that you aren't the Jew's daughter.”

“A bastard hope indeed. Then my mother's sins would fall on me.”

“Then I'm afraid you're damned by both parents. When I steer clear of Scylla, the monster—your father—I fall into Charybdis, the whirlpool—your mother. One danger or the other. You're lost either way.”

“My husband will save me. He's made me a Christian,” Jessica said.

“All the more blame to him. We had enough Christians already—as many as could comfortably live alongside one another. Making more Christians will raise the price of pigs. If we all become pork eaters, soon we won’t be able to buy a slice of bacon to cook on the coals.”

Lorenzo came into the garden.

“I'll tell my husband what you say, Launcelot. Here he comes,” Jessica said.

“I'll soon grow jealous, Launcelot, if you keep getting my wife into corners,” Lorenzo said.

“No need to worry, Lorenzo. Launcelot and I have quarrelled. He tells me outright there's no mercy for me in heaven because I'm a Jew's daughter. And he says you're a bad citizen because converting Jews to Christians puts up the price of pork.”

“I can answer to the state for that better than you can for swelling that Black woman's belly. The Moor is pregnant by you, Launcelot,” Lorenzo said.

“It's a bit much that the Moor should be more than she ought. But if she's less than an honest woman, a sexually virtuous woman, she's certainly more than I took her for,” Launcelot said.

“Every fool can twist a word these days. Soon the finest form of wit will be silence, and only parrots will be praised for talking. Go inside and tell them to prepare for dinner.”

“Already done, sir. They're prepared for dinner—they all have stomachs.”

“Good Lord, how you twist every word! Then tell them to prepare dinner.”

“That's done too. 'Cover' is the word you want now—cover the table,” Launcelot said.

“Then will you cover, sir?”

“Oh, no, sir. I know my place. I wouldn't put my hat on before you.”

“Still picking a quarrel with every opportunity! Must you display all your wit at once? Please understand a plain man's plain meaning. Go to the other servants. Tell them to cover the table, serve the food, and we'll come in to dinner.”

“The table, sir, will be served in; the food, sir, will be covered; and as for your coming in to dinner, sir, let moods and notions govern that.”

Launcelot went inside.

“What wonderful judgement in the way he dresses up his words! The fool has planted an army of fine expressions in his memory. I know plenty of fools in higher positions, dressed up just like him, who sacrifice meaning for a clever phrase. How are you, Jessica? Tell me, sweetheart, what do you think of Bassanio's wife?” Lorenzo asked.

“More highly than I can express. Bassanio ought to live a good, honest life, because blessed with a wife like that, he finds heaven's joys here on earth. If he doesn't mean to live a good, honest life here, then by rights he ought never to reach heaven. If two gods played some heavenly game and each staked an earthly woman, with Portia one of them, something else would have to be added to the other stake. This poor, rough world has no woman to match her,” Jessica said.

“And you have as good a husband in me as she is a wife.”

“No, but ask my opinion about that too.”

“I will shortly. First, let's go to dinner.”

“No, let me praise you while I have a stomach for it—while I still have the appetite,” Jessica said.

“Please, save it for table talk. Whatever you say, I'll digest it along with the other things.”

“Very well. I'll serve you up.”

Jessica and Lorenzo went in to dinner, with her promised account of his merits still to come.


## 18. Mercy and Justice

The Duke presided in the Venetian court, with the city's nobles attending. Antonio was there with Bassanio, Gratiano and Salerio.

“Is Antonio here?” the Duke asked.

“Ready, your Grace,” Antonio said.

“I'm sorry for you. You face an opponent made of stone, an inhuman wretch incapable of pity, without a grain of mercy.”

“I've heard how hard your Grace has worked to soften his severity. But since he remains unmoved, and no lawful means can put me beyond his malice, I'll set my patience against his fury. I'm prepared to suffer his tyranny and rage with a quiet spirit.”

“Someone call the Jew into court.”

“He's waiting at the door, my lord. Here he comes,” Salerio said.

Shylock entered.

“Make room. Let him stand before us. Shylock, everyone believes—and so do I—that you're carrying this show of malice to the last possible moment, and then you'll reveal a mercy and compassion even more extraordinary than your apparent cruelty. Although you now demand a pound of this poor merchant's flesh, we think you'll not only waive the penalty but, moved by human kindness and love, forgive half the original debt. You'll look with pity on his losses, piled on his back so quickly—enough to crush a prince among merchants and draw compassion from hearts of brass and flint, even from stubborn Turks and Tartars who've never been taught gentle courtesy. We all expect a kind answer, Jew,” the Duke said.

Shylock asked, “I've told your Grace what I intend. I've sworn by our holy Sabbath to claim the penalty due under my bond. If you deny it, let the danger fall on the legal charter that guarantees your city's rights, and on its freedom. You'll ask why I'd rather have a weight of dead flesh than three thousand ducats. I won't answer that. Suppose I say it's my whim. Is that an answer? What if my house is troubled by a rat and I choose to spend ten thousand ducats to have it poisoned? Does that answer you? Some men can't bear a roast pig with its mouth open. Some go mad at the sight of a cat. Others wet themselves when a bagpipe drones through its nose. Feeling rules passion, shaping it to what it likes or hates. So here's your answer. Just as there's no solid reason why one man can't bear a pig, another a harmless, useful cat, another a wool-covered bagpipe, but must give way to the humiliating compulsion to offend because he's offended, so I can give no reason, and will give none, beyond a settled hatred and a particular loathing for Antonio that makes me pursue this lawsuit that brings me no profit. Have you your answer?”

“That's no answer to excuse the course of your cruelty, you unfeeling man,” Bassanio said.

“I'm not obliged to please you with my answers.”

“Does everyone kill whatever he doesn't love?” Bassanio asked.

“Does anyone hate what he wouldn't kill?”

“An offence doesn't turn into hatred at once.”

“Would you let a snake bite you twice?”

“Please remember you're arguing with the Jew. You might as well stand on the beach and tell the tide not to rise to its usual height. You might as well ask a wolf why it's made a ewe cry for her lamb, or forbid the mountain pines to shake their tops and make a noise when the wind strikes them. Any impossible task would be as easy as softening his Jewish heart—and what could be harder than that? Please make no more offers, try nothing further. Let matters be settled plainly and quickly: give me my sentence, and the Jew his wish,” Antonio said.

“Here are six thousand ducats for your three thousand,” Bassanio said.

“Even if each of those six thousand ducats became six ducats—thirty-six thousand in all—I wouldn't take them. I'd have my bond,” Shylock said.

“How can you hope for mercy when you give none?” the Duke asked.

“What judgement should I fear when I do no wrong? Many of you own slaves you've bought. Like your donkeys, dogs and mules, you put them to low, servile work because you paid for them. Shall I tell you to free them? Marry them to your heirs? Why should they sweat under heavy loads? Give them beds as soft as yours, and food as richly seasoned. You'll answer, 'The slaves are ours.' And that's my answer. The pound of flesh I demand was dearly bought. It's mine, and I'll have it. Deny me, and shame on your law! Venice's decrees have no force. I stand here for judgement. Answer me: will I have it?”

“I have the authority to adjourn this court unless Bellario, the learned doctor of law I've summoned to decide this case, arrives today.”

“My lord, a messenger has just arrived from Padua with letters from the doctor. He's waiting outside,” Salerio said.

“Bring the letter. Call the messenger.”

“Take heart, Antonio. Courage, man! The Jew will have my flesh, blood, bones and all before you lose one drop of blood for me,” Bassanio said.

“I'm the flock's sick, castrated ram, the one most fit to die. The weakest fruit falls first. Let me fall. There's no better work for you, Bassanio, than to live on and write the words for my grave,” Antonio said.

Nerissa came into court dressed as a lawyer's clerk.

“Do you come from Padua, from Bellario?” the Duke asked.

“From both, my lord. Bellario sends his respects to your Grace,” Nerissa said.

She presented the letter.

“Why are you sharpening your knife so intently?” Bassanio asked.

“To cut the forfeited flesh from that bankrupt there,” Shylock said.

“It's on your soul, not your shoe's sole, that you sharpen that knife, cruel Jew. No metal—not even the executioner's axe—has half the edge of your malice. Can no prayer pierce you?” Gratiano asked.

“None you have the wit to make.”

“Damn you, you dog beyond all curses! Justice should be accused for letting you live. You almost shake my faith and make me believe Pythagoras, who thought animals' souls could enter human bodies. Your doglike spirit once ruled a wolf hanged for killing people. Its savage soul fled the gallows and entered you while you lay in your unholy mother's womb. Your appetites are wolfish: bloody, starved and ravenous,” Gratiano said.

“Until you can shout the seal off my bond, you're only hurting your lungs. Repair your wits, young man, before they're ruined beyond cure. I'm here for the law.”

“Bellario recommends a young, learned doctor to our court in this letter. Where is he?” the Duke asked.

“Waiting nearby to hear whether you'll admit him,” Nerissa said.

“With all my heart. Three or four of you go and bring him here courteously. Meanwhile, the court will hear Bellario's letter.”

“Your Grace should know that when your letter arrived I was very ill. At that moment a young doctor from Rome, named Balthasar, was visiting me in friendship. I explained the dispute between the Jew and Antonio the merchant. We consulted many books together. He brings my opinion, improved by his own learning, which I cannot praise highly enough. At my urging, he comes in my place to fulfil your Grace's request. Please don't let his youth prevent you from treating him with respect. I've never known so young a body with so old a head. I leave him to your gracious welcome. Putting him to the test will recommend him better than my words,” the clerk read.

“You've heard what learned Bellario writes. And here, I believe, is the doctor.”

Portia entered dressed as a doctor of law.

“Give me your hand. Do you come from old Bellario?” the Duke asked.

“I do, my lord,” Portia said.

“Welcome. Take your place. Do you know the dispute now before this court?”

“I've been thoroughly informed. Which is the merchant, and which the Jew?” Portia asked.

“Antonio, Shylock, step forward.”

“Is your name Shylock?”

“Shylock is my name,” Shylock said.

“The case you pursue is strange, yet it follows the rules so closely that Venetian law can't challenge your proceedings. You are subject to his power under this bond, aren't you?”

“Yes. So he says,” Antonio said.

“Do you acknowledge the bond?”

“I do.”

“Then the Jew must show mercy.”

“What compels me to do that? Tell me,” Shylock said.

“Mercy cannot be forced. It falls like gentle rain from heaven onto the earth below. It carries a double blessing: for the one who gives it and the one who receives it. It is most powerful in the powerful, and suits a king upon his throne better than his crown. His sceptre, the staff he carries as king, shows the force of earthly power, the symbol of awe and majesty that makes us fear a king. But mercy rises above that power. It sits enthroned within the hearts of kings. It belongs to God himself, and earthly power looks most like God's when mercy tempers justice. So, Jew, though justice is your plea, consider this: if strict justice ran its course, none of us would find salvation. We pray for mercy, and that very prayer teaches us to show mercy in our deeds. I've said this to soften the justice you demand. If you persist, this strict court of Venice must pass sentence against the merchant there,” Portia said.

“Let my deeds fall on my own head. I demand the law: the penalty due under my bond.”

“Can't he pay the money?”

“Yes. I offer it for him here in court—twice the amount. If that isn't enough, I'll guarantee ten times as much, with my hands, head and heart as the penalty. If even that won't satisfy him, it must be clear that malice is crushing honesty. Please, just this once, bend the law to your authority. Do a little wrong to do a great right. Stop this cruel devil getting his way,” Bassanio said.

“It cannot be done. No authority in Venice can change an established decree. It would be recorded as a precedent, and that example would let countless errors rush into the state. It cannot be.”

“A Daniel come to judge us—the wise judge from scripture! Yes, a Daniel! Wise young judge, how I honour you!” Shylock said.

“Please let me see the bond.”

“Here it is, most respected doctor. Here.”

“Shylock, you're being offered three times your money.”

“An oath! An oath! My oath is sworn in heaven. Shall I burden my soul by breaking that sworn oath? Not for all Venice.”

“The debt was not repaid on time, so the penalty in the bond has indeed fallen due. Under it, the Jew may lawfully claim a pound of flesh, to be cut nearest the merchant's heart. Show mercy. Take three times your money and tell me to tear up the bond,” Portia said.

“Only when it's paid in the way the bond itself specifies. You appear to be a worthy judge. You know the law. Your explanation has been entirely sound. I charge you by that law, of which you're such a worthy pillar, to give judgement. I swear by my soul that no human tongue can change me. I stand by my bond.”

“I beg the court with all my heart to give judgement,” Antonio said.

“Then this is the judgement. You must prepare your chest for his knife,” Portia said.

“Noble judge! Excellent young man!” Shylock said.

“The law's full intention and purpose support the penalty that is now due under this bond,” Portia said.

“Quite true. Wise and upright judge! How much older your wisdom is than your face!”

“Then bare your chest.”

“Yes, his chest. That's what the bond says, doesn't it, noble judge? 'Nearest his heart.' Those are the exact words.”

“That’s so. Are there scales here to weigh the flesh?”

“I have them ready,” Shylock said.

“Have a surgeon standing by at your expense, Shylock, to close his wounds so he doesn't bleed to death.”

“Does the bond specify that?”

“Not expressly, but what of it? It would be good to do that much out of charity.”

“I can't find it. It isn't in the bond.”

“Merchant, have you anything to say?” Portia asked.

“Very little. I'm ready and prepared. Give me your hand, Bassanio. Goodbye. Don't grieve that I've come to this for you. Fortune is kinder here than usual. Normally she lets a miserable man outlive his wealth, leaving him with hollow eyes and a wrinkled brow to face an old age of poverty. She cuts me off from that long penance. Give my respects to your honourable wife. Tell her how Antonio ended. Say how I loved you. Speak well of me when I'm dead. When you've told the story, let her judge whether Bassanio once had someone who loved him. Grieve only that you're losing your friend. He doesn't grieve at paying your debt. If the Jew cuts deep enough, I'll pay it at once—with all my heart,” Antonio said.

“Antonio, I'm married to a wife as dear to me as life itself. But neither life, nor my wife, nor the whole world is worth more to me than your life. I'd lose them all—sacrifice them all to this devil here—to save you,” Bassanio said.

“Your wife wouldn't thank you much if she were here to hear that offer,” Portia said.

“I have a wife I swear I love. I wish she were in heaven so she could beg some power there to change this dog of a Jew,” Gratiano said.

“It's just as well you make that offer behind her back. That wish would otherwise make for a troubled household,” Nerissa said.

“These are Christian husbands. I have a daughter. I'd rather she'd married any descendant of Barabbas, that criminal, than a Christian,” Shylock thought.

“We're wasting time. Please proceed with the sentence,” Shylock said.

“A pound of this merchant's flesh is yours. The court awards it. The law gives it,” Portia said.

“Most just judge!”

“And you must cut that flesh from his chest. The law allows it. The court awards it,” Portia said.

“Most learned judge! A sentence! Come, prepare yourself.”

“Wait a moment. There's something else. This bond gives you not one drop of blood. Its exact words are 'a pound of flesh'. Take your bond, then. Take your pound of flesh. But if, while cutting, you spill one drop of Christian blood, your land and property are confiscated by the state of Venice under Venetian law.”

“Upright judge! Listen, Jew. A learned judge!” Gratiano said.

“Is that the law?” Shylock asked.

“You'll see the statute yourself. Since you insist on justice, be certain you'll have it—more than you desire,” Portia said.

“Learned judge! Listen, Jew. A learned judge!” Gratiano said.

“Then I'll take the offer. Pay three times the bond and let the Christian go,” Shylock said.

“Here's the money,” Bassanio said.

“Wait. The Jew shall have complete justice. Wait—no hurry. He shall have nothing but the penalty,” Portia said.

“Jew! An upright judge, a learned judge!” Gratiano said.

“Prepare to cut the flesh. Spill no blood, and cut neither less nor more than exactly one pound. If you take more or less, by even the tiniest amount, even one twentieth of a scruple, a tiny unit of weight—if the scale tips by so much as the weight of a hair—you die, and all your property is confiscated.”

“A second Daniel! A Daniel, Jew! Now, unbeliever, I've got you at a disadvantage.”

“Why does the Jew hesitate? Take your penalty.”

“Give me the money I lent and let me go,” Shylock said.

“I have it ready. Here it is,” Bassanio said.

“He's refused it in open court. He shall have only justice and his bond,” Portia said.

“A Daniel, I still say! A second Daniel! Thank you, Jew, for teaching me that name,” Gratiano said.

“Can't I even have my original loan back?” Shylock asked.

“You shall have nothing but the penalty. Take it at your peril, Jew,” Portia said.

“Then may the devil help him enjoy it. I won't stay for more argument,” Shylock said.

“Wait, Jew. The law has another hold on you. Venetian law says that if someone who is not a citizen is proved to have tried, directly or indirectly, to take a citizen's life, the intended victim shall seize half his property. The other half goes to the state's treasury. The offender's life lies entirely at the Duke's mercy, with no one else's voice able to intervene. That, I say, is your position. Your own actions plainly show that, directly and indirectly, you've plotted against this defendant's very life. You've incurred the penalties I've just described. Kneel, then, and beg the Duke for mercy.”

“Beg for permission to hang yourself. But with your wealth confiscated you can't even afford a rope. The state will have to pay for your hanging,” Gratiano said.

“To show you the difference between our spirits, I pardon your life before you ask. Half your wealth belongs to Antonio. The other half goes to the state, which may reduce its claim to a fine if you show humility,” the Duke said.

“Yes, the state's half. Not Antonio's,” Portia said.

“No. Take my life and everything. Don't pardon that. You take my house when you take the support that holds it up. You take my life when you take the means by which I live,” Shylock said.

“What mercy can you show him, Antonio?”

“A hangman’s rope, free of charge. Nothing else, for God's sake,” Gratiano said.

“If the Duke and court agree to waive the state's claim to half his property, I'm satisfied, provided he lets me hold the other half in use and hand it over at his death to the gentleman who recently stole his daughter. Two further conditions: in return for this favour, he must immediately become a Christian. And he must register a deed here in court leaving everything he owns at his death to his son-in-law Lorenzo and his daughter,” Antonio said.

“He will do it, or I'll withdraw the pardon I've just granted,” the Duke said.

“Do you agree, Jew? What do you say?” Portia asked.

“I agree,” Shylock said.

“Clerk, draw up a deed of gift.”

“Please let me leave. I'm unwell. Send the deed after me, and I'll sign it.”

“Go. But sign it,” the Duke said.

“You'll have two godfathers at your baptism. If I'd been judge, you'd have had ten more—twelve men of a jury to send you to the gallows, not the baptismal font,” Gratiano said.

Shylock left the court. He had asked leave to go because he was unwell. The deed would follow for his signature.

“Sir, please come home with me to dinner,” the Duke said.

“I humbly ask your Grace to excuse me. I must travel towards Padua tonight and need to leave at once,” Portia said.

“I'm sorry you haven't the time. Antonio, reward this gentleman. I think you're deeply bound to him.”

The Duke departed with his attendants. Antonio and Bassanio remained to thank the young doctor.

“Worthy sir, your wisdom has saved my friend and me from terrible penalties today. In return, we freely offer you the three thousand ducats that were due to the Jew, to repay your generous efforts,” Bassanio said.

“And beyond that, we remain forever in your debt in affection and service,” Antonio said.

“Someone who's satisfied is well paid. Saving you satisfies me, so I consider myself well paid. I've never wanted more profit than that. Please recognise me when we meet again. I wish you well. Goodbye,” Portia said.

“Dear sir, I must press you further. Take some keepsake from us as a tribute, not a fee. Please grant me two things: don't refuse me, and forgive my insistence,” Bassanio said.

“You press me hard, so I'll give in.”

Portia turned to Antonio.

“Give me your gloves. I'll wear them for your sake,” Portia said.

Then she addressed Bassanio.

“And as a token of your love, I'll take this ring. Don't draw your hand back. I'll take nothing else. In friendship, you mustn't refuse me,” Portia said.

“This ring, sir—it's a trifle. I'd be ashamed to give you this,” Bassanio said.

“I'll have nothing else. Now I think of it, I've taken a fancy to it.”

“More depends on this ring than its price. I'll give you the most expensive ring in Venice and have a public announcement made to find it. Only excuse me from giving this one.”

“I see you're generous with offers. First you taught me to beg. Now you're teaching me how a beggar should be answered.”

“My wife gave me this ring. When she put it on my finger, she made me swear never to sell it, give it away or lose it,” Bassanio said.

“Many men use that excuse to keep their gifts. Unless your wife is mad, once she knows how well I've earned the ring she won't remain angry forever because you gave it to me. Well, peace be with you.”

Portia and Nerissa left. Bassanio still had his ring when they went.

“Bassanio, let him have the ring. Weigh what he's done for us, and my love as well, against your wife's command,” Antonio said.

“Go, Gratiano. Run and catch him. Give him the ring, and if you can, bring him to Antonio's house. Hurry!” Bassanio said.

Gratiano hurried after the doctor with the ring.

“Come. We'll go there now. Early tomorrow morning we'll both hurry to Belmont. Come, Antonio,” Bassanio said.

Bassanio and Antonio left for Antonio's house. In the morning they would travel to Belmont together.


## 19. The Second Ring

Portia and Nerissa came into the street, still dressed as doctor and clerk.

“Find the Jew's house. Give him this deed to sign. We'll leave tonight and be home a day ahead of our husbands. Lorenzo will be very glad of this document,” Portia said.

Gratiano caught up with them, carrying Bassanio's ring.

“Sir, I'm glad I've caught you. Bassanio has thought again and sent you this ring. He asks you to join him for dinner,” Gratiano said.

“That isn't possible. But I accept his ring with many thanks. Please tell him so. And please show my young clerk the way to Shylock's house,” Portia said.

“I will.”

“Sir, a word with you,” Nerissa said.

“I'll see if I can get my husband's ring—the one I made him swear to keep forever,” Nerissa whispered.

“I'm sure you can. Our husbands will swear themselves hoarse insisting they gave the rings to men. We'll brazen it out and outdo their oaths too,” Portia whispered.

Portia raised her voice to address the others again.

“Go quickly. You know where I'll wait,” Portia said.

“Come, good sir. Will you take me to this house?” Nerissa asked.

Nerissa went with Gratiano to find Shylock's house. Portia left to wait for her, carrying Bassanio's ring.


## 20. On a Night Like This

Moonlight lay along the approach to Portia's house. Lorenzo and Jessica were outside together.

“The moon shines brightly. On a night like this, when the sweet wind gently kissed the trees and they made no sound, I think Troilus went up onto the walls of Troy and sighed his soul towards the Greek tents where his love Cressida lay,” Lorenzo said.

“On a night like this, Thisbe, who had gone to meet her lover Pyramus, hurried fearfully over the dew, saw the lion's shadow before she saw the lion, and ran away in fright,” Jessica said.

“On a night like this, Dido stood on the wild seashore with a willow branch, the sign of a forsaken lover, in her hand, waving for her departing lover to come back to Carthage.”

“On a night like this, Medea gathered the enchanted herbs that made old Aeson young again.”

“On a night like this, Jessica stole from the wealthy Jew and ran with her spendthrift lover from Venice all the way to Belmont.”

“On a night like this, young Lorenzo swore he loved her dearly, stealing her soul with promises of faithfulness—not one of them true,” Jessica said.

“On a night like this, pretty Jessica, like a little shrew, slandered her lover, and he forgave her.”

“I'd outdo you at this 'on a night like this' game if someone weren't coming. Listen. I hear a man's footsteps.”

Stephano came quickly towards them through the quiet.

“Who hurries through the quiet night?” Lorenzo asked.

“A friend,” Stephano said.

“A friend? Which friend? Your name, please, friend,” Lorenzo said.

“Stephano. I bring word that my mistress will be here in Belmont before daybreak. She's wandering among the holy roadside crosses, stopping to kneel and pray for happiness in marriage.”

“Who's with her?”

“Only a holy hermit and her maid. Has my master returned?”

“No, and we've had no word from him. Come inside, Jessica. Let's prepare a proper welcome for the lady of the house.”

Launcelot arrived with considerably less quiet.

“Hey! Hey! Whoa, ho! Hey! Hey!” Launcelot said.

“Who's calling?” Lorenzo asked.

“Hey! Have you seen Master Lorenzo? Master Lorenzo! Hey! Hey!”

“Stop shouting. Here I am.”

“Hey! Where? Where?”

“Here,” Lorenzo said.

“Tell him a courier has come from my master with his messenger’s horn full of good news. My master will be here before morning.”

Launcelot departed after delivering his message.

“Sweetheart, let's go inside and wait for them. No—why go inside? Stephano, please tell the household their mistress is nearly home. And bring the musicians out into the open air,” Lorenzo said.

Stephano went inside to alert the household and bring out the musicians. Lorenzo stayed with Jessica beneath the moon.

“How sweetly the moonlight sleeps on this bank. We'll sit here and let the music steal into our ears. Soft stillness and the night are perfect for the strains of sweet harmony. Sit, Jessica. Look at heaven's floor, thickly inlaid with little plates of shining gold. Every heavenly sphere, even the smallest one you see, sings like an angel as it moves, singing without end to the young-eyed cherubs. Immortal souls hold that same harmony. But while this muddy garment of decaying flesh closes heavily round the soul, we cannot hear it,” Lorenzo said.

The musicians came out into the night air.

“Come, wake Diana, the moon goddess, with a hymn. Let your sweetest playing reach your mistress's ears and draw her home with music,” Lorenzo said.

They began to play. Jessica listened beside Lorenzo.

“Sweet music never makes me merry,” Jessica said.

“That's because your whole spirit is absorbed in it. Watch a wild, unruly herd, or young unbroken horses, bounding madly, bellowing and neighing with the heat of their blood. Let them hear a trumpet or a tune, and they'll stop together, their savage eyes made gentle by music's power. That's why the poet imagined Orpheus could move trees, stones and rivers with his song. Nothing is so wooden, hard or furious that music can't change its nature for a time. A man with no music in himself, unmoved by harmonious sounds, is fit for treason, plots and robbery. His spirit moves as dully as night, his feelings dark as the underworld. Don't trust such a man. Listen to the music,” Lorenzo said.

Portia and Nerissa approached the house. From outside they could see its light and hear its music.

“That light is burning in my hall. How far that little candle throws its beams. So shines a good deed in a wicked world,” Portia said.

“When the moon was shining, we couldn't see the candle,” Nerissa said.

“Greater glory dims the lesser. A deputy shines as brightly as a king until the king arrives. Then his splendour drains away like a little inland stream pouring into the ocean. Listen—music.”

“Your own household musicians, madam,” Nerissa said.

“Nothing is good without regard to its setting, I see. It sounds much sweeter than in daylight.”

“Silence gives it that quality.”

“A crow sings as sweetly as a lark when no one's listening to either. And if the nightingale sang by day, with every goose cackling, we'd think her no better a musician than a wren. How many things need the right moment to bring out their true perfection and deserve their proper praise. Quiet there! The moon sleeps with her beloved Endymion and doesn't want to be woken.”

The music stopped.

“Unless I'm much mistaken, that's Portia's voice,” Lorenzo said.

“He knows me as a blind man knows a cuckoo: by the bad voice,” Portia said.

“Dear lady, welcome home.”

“We've been praying for our husbands' welfare. We hope they're better for our prayers. Have they returned?”

“Not yet, madam. But a messenger has come ahead to announce their arrival.”

“Go inside, Nerissa. Tell my servants not to mention that we've been away. And don't you mention it, Lorenzo. You either, Jessica.”

A trumpet sounded nearby.

“Your husband's near. I hear his trumpet. We won't tell tales, madam. Don't worry,” Lorenzo said.

“This night seems like daylight fallen ill, just a little paler. A day with the sun hidden,” Portia said.

Bassanio arrived with Antonio, Gratiano and their followers. Portia had reached home before them.

“You're so bright we'd have daylight at the same time as people on the other side of the world if you'd walk abroad when the sun is absent,” Bassanio said.

“Let me give light, but never be light and unfaithful. A light wife makes a heavy-hearted husband, and may Bassanio never be that because of me. God arrange everything for the best. Welcome home, my lord,” Portia said.

“Thank you, madam. Welcome my friend. This is Antonio, the man to whom I'm bound beyond measure.”

“You should be bound to him in every sense. I hear he was very much bound for you,” Portia said.

“No more than I've now been happily released from,” Antonio said.

“Sir, you're most welcome here. I'll show it in deeds as well as words, so I'll spare you a long speech of courtesy.”

“By that moon, I swear you're wronging me. I gave it to the judge's clerk. I wish whoever got it were castrated, since you take it so much to heart, love,” Gratiano said.

“A quarrel already? What's wrong?”

Gratiano said, “A little hoop of gold. A paltry ring she gave me, with a motto like the verses knife-makers scratch on their blades: 'Love me, and leave me not.'”

“Why talk about the motto or the price? When I gave it to you, you swore you'd wear it until you died and take it to your grave. Even if not for my sake, you should have respected those passionate oaths and kept it. Gave it to a judge's clerk? God be my judge, the clerk who got it will never grow a beard,” Nerissa said.

“He will if he lives to be a man.”

“Yes—if a woman lives to be a man.”

“By this hand, I gave it to a youth. A boy, a little stunted boy no taller than you. The judge's clerk. A talkative boy who begged it as his fee. I couldn't find it in my heart to refuse him.”

“You were wrong, Gratiano. I'll be plain. You shouldn't have parted so lightly with your wife's first gift, a thing fastened to your finger with oaths, riveted to your flesh with faith. I gave my love a ring and made him swear never to part with it. Here he stands. I'd swear on his behalf that he wouldn't give it up or pull it off for all the wealth in the world. Truly, you've given your wife a cruel reason to grieve. If it were me, I'd be furious,” Portia said.

“I'd better cut off my left hand and swear I lost the ring defending it,” Bassanio thought.

“Bassanio gave his ring to the judge who begged for it—and deserved it too. Then his boy, the clerk, who'd done some writing for us, begged for mine. Neither master nor servant would take anything except the two rings,” Gratiano said.

“What ring did you give, my lord? Not the one I gave you, I hope,” Portia said.

“If I could add a lie to a fault, I'd deny it. But you can see the ring isn't on my finger. It's gone,” Bassanio said.

“Your false heart is just as empty of truth. By heaven, I'll never come to your bed until I see the ring,” Portia said.

“And I won't come to yours until I see mine,” Nerissa said.

“Sweet Portia, if you knew to whom I gave the ring, if you knew for whose sake I gave the ring, if you understood what I gave the ring for, and how unwillingly I parted with the ring when nothing would be accepted but the ring, your anger would soften,” Bassanio said.

“If you'd known the virtue of the ring, or half the worth of her who gave the ring, or your own honour's duty to keep the ring, you wouldn't have parted with the ring. What man would be so unreasonable, so lacking in decency, as to insist on something sacred if you'd defended it with any feeling? Nerissa tells me what to believe. I'll stake my life that some woman got the ring,” Portia said.

“No. By my honour and my soul, no woman got it. It was a doctor of law who refused three thousand ducats and asked for the ring. I refused him and let him leave displeased—the very man who'd saved my dear friend's life. What can I say? I had to send it after him. Shame and courtesy pressed me on every side. My honour wouldn't let ingratitude stain it so deeply. Forgive me. By these blessed lights of the night, if you'd been there, I think you'd have begged me to give the worthy doctor the ring.”

“Then keep that doctor away from my house. Since he has the jewel I loved, the thing you swore to keep for me, I'll be as generous as you. I won't deny him anything I possess—not my body, not my husband's bed. I'll certainly know him intimately. Don't spend a night away. Watch me like Argus with his hundred eyes. If you don't, if you leave me alone, then by my honour—which is still mine—I'll take that doctor to bed,” Portia said.

“And I'll take his clerk. Think carefully before you leave me to guard myself,” Nerissa said.

“Go ahead, then. But don't let me catch him. If I do, I'll damage that young clerk's pen—the one between his legs,” Gratiano said.

“I'm the unhappy cause of these quarrels,” Antonio said.

“Don't grieve, sir. You're welcome all the same,” Portia said.

Bassanio said, “Portia, forgive this wrong I was driven to commit. Before all these friends, I swear by your beautiful eyes, where I see myself—”

“Listen to that! He sees himself double in my two eyes, one in each. Swear by your double self, then—you two-faced man. There's a trustworthy oath!”

“But hear me. Forgive this fault. I swear by my soul I'll never break another promise to you.”

“I once lent my body to secure his money. It would have been lost but for the man who got your husband's ring. I'll guarantee him again, with my soul as the penalty, that he'll never knowingly break faith again,” Antonio said.

“Then you shall be his guarantor. Give him this, and tell him to keep it better than the other,” Portia said.

“Here, Bassanio. Swear to keep this ring,” Antonio said.

“Heaven! It's the very one I gave the doctor,” Bassanio said.

“I got it from him. Forgive me, Bassanio. By this ring, the doctor slept with me,” Portia said.

“And forgive me, dear Gratiano. That same little stunted boy, the doctor's clerk, slept with me last night in return for this,” Nerissa said.

“This is like repairing roads in summer when they're good enough already. Have our wives already slept with other men and made fools of us, before we've even deserved it?” Gratiano asked.

“Don't speak so crudely. You're all astonished. Here's a letter from Bellario in Padua. Read it at your leisure. You'll learn that Portia was the doctor and Nerissa the clerk. Lorenzo can confirm I left as soon as you did and have only just returned. I haven't even entered my house. Antonio, welcome. I have better news for you than you expect. Open this letter. You'll find three of your richly loaded ships have arrived safely in harbour, quite unexpectedly. You shall not know the strange accident by which this letter came into my hands,” Portia said.

“I'm speechless,” Antonio said.

“Were you the doctor, and I didn't know you?” Bassanio asked.

“Were you the clerk who's going to sleep with my wife?” Gratiano asked.

“Yes. But a clerk who never means to do it, unless he lives to become a man,” Nerissa said.

“Sweet doctor, you'll share my bed. When I'm away, sleep with my wife,” Bassanio said.

“Dear lady, you've given me my life and my livelihood. Here I read for certain that my ships have reached safe anchorage,” Antonio said.

“Well, Lorenzo! My clerk has some comfort for you too,” Portia said.

“Yes, and I'll give it without a fee. Here, for you and Jessica, is a deed of gift from the rich Jew, leaving you everything he owns when he dies,” Nerissa said.

“Dear ladies, you drop manna, that miraculous food from heaven, into starving people's path,” Lorenzo said.

“It's nearly morning, and I'm sure you still want a full account of what happened. Let's go inside. Put your questions to us there under oath, and we'll answer everything faithfully,” Portia said.

“Agreed. The first question I'll make Nerissa swear to answer is whether she'd rather wait until tomorrow night or go to bed now, with two hours left until daybreak. Even if day had come, I'd wish it dark so I could lie with the doctor's clerk. As long as I live, nothing will worry me more than keeping Nerissa's ring safe,” Gratiano said.

They went inside.


# Reading notes: meaning, context, and what remains uncertain

This is a modern-language adaptation of William Shakespeare's complete five-act play, not a replacement plot or a modern translation copied from another author. The play edition preserves the sequence of speeches. The novel edition uses the same adapted dialogue and adds an external narrator for setting and movement. Those connecting passages are adaptation, not recovered Shakespearean prose. Neither edition claims access to Shakespeare's private intentions. A character's assertion is not automatically the play's verdict.

This revised edition uses an explanatory approach: supported references and compressed arguments are sometimes expanded within a speech. The private editorial ledger records material additions and corrections. Explicit self-corrections have not been invented merely to explain a character's verbal mistake. Contextual notes keep disputed readings separate from the performance.

The story remains in its original Venice and Belmont. A ducat remains a ducat: replacing it with a modern dollar amount would imply a precision we do not have. A bond is a formally binding promise to repay, with a stated penalty for default. The Rialto is the Venetian commercial meeting place. Belmont's marriage test and the courtroom's law should be read as the play's dramatic circumstances, not a guide to modern law.

## Act 1, scene 1 — Antonio's sadness

Antonio rejects his friends' guesses about money and love. The scene does not settle the cause of his sadness. His devotion to Bassanio admits different performances and interpretations. Bassanio openly links courtship, social status and financial recovery; the text does not let us cleanly separate love from money. His second arrow is a proposal to risk another loan in order to recover both. Janus has two faces; Nestor represents ancient wisdom. Jason's quest for the Golden Fleece makes Portia both a person desired and a prize pursued.

## Act 1, scene 2 — Portia's choice

The dead father's legal will controls the living daughter's will, or desire. Modern language cannot preserve every shade of that pun in every sentence. Portia is intelligent and wealthy but constrained in marriage. Her caricatures of nationalities are jokes made by this character, and her final remark about Morocco expresses racial prejudice. It has not been removed or presented as an objective description. The Scottish, English and French suitors' exchange turns blows into loans and guarantees, also evoking political rivalries.

## Act 1, scene 3 — The bond

Shylock distinguishes being a good person from being financially sound. His refusal of pork invokes dietary separation and the Gospel story of demons entering pigs. Jacob's spotted lambs provide his biblical argument for profitable increase; Antonio disputes its relevance to interest. Shylock's private hostility is explicit, while his presentation of the flesh bond as a friendly joke remains dramatically unsettling. Antonio admits his abuse and says he may repeat it. The adaptation preserves nine times the loan as his expected return, not three times.

## Act 2, scene 1 — Morocco

Morocco argues that complexion does not measure courage or worth, then boasts of his courage. His comparison with Hercules and a servant explains the anxiety of a strong man submitting to chance. Portia's statement that he has as good a chance as earlier suitors is carefully qualified; the previous scene tells us how little she liked those men. The oath against pursuing another woman for marriage makes a wrong choice costly. Morocco's brief reply can be attached to the warning or the oath; this edition renders his assent and decision to proceed, without making him spell out which clause he answers.

## Act 2, scene 2 — Launcelot and his father

Launcelot gives both sides of his conscience argument enough rope to become ridiculous. He and Gobbo misuse impressive words: infection for affection, fructify where clarify or explain is wanted, impertinent for pertinent, defect for effect or substance. These mistakes are intentional. His joke about a wise father knowing his child hints at uncertain paternity. His palm-reading arithmetic does not add up; the nonsense is retained. His complaints about starvation conflict with Shylock's later account of his appetite. Neither is silently made the neutral narrator's truth.

## Act 2, scene 3 — Jessica's letter

Jessica calls her house hell and then condemns her own shame at being her father's child. Both statements remain. Her escape, conversion and marriage are linked in her declared plan. Launcelot makes a paternity joke here: he suggests that a Christian may actually have fathered Jessica. That is his provocative remark, not a narrator's discovery about her parentage. The base text has 'did'; the Folger edition instead reads 'do', which supports a forward-looking joke about a Christian winning her. This edition follows the base text's past wording and records the alternative rather than silently mixing them.

## Act 2, scene 4 — Preparing the escape

A masque is a costumed entertainment, and torchbearers help carry it through the streets at night. Jessica can leave dressed as a pageboy under that cover. Lorenzo praises her while disparaging her father's religion. His letter tells him about both the escape and the valuables. The adaptation corrects the legacy source's repeated Salanio label for the brief answering line by assigning the companion's answer to Salarino.

## Act 2, scene 5 — Closing the house

Shylock's dream of moneybags troubles him. Launcelot's portent involving different days, dates and times is deliberate nonsense. 'Reproach' substitutes for 'approach', allowing Shylock to answer the actual insult. Hagar is the biblical servant whose child stands outside the line of inheritance Shylock identifies with. His references to her turn religious genealogy into a servant's insult. The locked house will not prevent the loss he fears.

## Act 2, scene 6 — Jessica leaves

The waiting friends compare pursuit with possession before Lorenzo arrives. Jessica's comments about light mean both visibility and sexual or moral lightness. 'Gentile' and 'gentle' overlap in Gratiano's compliment: he praises her by imagining her outside Jewishness. The gold accompanies the lovers. The scene does not provide a private account of what Jessica will later feel about taking it.

## Act 2, scene 7 — Gold

Morocco reasons that universal desire and Portia's worth point towards the precious metal. The skull contradicts that reasoning. The scroll's warning concerns appearances, not a rule that every valuable or beautiful thing is bad. The gold 'angel' is a coin as well as a figure; his metaphor imagines Portia's portrait as an angel inside gold. Portia's farewell again generalises about complexion.

## Act 2, scene 8 — Reported grief

Shylock's cries are reported and impersonated by hostile men. We are not watching an independently witnessed version of that scene. His 'stones' are jewels, with a possible sexual joke for the boys repeating the word. Salarino's account of Antonio's farewell is also a character's report. The adaptation keeps those frames, rather than moving the reports into an all-knowing narrator's direct account.

## Act 2, scene 9 — Silver

Arragon rejects the crowd and assumes his own merit. The fool's portrait makes that confidence the target of the joke. Portia's remark about offending and judging challenges his right to decide his own case. Rank and actual worth need not agree, even though Arragon believes that insight supports his choice. 'Silvered' can suggest an attractive surface and an appearance of age or wisdom.

## Act 3, scene 1 — Shared humanity and revenge

Shylock's famous questions move from shared bodies and feelings to retaliation. Preserving only the humane opening would change the argument. His opponents turn 'flesh and blood' into a sexual taunt. The news from Tubal makes grief over Jessica and satisfaction at Antonio's losses alternate rapidly. The turquoise ring is not merely another price: it came from Leah before marriage. The text supplies the memory's importance without explaining all of that earlier relationship.

## Act 3, scene 2 — Lead, love, and a letter

Portia's language approaches a declaration while she says it is not love. She knows the correct chest and says teaching Bassanio would break her oath. The song's relationship to his choice is open to interpretation; the novel does not turn it into proven coaching. The singer is left unspecified. Bassanio's attack on appearances includes beauty bought through cosmetics and hair taken from a dead person. His comparison involving an Indian woman reflects the passage's prejudiced hierarchy of appearances; it is not a modern endorsement. Portia's transfer of self, property and authority to Bassanio is spoken by the capable woman we have already met. The ring establishes the later test. Gratiano and Nerissa's 'stake' joke is sexual as well as financial.

## Act 3, scene 3 — Why the Duke cannot simply intervene

Antonio argues that Venice's commercial credibility depends on foreigners being able to rely on its laws. This is his explanation inside the drama. He also gives his own account of why Shylock hates him. Earlier scenes have supplied additional causes. The adaptation preserves both rather than making his account exhaustive.

## Act 3, scene 4 — Portia's disguise

Portia tells Lorenzo she will retire to prayer, then sends Balthasar to Bellario for papers and clothes and explains the male disguise to Nerissa. The order matters. Her claim to Lorenzo is not silently narrated as the journey she actually makes. The servant Balthasar is distinct from the identity she uses in court, despite the shared name. Her young man's performance is a comic account of how masculinity can be imitated.

## Act 3, scene 5 — A fool's theology

Launcelot's logic traps Jessica between her father's religion and hypothetical adultery by her mother. She answers through conversion and marriage; he replies with food prices. The Moor/more joke and Lorenzo's reference to a pregnant Black woman retain their uncomfortable racial and sexual context. 'Cover' allows Launcelot to pretend an order to lay the table means putting on his hat before a superior. Jessica's extravagant praise of Portia is her praise, not the narrator certifying perfection.

## Act 4, scene 1 — Mercy and its aftermath

Portia's speech says mercy is voluntary and places it above the coercive symbols of power. She then grants the bond's literal penalty before using its literal wording against Shylock. The adaptation preserves that sequence. Shylock's slave comparison exposes a form of purchased human control accepted by those judging him. 'Daniel' invokes the biblical model of a wise young judge and is later thrown back at him. The offered repayment changes from twice to three times the debt in the original scene; that inconsistency is retained.

The second legal turn treats Shylock as an alien who has attempted a citizen's life. The Duke pardons him, but later makes that pardon conditional on the imposed terms. Antonio's conditions include immediate Christian conversion and a legal gift to Lorenzo and Jessica. This is coercion in the action, whatever meaning a performance gives to the speakers' word 'mercy'. The technical effect of holding half the property 'in use' has interpretive complications; the adaptation keeps the term and the stated destination at Shylock's death rather than inventing a modern trust instrument. The other half's state claim is to be waived on the proposed conditions. Human listening and moral interpretation remain the reader's, not something an automated quality check can certify.

## Act 4, scene 2 — The rings

Portia and Nerissa arrange to return before their husbands. Bassanio's gratitude to the apparent doctor, and Antonio's intervention, have overcome the promise he made to his wife. Nerissa intends to obtain Gratiano's ring too. No second court case or additional adventure has been invented between scenes.

## Act 5, scene 1 — The night and the return

The lovers' opening names come from stories with danger, betrayal or sorrow around love: Troilus and Cressida, Thisbe and Pyramus, Dido and Aeneas, Medea and Jason. Aeson, whom Medea rejuvenates, is Jason's father. A willow can signify forsaken love. Their exchanges are more complicated than a list of perfect romances, but their own teasing does not establish that their marriage will end like those stories.

Lorenzo describes the old idea that heavenly spheres make music, and invokes Orpheus's music moving nature. His claim about unmusical people is his rhetoric. Jessica's response to music remains her own brief statement. Argus is the watcher with a hundred eyes. Portia's claim that the doctor slept with her is a technically playful truth about her disguise, not an added affair. The source does not explain how she obtained the letter about the three surviving ships; the adaptation keeps that unexplained arrival. Gratiano's final 'ring' also carries a sexual double meaning. The play ends with it, not with an invented reconciliation speech from Shylock or Jessica.

## Textual checks for the revised edition

Gratiano's remark about paying for the world with worry retains the source's compressed metaphor: the thing lost may be life, enjoyment of the world, or worldly advantage. It has not been reduced to a single psychological explanation. His marriage-market joke keeps its language of buying and selling without supplying an age or a reason for the woman's supposed unmarketability.

Launcelot reverses ribs and fingers deliberately; the revision makes his thinness clear without correcting that nonsense. His palmistry speech associates the opened hand with oath-taking on a Bible. Its syntax is troublesome, and the feather-bed danger can carry a sexual joke without identifying a specific accident. See the [1923 Yale edition's notes](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Merchant_of_Venice_%281923%29_Yale/Notes) and [Shakespeare Navigators, Act 2 scene 2](https://shakespeare-navigators.ewu.edu/merchant/MerchantText22.html).

For the paternity joke and its alternative wording, compare [Shakespeare Navigators, Act 2 scene 3](https://shakespeare-navigators.ewu.edu/merchant/MerchantText23.html) with [Folger's text](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/the-merchant-of-venice/read/2/3/). These are textual alternatives, not new facts about Jessica.

The recurring **gentle/Gentile** joke links social gentleness to religious exclusion. Both senses are retained where audible wordplay carries the line; neither is the narrator's judgement. See [Folger's language discussion](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/the-merchant-of-venice/reading-shakespeares-language-the-merchant-of-venice/). The silver scroll's ‘silvered over’ image is retained without deciding that it means grey hair, a coating, or a mirror; its fool's-head taunt is made explicit.

Jessica's ‘if on earth he do not mean it’ is textually difficult. This edition takes it as intending the upright life she has just urged. Other editors understand appreciation or moderation, or emend the word to ‘merit’. The adaptation no longer silently uses that last reading as ‘earn’. Compare the [public-domain annotated edition's discussion](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/51/Shakespeare%27s_The_merchant_of_Venice%3B_%28IA_shakespearesmerc04shak%29.pdf).

Bassanio’s fear about ‘enjoying’ his love can concern attaining love or the experience itself; the revised wording avoids an absolute claim about never possessing Portia. His praise of lead keeps the base text’s ‘paleness’; ‘plainness’ is an alternative editorial reading.

Bassanio’s scarf-and-Indian-beauty comparison belongs to his argument about attractive surfaces concealing what he disparages. The revision retains the scarf and its veiling, with the possible disparaging irony explained here rather than made an explicit claim in his speech. The song’s ‘fancy’ is rendered as infatuation, love bred through looking; the edition does not assign the song to Portia or declare it a deliberate hint.

The scarf comparison is compressed and disputed: the literal words call the figure an “Indian beauty”, while the surrounding examples condemn deceptive ornament. The ironic, disparaging interpretation is documented in the Yale notes, but a literal positive reading remains possible. The modern speech retains the image rather than settling that dispute. Neither reading proves Shakespeare’s personal judgement. The song’s cradle can also suggest infatuation dying young, alongside the eyes where it is born.

Two further obvious transcription problems in the legacy base text are repaired: Shylock’s “in luck” amid his lament is read as “ill luck”, and Launcelot’s “I pray tree” as “I pray thee”. The repairs agree with Folger’s text and the immediate sense.
