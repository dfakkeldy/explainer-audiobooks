## Chapter 1 - The Ledger

Seven o'clock in the morning, July 1, 2026. The little background job that exports your notes to the Mac — the one that runs before you leave for the route — crashed. The error it left behind reads, in part, "resource deadlock avoided," which, as a title for that particular morning, is hard to beat. The daily briefing was pointing at the wrong vault. The launch-tracker export came back empty. The note pipeline, when you finally checked, had been quietly stale since June 9 — three weeks of silence, and nothing had told you. And underneath all of it sat the launch tracker itself, holding the honest arithmetic: roughly three hundred and eleven hours of launch work remaining across five apps, eighty-one tasks already overdue, and all five apps then scheduled to launch in one pileup at the turn of July into August. That was the situation the plan you wrote later that same day was built to replace.

On a morning like that, there is a familiar move available. Someone — a friend, a video, a book like this one — puts a hand on your shoulder and says: believe in yourself. Look how far you've come.

This book is not going to do that. Not because the encouragement would be false, but because you, specifically, would not accept it — and you'd be right not to. You are the kind of person who checks. You will play this chapter on a bad day, in the truck, and then you will go home and run the numbers yourself. So this book makes a different offer. Instead of a pep talk: a ledger. Everything that exists as of July 2, 2026, dated and sourced, zeros included. If optimism shows up, it will have to arrive as a conclusion, not as an opening assumption.

That happens to be the rule your own tooling already enforces. Every one of your app repositories keeps a devlog — a public build diary, regenerated from the real commit history. Every automated devlog pull request carries a human review checklist that has to be ticked before anything merges, and one line on that checklist reads, exactly: "No revenue, download count, user count, launch date, or platform-account claim was invented." Another line: "I am intentionally choosing what to post manually; nothing here auto-posts to social media." Even the AI that drafts each weekly summary is fenced in writing — it may use only the factual digest of that week's commits, it may not invent release dates, user counts, or testimonials, and if the week was mostly maintenance, the instruction tells it to say that plainly. The devlog states its own purpose in two lines worth keeping. One: "It's all open source. You can audit every claim below." The other: the point of publishing isn't bragging rights — it's that "you should be able to see exactly what you're trusting."

And that ledger has already corrected itself once, which is how you know it's a real one. On June 19, 2026, the devlog's headline commit count got fixed: a squashed local clone had produced an undercount — "six hundred plus" where the truth was higher — and the correcting commit says it flatly: set the devlog commit total to nine hundred and fifty-six, the authoritative GitHub count. The per-week numbers were re-summed until they matched the total. Notice the direction of that error. The mistake was modesty. This book runs under the same checklist: every number in it carries a date and a source, and where the honest entry is zero, the entry says zero.

One more line for context, from your own marketing notes dated July 1, 2026, describing the story the way you'd tell it to a stranger: "a 47-year-old rural mail carrier taught himself iOS with AI agents in about ninety days." That's the claim. Now open the columns and see whether the ledger carries it.

Start with the asset side: five real applications exist, and each one has a first commit with a date stamped on it. Git — the version-control system underneath all of this — timestamps every change, so beginnings can't be backdated.

Echo, first commit April 19, 2026 — the opening entry of the whole ledger. Echo is the flagship: an open-source audiobook study player. It aligns an audiobook you own with its ebook, so the text follows along word by word while you listen; it can narrate an ebook aloud on-device; and it turns your listening into spaced-repetition flashcards. Its tagline: "For Every Mind — turn listening into learning." What stands out about that first commit is the subject line, which already reads like a working thing: an initial commit describing a stable audiobook player, with speed control at one and a quarter times, and chapter looping. Day one, it played books.

Turn Timer, first commit May 17, 2026. The README calls it "a visual sequence timer for turns, routines, and real-world countdowns" — a colored circle that drains clockwise, so you see how much time is left instead of reading it off digits. Ledger honesty: the repository is still named VisualTimer, because the app is mid-rebrand. It was Visual Timer at birth, became "Visual Game Timer" two days later, and became Turn Timer in a rebrand merged on June 27, 2026. Even the name has a paper trail.

NS Marks the Spot, first commit May 18, 2026 — one day after the timer. An iOS map app that overlays georeferenced historical maps of Nova Scotia on the modern map, with a transparency slider you drag to fade the nineteenth century in and out over the roads you actually drive. The historical layers are chiefly the Hugh Fletcher maps — geological and topographic surveys of Nova Scotia, primarily published in the late eighteen hundreds — with scans that come from the David Rumsey collection. MacroMark, first commit June 2, 2026, with the core features implemented that same day. The README describes a zero-friction, voice-to-text capture tool for personal knowledge management: you dictate into your Apple Watch, and the words land as plain-text Markdown files in your notes on the iPhone, synced through iCloud Drive. Everything you capture in a day appends to a single daily note, and a feature called Verbal Macros turns spoken triggers into formatting — say "Heading One" out loud, and the file gets a heading mark.

And Routey, first commit June 22, 2026 — the newest, and the one closest to home. The repo description: an offline-first iOS app for rural mail carriers — sort, snap, deliver. The tagline: "Rural mail carrier logistics — built from the truck, not the boardroom." Its first commit was no placeholder, either: the version one point oh foundation landed as thirty-three files and roughly seventy-six hundred lines of code in a single push.

For that question, there is different evidence, and it points the other way.

Start with the largest dataset anyone has ever aimed at it. Pierre Azoulay, an economist at MIT, together with Benjamin Jones of Northwestern's Kellogg School and their co-authors J. Daniel Kim and Javier Miranda, published a study in 2020 in a journal called American Economic Review: Insights, under the title "Age and High-Growth Entrepreneurship." They did not survey a few hundred founders and extrapolate. They used United States Census Bureau administrative data — the government's own records — covering two point seven million American company founders whose firms hired at least one employee, between 2007 and 2014. Effectively everyone who started a real, employing company in America across those eight years.

The mythology says founders are kids in hoodies. The data says: the average age of a founder, across all two point seven million, was forty-one point nine. Not twenty-five. Nearly forty-two. Then it gets better for you, not worse. Narrow the frame to the fastest-growing one-in-a-thousand startups — the top zero point one percent — and the average founder age rises to forty-five. Narrow it again, to founders whose companies reached a successful exit, an acquisition or a public offering, and the average age is forty-six point seven. Sit with that for a second. The average age at the moment the whole industry treats as the finish line is forty-six point seven. You are forty-seven. On this axis — the one that measures building things rather than getting hired to build them — you are not late. You are the average, give or take a few months.

The study puts it in odds as well. A fifty-year-old founder is about one point eight times more likely than a thirty-year-old to found one of those top-growth companies. A forty-year-old is about one point three times as likely as a twenty-five-year-old. And there is one more finding — hold onto it, because it comes back near the end of this chapter: founders with three or more years of experience in their specific industry were about twice as likely to found a one-in-a-thousand fastest-growing company. Experience in the domain, not youth, was the multiplier.

One honest caveat, then one supporting case. The caveat: this study measures company founders with employees — venture-scale firms, not one-person App Store shops — so carry it as evidence about age and building, not as a forecast for indie apps. The case: David Duffield founded PeopleSoft, the enterprise human-resources software company, in 1987 at age forty-six, and co-founded Workday in 2005 at sixty-four. He carries his own caveat — Duffield was a lifelong enterprise-software professional, not a career changer — so he is evidence that founding software companies past forty-five, and past sixty, can succeed at the largest scale. He is not evidence about learning to code late. For that question — whether the skill itself can be built and held at your age — there is a separate pile.

In 2013, two researchers at North Carolina State University, Patrick Morrison and Emerson Murphy-Hill, asked the age question directly using Stack Overflow — the question-and-answer site where working programmers earn reputation points when their peers judge their answers useful. They presented the work in May 2013 at a conference called Mining Software Repositories, a research field that studies the trails programmers leave behind in public. Their dataset: over eighty thousand Stack Overflow profiles that included age. Their finding, in North Carolina State's own words: "Programmer reputation scores increase relative to age well into the 50's." Not plateau. Increase. They also traced breadth: the range of topics a programmer can answer narrows from the mid-teens to about age thirty, then widens again through the thirties into the early fifties. And one detail lands squarely on your desk. On the platforms that were newest at the time — iOS and Windows Phone seven — programmers aged thirty-seven and up showed significant advantages. On the new platform, the older programmers knew more. Murphy-Hill's summary of the whole exercise: "The data doesn't support the bias against older programmers — if anything, just the opposite."

Then the adoption question: do people actually start this late, or only in inspirational posters? Philip Guo, a professor at UC San Diego, ran what is described as the first known study of older adults learning programming, presented in 2017 at CHI, the major human-computer-interaction conference, where it took an Honorable Mention award. Be precise about what it is: a survey of five hundred and four people aged sixty to eighty-five, from fifty-two countries, drawn from users of Python Tutor, Guo's free tool for visualizing code as it runs. It measured motivations and frustrations, not ability — so don't lean on it as proof that older learners perform well. Lean on it for what it actually shows: people thirteen to nearly forty years older than you are learning to program, in numbers, across fifty-two countries, for reasons Guo catalogued — keeping the brain challenged while aging, making up for missed opportunities in youth, connecting with younger family members, improving job prospects. At forty-seven, you are not knocking on a door nobody your age has opened. You are younger than every single person in that study.

## Chapter 4 - No Degree, No Permission

## Chapter 5 - The Tool That Looks Like a Rival

In October 2024, on a quarterly earnings call, Sundar Pichai — Google's chief executive — told investors that more than twenty-five percent of all new code at Google was now generated by AI, then reviewed and accepted by engineers. Six months after that, in April 2025, Satya Nadella — Microsoft's chief executive — said that twenty to thirty percent of the code inside Microsoft's repositories was, in his phrase, "written by software," with more progress in Python and less in C plus plus. Those are the two largest software employers on the planet, stating on the record that machines now write a meaningful share of their code.

And on April 19, 2026 — a year and a half after that earnings call — a rural mail carrier pushed his first commits to a brand-new repository called Echo. That was you. Your own devlog states the starting position without blinking: "written by a mail carrier with no prior Swift experience, in the hours around a full-time delivery route." And the complete inventory of your prior programming life, in your own written words: "some Python scripts for GIS and a Visual Basic call logger in high school." GIS being mapping software; the call logger being a high-school project.

So let's say the fear out loud, in its strongest form, because it has earned that. Why would anyone apprentice into a trade at the precise moment machines learn to do it? This is not a fringe worry, and it is not hypothetical. Google's long-running DORA research program put AI adoption among surveyed software professionals at ninety percent in its 2025 report, with a median of two hours a day spent working with the tools. The Stack Overflow Developer Survey of 2025 — the big annual survey of working developers — found eighty-four percent of respondents using or planning to use AI in their development process, and fifty-one percent of professional developers using it every day.

Here is the strong form of the argument. Markets do not pay for what is abundant. A senior engineer sells judgment — architecture, tradeoffs, the scar tissue from systems that failed under them. A beginner sells one thing: the ability to produce ordinary, working code. If a machine now produces ordinary, working code on demand, the beginner is the first person priced out. Not the veterans — the newcomers. And on April 19, 2026, you were about as new as a newcomer gets. If that argument holds, this whole project is a man perfecting his hand-lettering the year the printing press arrives.

That version of the fear deserves an answer made of measurements, not reassurance. Fortunately, people have measured.

Start with the strongest evidence for the tool. In 2023, a controlled experiment led by Sida Peng — with his colleagues Eirini Kalliamvakou, Peter Cihon, and Mert Demirer — was published on the arXiv preprint server, the public shelf where researchers post papers. The design was clean: recruit software developers, give every one of them the same task — build an HTTP server, a small web server, in JavaScript, as fast as they can — and give half of them GitHub Copilot, GitHub's AI coding assistant. The group with the assistant finished fifty-five point eight percent faster. In GitHub's own write-up of the study, the averages were one hour and eleven minutes with the tool against two hours and forty-one minutes without, with a p-value of point zero zero one seven — comfortably past the line of statistical significance. The honest uncertainty band was wide, running from twenty-one percent faster to eighty-nine percent faster, but notice that the entire band sits on the side of faster. And there is a quieter number inside the study: seventy-eight percent of the assisted group completed the task at all, against seventy percent of the control group. The tool did not just speed people up. It carried some people over the finish line who otherwise would not have arrived. The paper's abstract closes with a sentence that reads like it was mailed to your address: "Observed heterogenous effects show promise for AI pair programmers to help people transition into software development careers." The authors looked at how the gains spread across their subjects and read the pattern as promising for career-changers. That one sentence is the entire supported claim — no subgroup percentages exist in the record I'm working from — and it still says what it says.

Now the strongest evidence against, dealt face-up. In July 2025, the research group METR published a randomized controlled trial with a very different shape: sixteen experienced open-source developers, two hundred and forty-six real tasks, working inside mature repositories where they each averaged five years of prior experience. The AI condition mostly used Cursor Pro, an AI-powered code editor, running the Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet models. The result: allowing AI made these developers nineteen percent slower. And there was a twist worth sitting with. Before the study, the developers forecast that AI would cut their completion time by twenty-four percent. Afterward, they believed it had cut their time by twenty percent — when in reality it had added nineteen. The tool slowed them down while convincing them it had sped them up. That is the sharpest card the fear holds, and it is a real card.

The middle of the evidence is muddier, and worth hearing too. DORA's 2024 Accelerate State of DevOps report found that a twenty-five percent increase in AI adoption was associated with an estimated one point five percent decrease in delivery throughput and a seven point two percent decrease in delivery stability — associations tied to that increase in adoption, not absolute year-over-year declines. The same increase was associated with a seven point five percent improvement in documentation quality, and about three-quarters of respondents reported productivity gains. The report's authors offered a hypothesis for the dip: AI makes it easier to write more code, and bigger batches of change carry more risk. A year later, DORA's 2025 State of AI-assisted Software Development report — built on a survey of nearly five thousand technology professionals plus more than a hundred hours of interviews — put adoption at ninety percent, which Google described as "a fourteen percent increase over the prior year," with over eighty percent of respondents saying AI had increased their productivity; the top use case was writing new code, at seventy-one percent. Meanwhile, Stack Overflow's 2025 survey caught trust moving the other way: forty-six percent of developers said they don't trust the accuracy of AI output, up from thirty-one percent the year before, and positive sentiment had slipped from above seventy percent in 2023 and 2024 down to sixty. The single biggest frustration, cited by sixty-six percent of respondents, was — their words — "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite." Forty-five percent said debugging AI-generated code takes more time.

So one rigorous study says fifty-five point eight percent faster, another says nineteen percent slower, and neither is lying. Look at where each one was standing. Peng's subjects built a fresh web server from a standing start — no history, no house style, no sprawling old codebase to hold in their heads. Greenfield work. METR's subjects were the opposite case, and the study itself is careful about its own scope: experienced maintainers on codebases they knew deeply — the setting most unlike a newcomer building greenfield apps. When a developer already carries five years of one repository's context in their skull, the machine has little to add and plenty to disturb; every suggestion it makes has to compete with knowledge it does not have. When a developer is starting from zero on something new, the machine supplies exactly what is missing — the syntax, the idiom, the boilerplate, the thousand small conventions that take a beginner months to absorb. DORA's 2025 report frames it — and this is the report's framing in essence, not a verbatim quote — as AI being an amplifier: it strengthens teams that already work well and amplifies the existing problems of teams that don't. And remember the completion rates in the Peng experiment — seventy-eight percent versus seventy. More people finished at all.

Put the two studies side by side and the honest pattern is not "AI helps" or "AI hurts." It is this: the tool compresses the distance from beginner to competent the most, and it helps least — sometimes negatively — where deep, embedded, particular context already exists. Which means the fear has the geometry backwards. The fear pictures the machine as your competitor. The measurements picture the machine as aimed at your exact profile: no prior Swift, greenfield apps, hours squeezed around a mail route. The carrier holding the tool is the beneficiary. You did not enter the trade as the machines were taking it. You entered the one trade where the machine hands its biggest advantage to the newest person in the room.

That is the theory. Your own repositories are the demonstration, and every piece of it carries a date.

## Chapter 6 - Two Million Spines

There is a bookshelf you have already walked down, because you built the tour yourself. It sits in the first chapter of Findable, the App Store Optimization audiobook you generated with your own pipeline and published in your public repo in June of twenty twenty-six — eight chapters, about three hours by the book's own estimate, thirty-three thousand five hundred and seventy-three words, written by Claude and committed on June 18, 2026. The chapter is called "The Shelf With a Million Books," and it opens like this. "Picture a bookshelf the size of a city. Not a library. A shelf. It runs for block after block in every direction, taller than you can see, and every inch of it is packed with spines pressed shoulder to shoulder. There are around two million of them. There are no signs hanging from the ceiling telling you where to go. There is no front desk. There is just the shelf, going on and on, and you, standing somewhere in the middle of it, holding the one thing you made." And then the turn of the knife: "That shelf is the App Store. And the thing you made is one of those two million spines."

One honesty note before we lean any weight on that picture, because this book promised to carry its receipts. "Around two million" is Findable's own round number. Findable, like every book in your library, is written by AI and, by its own public disclosure, spot-checked rather than expert-reviewed. No independently audited count of the App Store sits anywhere in the research behind this chapter. So the image belongs to the book, not to an auditor. Take the two million as a shape, not a census. The shape, though, is not in dispute. The shelf is enormous, it is crowded, and it is getting more crowded, because the tools that helped you build five apps in a spring are helping everyone else build apps too.

So let the fear stand up and speak first, at full height, because you asked for this book on a day when it was speaking loudly. It goes like this. The store is flooded. Whatever you ship arrives as one spine among a couple of million, indistinguishable at arm's length from every other spine, and the flood is rising faster than ever now that anyone with a chat window can generate an app in a weekend. Your own book says the quiet part plainly: "The gap between 'I made something people would love' and 'people who would love it can find it' is wide, and it is quiet, and a lot of good apps fall into it and are never heard from again." That is not a critic talking. That is the book you made, warning you about the shelf you are about to climb onto. So concede it, first, before earning the right to answer: the store is flooded. That part of the fear is simply true. The question this chapter actually has to answer is narrower and more useful — flooded with what, and does the flood touch what you hold?

Start with the honest distribution, named and dated, because "most apps fail" is a mood until someone counts. The best public count comes from RevenueCat, the subscription-infrastructure company — its code sits inside tens of thousands of apps and processes their subscription revenue, which means it sees real money, not survey answers. Its twenty twenty-five State of Subscription Apps report draws on more than seventy-five thousand subscription apps and over ten billion dollars in tracked revenue. Two findings matter here, and both deserve to be spoken slowly. First: nearly twenty percent of subscription apps reach a thousand dollars in monthly revenue within their first two years, and about five percent reach the ten-thousand-dollar mark. Flip that around and it means roughly four out of five never see a thousand dollars a month in that window. Second, and this is the one that has to be quoted in its exact shape, because the source does not fully specify the metric and paraphrasing it would smuggle in precision that is not there: at eight thousand eight hundred and eighty dollars, the top five percent of newly launched apps make over four hundred times as much money after their first year, compared to the bottom twenty-five percent, who make no more than nineteen dollars. Not nineteen dollars a month. Nineteen dollars, after their first year, for the bottom quarter of newly launched apps. Sit with that number, because it is the floor of this business and you will meet people standing on it.

And the concentration is getting worse, not better. The same top-to-bottom gap was roughly two hundred times in the prior year's report; a year later it had doubled to four hundred. The report also puts the median realized revenue per install at day sixty at thirty-one cents — one download is worth, at the middle of the distribution, about the price of a gumball. Even the good categories are only relatively good: photo and video apps reach that thousand-a-month bar about twenty-one point four percent of the time, gaming twenty percent, business apps fourteen point seven. One caveat, spoken once: RevenueCat's data covers only apps that use RevenueCat's own toolkit, so the sample skews toward subscription apps and independent developers — not the whole store. If anything, that makes it more relevant to you, because that sample is your neighborhood. So say the finding plainly, the way the book's contract requires: most apps earn almost nothing. The bookshelf the size of a city is real, and most of the spines on it never earn back a tank of gas.

Now the narrower question: flooded with what? Your own Master Plan — written and ratified on July 1, twenty twenty-six, the day before this book was asked for — faces the AI-flood fear directly, and its answer is the spine of this chapter. Quote: "partly true — AI floods the store. But the flood is commodity apps from people with no story, no niche authority, and no trust. What gets scarcer as the flood rises is exactly what you hold: a true story, domain authenticity, accessibility depth, no-subscription, local-first positioning against enshittification, and a public honesty ledger." And the plan's closing line, in its own words: harder for the median entrant does not mean harder for you. One honesty check on that passage, because it contains a boast the ledger cannot fully back: the plan claims that nobody else delivering mail is building Routey, and that is the plan's line about story ownership, not an established market fact — a direct competitor called Carrier Route Pilot shipped in May twenty twenty-six and, as of July 1, had zero ratings. So the field is not empty. It is nearly empty, and the one other entrant has yet to collect a single rating, but the claim belongs to the plan, and the correction belongs here.

Findable makes the same argument from the other direction, about what drowns. Its words: "A slightly-better clone of a beloved app is the saddest thing in the whole store: it competes head-on with something it cannot beat. A genuinely different app, by contrast, competes with nobody, because there's nobody else like it on the shelf." He cannot out-shout the giants, the book says of its worked-example developer — meaning you — but he can out-specific them. Out-specific, never outspend. And here is what out-specific looks like on your own shelf. The fear also assumes that small developers, as a class, are being ground under — so look at the best available data on that class, provenance first, because the provenance matters. Apple claims the program benefits the vast majority of developers selling digital goods on the store, and whatever you make of the marketing, your own July 1 planning already computes every app's proceeds at the fifteen percent rate. ## Chapter 7 - The Small-Money Years

Start with the number as it stands this morning. It is July 2, 2026, and your app revenue to date is zero dollars. Not small. Zero. Five apps in various stages of nearly-done, and — in the words of your own Master Plan, written and ratified yesterday, July 1 — "What you don't have is a single shipped app." You asked for this book on a day when the honest revenue math felt like not much hope, so this chapter will not begin by arguing with you. So the fear, steel-manned: most apps earn almost nothing, the winners are pulling further away, and you have zero apps on the board. All true. Now the question underneath the fear, because the plan answers it directly. ## Chapter 8 - The Wind at Your Back

There are two kinds of days on the route, and the odometer can't tell them apart. Same kilometres, same boxes, same reach out the same window a few hundred times. But on one of those days a northeast wind is coming in hard and every stop is a small fight — the door, the flag, the mail itself trying to leave your hand — and on the other the air is still and the whole day just gives a little. Nobody knows this better than you: effort is not the only variable. Weather matters. And for several chapters now, this book has mostly been checking the weather against you — the age, the missing degree, the tool that looks like a rival, the two million spines already on the shelf. Fair enough. That was the deal. But an honest ledger has two columns, and this chapter is the other one: the winds that are actually at your back.

Here's the rule before a single wind gets named, because on a bad day you won't trust anything in this chapter without it. Every tailwind in the next half hour can be named, dated, and checked. Anything that couldn't be was cut — and the cutting genuinely happened. It sits in the research file behind this chapter, dated July 2, 2026, which splits its findings into supported facts on one side and a list marked not supported, do not state as fact, on the other. You should hear what died on that list, because a chapter that only parades the survivors is doing exactly the thing this book promised not to do.

So, the cuts. The Canada Digital Adoption Program — CDAP, the big federal grant that handed small businesses fifteen thousand dollars and access to an interest-free loan of up to a hundred thousand through the Business Development Bank of Canada — closed to new applications on February 19, 2024, fully subscribed, even though it had been expected to run into 2025. It would have been the fattest wind in this chapter. It's gone. A grant aggregator called GrantCompass, checked as of March 2026, could find no successor in either of the last two federal budgets — which this book records as "no successor found," not as any government's statement that none is coming. Also cut: a provincial digital adoption grant from Nova Scotia Business Inc. that turned out, on inspection, to be a pandemic program from June 2020, keyed to how badly your April 2020 revenue had collapsed, with no evidence it exists in 2026. Cut: the tidy claim that Nova Scotia businesses "must make their apps and websites accessible by 2030" — the province's accessibility law is real, but its digital standard is not yet in force, and 2030 is a framework goal the province itself has admitted it will partly miss. Cut: a dramatic story about the first European accessibility lawsuits landing in France in late 2025, because the only place it appeared was vendor marketing. And one number got corrected on the way in: a training subsidy that an aggregator claimed covered one hundred percent of costs actually covers up to seventy-five percent, per the province's own program page. Seventy-five is the number you'll hear.

That's the method showing its work. What survives it is smaller than the brochure version. It's also real, and every piece of it is yours to check.

The first wind lives inside the store itself. The App Store Small Business Program — Apple's reduced-commission tier, announced in November 2020 and running since January 1, 2021 — cuts Apple's cut from thirty percent to fifteen for any developer who earned up to a million US dollars in proceeds across all their apps in the previous calendar year. The second wind is stranger, because it looks like a virtue and is actually a market. You've been doing accessibility work in Echo since the beginning — and it turns out the demand side of that skill is now written into law on two continents, with dates attached.

Start in Europe. The European Accessibility Act — the EU directive from 2019 that makes accessibility mandatory for covered digital products and services — became enforceable on June 28, 2025. A company doesn't escape it by being in Toronto or Texas. Now the caveat, said once and cleanly: there's a microenterprise exemption — businesses with fewer than ten people and no more than two million euros in turnover are exempt from the services obligations, which means your own solo app shop is itself exempt.
