# Original-language translation

Read this when the requested base is Greek, Latin, or another language rather than
an existing English text.

## Evidence before fluent English

Identify an accessible original-language edition and its licensing, editorial
provenance, and numbering. For ancient works, say “the Greek text of [edition]”
rather than implying access to an author's surviving manuscript. Keep the original
passage beside its working English and record the source IDs. Preserve diacritics
and source spelling in the research copy even if the reading edition simplifies names.

Work from the source language. For uncertain phrases, examine morphology, syntax,
word senses in context, and nearby narrative usage using authoritative lexica,
grammatical resources, or scholarly commentary. Verify these resources when using
them and cite the exact passage or entry in the working notes. Familiar English
word associations are not evidence of an ancient word's meaning.

Consult independently produced, appropriately licensed translations as cross-checks
for difficult passages, not as an undisclosed substitute for reading the source.
Agreement does not settle a disputed reading. If competent source-language analysis
or suitable resources are unavailable, disclose that limit and label the result an
adaptation of its actual base. Do not manufacture a direct-translation claim.

For a material ambiguity, log the source wording, plausible readings, supporting
references, chosen rendering, and what is lost. Preserve uncertainty in the English
where practical; put alternatives in notes when the narrative needs one reading.
Do not annotate every straightforward word merely to create an appearance of rigor.

## Homer: Iliad and Odyssey

Treat the two poems as separate books and projects unless the user requests a
combined edition. Preserve each poem's own sequence rather than filling it out
with episodes familiar from later retellings of the Trojan cycle. Keep distinctions
between narrator statements, character claims, similes, divine action, and prophecy.

Track recurring epithets, formulas, appeals, genealogies, and hospitality or exchange
scenes. Their recurrence can carry structural and cultural meaning. Do not delete
them as redundant or vary every repeated phrase for superficial freshness. An
English epithet should be intelligible in its passage; establish a reason before
changing a recurring rendering.

Maintain a context-sensitive term ledger for concepts of anger, honor, fame,
homecoming, fate, obligation, and relations between guests and hosts. A modern
one-word equivalent may mislead, and the same Greek word need not receive the same
English word everywhere. Preserve consequential distinctions with readable phrases
or short notes. Avoid treating ancient institutions as exact equivalents of modern
law, religion, psychology, or politics.

Use a consistent naming convention and explain notable alternate names once.
Translate long speeches with their reasoning intact. Preserve extended similes and
the shifts between ordinary life, battle, and divine scenes. Aim for audible rhythm
without claiming to reproduce Greek meter through English prose.

A useful delivery description is: “A modern English rendering based on [identified
Greek edition], with contextual notes and recorded interpretive choices.” State
whether specialist review has actually occurred and which passages remain uncertain.
