# Voice Design (nonfiction)

An optional tool for finding the book's voice before drafting. Aim at one
specific voice rather than steering away from a list of things to avoid: a
writer holding forty prohibitions writes defensively, and defensive writing is
flat.

## Control panel

Record one bounded choice per control. "Middle" is a real answer, but a panel
where every control is middle produces the house style nobody remembers.

| Control | Useful range |
|---|---|
| Narrator stance | invisible explainer ↔ implicated participant |
| Sentence movement | clipped, declarative ↔ elastic, subordinating |
| Diction | plain ↔ idiomatic ↔ technical ↔ formal |
| Evidence handling | cited in the sentence ↔ absorbed into narrative |
| Concession | limits stated outright ↔ limits implied by precision |
| Humour | absent ↔ dry ↔ wry ↔ affectionate |
| Exposition | withheld until the reader needs it ↔ openly narrated |
| Story density | one anchor case per chapter ↔ continuous narrative spine |
| Emphasis | flat throughout ↔ selectively heightened |
| Direct address | constant "you" ↔ rare |

Also record:

- **3–5 sample sentences written for this project.** Not borrowed, not generic.
  These are the concrete target; the table above only bounds it. This is the
  single highest-value field in the file — a panel without samples is a
  description of a voice, not a voice.
- Anything this listener has said grates when narrated. So far: telling the
  listener how to feel about an idea ("let that land", "hold on to this"),
  announcing honesty or importance instead of showing it, and announced
  transitions. That is taste, not a checklist.
- Permitted variation by chapter job — an orientation chapter and a failure
  analysis should not sound identical.

Do not imitate a living author. When the user names one, identify the craft
properties they actually want — compressed syntax, patient mechanism-first
explanation, restrained humour, precise uncertainty — and build an original
combination.

## Habits that have helped

**Put people and things in the subject slot.** Concepts belong in the predicate.
"The document matters because a bid and a paid sale differ" is a concept
performing an action it cannot perform. "A raised card is not a receipt, and a
receipt is not a deed" says the same thing with objects a listener can picture.
When a sentence opens with *The <abstract noun>*, ask who is actually doing
something.

**Name one, not five.** When you can list planning law, tenancy, occupancy,
safety, and the redemption right, you have researched well and written badly. A
listener retains one vivid item and loses all five. Choose the one that carries
the point and give it a consequence. Exhaustiveness belongs in the reference
appendix, which is never narrated.

**Convert modals into people.** Statutory and API sources arrive in *may* and
*must*, and prose absorbs them until everything sounds conditional. "The holder
may collect rent" becomes "The rent cheques start coming to you." Where a modal
must survive, name who is bound by it.

**Vary the rhythm deliberately.** A one-sentence paragraph after a long one is
the cheapest emphasis in prose and costs no adjectives.

## What a story is, and is not

A hypothetical is not a story. "Consider a modest fictional building on Harbour
Road" is a worked example wearing a costume: nothing happened, nobody decided
anything, and there is nothing to remember.

A story has a named actor, a place, a date, and **a reversal** — something a
reasonable person would not have predicted. A split-brain patient explaining he
picked the shovel to clean out the chicken shed is a story. Shoppers preferring
the right-most of four identical stockings and then citing the workmanship is a
story. Both teach a mechanism no definition would have lodged.

Sources are documented and institutional: published decisions, papers,
post-mortems, news reports, real repository history, named public figures acting
in public roles. Not private individuals.

Collect these during research, in the story-ledger section of
`source/research.md`, **before the outline freezes**. This is the actual reason
books come out storyless — the fact pack gathers statutes and API surfaces, so
at drafting time the author has no narrative material on hand and invents a
hypothetical. You cannot write stories from a fact pack that contains none.

## For idea-driven subjects, the lineage *is* the mechanism

The strongest explanation of a concept is usually the problem it was invented to
solve, told as the story of someone hitting that problem.

Descartes asking whether a body is a machine → whether reasoning can be
mechanised → McCulloch and Pitts → Rosenblatt's perceptron → Minsky's demolition
→ backpropagation. That chain is not decorative history competing with the
technical content for word budget. It **is** an explanation of what a neural
network is and why it has the shape it has.

This matters because of how it fails. When a listener says "I never learned what
a neural net is" and the lineage gets replaced with mechanism, the only remaining
way to say what a neural net is, is the notation — and a listener who is driving
cannot follow notation. The history was carrying the explanation; removing it
leaves a vacuum that mathematics rushes in to fill. Extend the spine until it
reaches the mechanism. Never trade it away for the mechanism.
